# Editorial style

## Audience and purpose

Write for an interested non-expert who wants to understand wine, while
remaining useful to a knowledgeable reader. Introduce technical terms when
they clarify the subject, define them in context, and avoid using specialist
language merely to sound authoritative.

An article should help the reader understand why a grape, place, method, or
style behaves as it does. It should not read like marketing copy, a tasting
grid, a database record expanded into sentences, or a collection of trivia.

## House voice

Use concise, direct, explanatory prose. The voice is neutral but not sterile:
informed comparisons and clearly signaled interpretation are welcome.

Prefer:

> Syrah's expression changes substantially with climate. Cooler examples often
> emphasize peppery and savory aromas, while warmer sites tend to produce
> broader, more fruit-driven wines.

Avoid:

> Syrah is a bold, world-famous red bursting with blackberry, plum, violet,
> leather, tobacco, cedar, and exotic spice.

Avoid promotional language such as *prestigious*, *world-class*, *hidden gem*,
*noble*, *best*, and *must-try* unless the term itself is being discussed or a
specific attributed judgment is relevant.

## State the point directly

Prefer a positive explanation to a running argument with an imagined reader.
Drafts can fall into a repetitive corrective frame:

> Schist influences rooting and drainage. It does not transfer mineral flavour
> directly into wine.

When the correction is secondary, omit it and state the useful mechanism:

> For growers, schist affects how roots penetrate the soil, how water drains,
> how heat is absorbed, and how vineyards must be established.

Apply the same scrutiny to *not merely*, *not simply*, *not only*, and
*rather than*. Do not preserve an unnecessary rebuttal by changing its
connective. Keep a negative distinction when it prevents a consequential
misunderstanding, such as confusing two legal categories, treating a protected
name as generic, or misdescribing a technical process.

Use concrete subjects and verbs when the actor or mechanism is known. Prefer
“growers broke and rearranged the rock” to “the soils are human-made,” and
“regulators sought to control the trade” to “regulation sought to control it.”
Cut an abstract setup sentence when the following sentence can make the point
itself.

Within a section, state a conclusion once. If the mechanism, comparison, or
example has already established the point, do not append a second sentence that
restates the lesson in more general terms.

## Explain rather than classify

Statements about structure or behavior should lead toward explanation.

Weak:

> Nebbiolo has high tannin and pale color.

Better:

> Nebbiolo can accumulate substantial tannin despite producing relatively
> pale-colored wines because skin pigmentation and tannin concentration are
> different properties.

Explain relevant causes: climate, site, grape physiology, farming, production
method, maturation, or age. Do not force a causal story when the evidence does
not support one.

## Describe variation

Wine characteristics are conditional. Qualify generalizations with their
important sources of variation, which may include:

- climate and weather;
- site, soil, elevation, and exposure;
- yields and harvest decisions;
- winemaking and maturation;
- bottle age;
- regional traditions and legal requirements.

Use words such as *typically*, *often*, *can*, *especially*, and *depending on*
with purpose. Do not add them mechanically to make unsupported claims appear
cautious.

Comparisons can illuminate a subject when the comparison is specific:

> Baga can resemble Nebbiolo in combining relatively pale color with
> substantial acidity and tannin.

Avoid equivalences that erase important differences, such as calling one grape
"the [better-known grape] of [country]."

## Sensory language

Mention an aroma, flavor, texture, or structural trait when it is characteristic
and helps explain the wine. Prefer a small number of contextual observations to
a broad descriptor inventory.

Connect sensory claims to conditions when possible: cool versus warm sites,
young versus mature wine, particular methods, or a well-established varietal
compound. Do not imply that every wine from a grape or region shares an exact
set of flavors.

Do not assign numeric or ordinal scores to qualitative properties. There is no
house scale for acidity, tannin, body, fruitiness, aroma, quality, ageability,
or difficulty.

## Facts, interpretation, and uncertainty

Separate what evidence establishes from what writers, growers, or drinkers
commonly infer. Useful formulations include:

- `DNA analysis indicates ...`
- `The earliest documented use found so far ...`
- `The variety was traditionally considered ...`
- `The origin remains disputed ...`
- `A commonly repeated account claims ..., but ...`

Do not turn repeated wine lore into fact through confident wording. When good
sources disagree, explain the disagreement in proportion to its importance.

Informed judgment is allowed, but identify it as comparison or interpretation
rather than measurement.

## Numbers and precision

Use numbers for genuinely quantitative information, including dates, planted
area, elevations, temperatures, legal yields, minimum alcohol, and supported
genetic probabilities. Give the place, period, unit, and source needed to make
the number meaningful.

Planting totals, regulations, and similar figures change. Include an as-of year
and avoid false precision when the underlying measurement is approximate.

Never invent exactness to resolve an ambiguous historical, sensory, or
viticultural claim.

## Structure

Each article begins with minimal YAML frontmatter followed by one level-one
heading that matches the canonical title. The opening one or two paragraphs
should identify the subject, place it in context, and state what makes it worth
understanding.

Use the relevant template as guidance, not as a form. Omit an optional section
when it would repeat another section or contain only generic filler. Prefer a
few developed paragraphs to many shallow subsections.

End with `## Related topics` when there are a few genuinely useful existing
articles to link, followed by `## Sources`. Do not add a related topic merely
to make the list longer.

Default to roughly 700–1,200 words. This is a soft depth budget, not a target or
hard limit: a genuinely complex subject may need more, and a focused subject
may need less. Prefer omitting secondary examples, marginal history, and
fine-grained mechanism to making an article exhaustive. Stop when the central
explanatory work is done.

## Mechanics

- Use sentence-style capitalization for headings.
- Use the name appropriate to the sentence while linking to the canonical page.
- Italicize book and journal titles and scientific names such as *Vitis
  vinifera*.
- Expand an unfamiliar abbreviation on first use.
- Use Unicode accents and diacritics in prose and titles when they are part of
  the name; filenames remain simple stable identifiers.
- Prefer paragraphs over tables unless a table materially clarifies repeated,
  comparable facts.
- Do not embed application components, callouts that require a particular
  renderer, or raw HTML for layout.
