# Port

Port is a fortified wine protected as the Porto denomination of origin and made
from grapes grown in Portugal's [Douro](../regions/douro.md) Demarcated Region.
The English name is not a generic term for any sweet red wine strengthened with
spirit: *Port*, *Port wine*, *Porto*, and *vinho do Porto* are protected names
for wine that meets the region's production and certification rules.[^1]

Most Port is sweet and relatively high in alcohol because grape spirit is added
while alcoholic fermentation is still under way. Yet this common method leads
to markedly different wines. Grape colour and composition, the timing of
fortification, extraction, blending, vessel, oxygen exposure, filtration, and
bottle age all matter. Ruby, tawny, vintage, late-bottled vintage, white, and
rosé are therefore useful legal and production categories, not interchangeable
quality ranks or promises of one flavour.

## How it is made

Port begins as fermenting grape must. Red Port commonly uses blends that may
include [Touriga Franca](../grapes/touriga-franca.md),
[Touriga Nacional](../grapes/touriga-nacional.md),
[Tinta Roriz](../grapes/tempranillo.md), and many other authorized varieties.
Because fortification follows only a short fermentation, colour and tannin must
be extracted quickly. Traditional foot treading in shallow stone *lagares* is
one method; mechanical treading and pump-overs can pursue the same practical
goal with different extraction effects.

At the intended balance of alcohol and remaining sugar, certified grape spirit
is added; red Port is normally run off its solids as this happens. The resulting
rise in alcohol inhibits the yeast and stops fermentation, retaining sugar that
would otherwise have been converted to alcohol. Adding the spirit earlier
generally leaves more sugar; adding it later leaves less. Current rules require
spirit of viticultural origin approved by the Instituto dos Vinhos do Douro e
do Porto (IVDP), and most Port falls between 18 and 22 percent alcohol by
volume, with special provisions for light dry white Port.[^1] The spirit is not
merely a neutral preservative: research indicates that its volatile composition
can contribute to the young wine, although grape material, fermentation, and
subsequent maturation remain major influences.[^2]

## Blending and maturation

After the first winter, tasting and analysis help determine a lot's likely
destination. Blending may combine grapes, parcels, fermentation vessels, and
maturation lots. Most basic ruby and tawny Ports also combine harvest years;
vintage and late-bottled vintage must come from one harvest, but may still be
blends of several vineyards, grapes, or vats from that year. Blending can build
balance and maintain a producer's continuity, but it also prevents a category
name from acting as a complete recipe.

Maturation supplies the broadest divide. Large vats or stainless steel limit
the wine's contact with oxygen and tend to preserve deeper colour and more
immediate fruit. Smaller wooden casks, transfers, and longer storage expose
wine to more oxygen relative to its volume. Pigments and tannins change, colour
lightens, and fruit-led aromas give way to more developed, oxidative character.
This is a continuum rather than a switch: vessel size and condition,
temperature, ullage, racking, time, and the starting wine all change its course.
Bottle maturation is different again because oxygen availability is much more
restricted.

## Principal categories

**Ruby** is usually a blend of relatively young red Ports matured to limit
oxidative development. Its name describes the intended colour and youthful
direction, not a grape or vintage. Reserve ruby is a separately approved
category, while vintage and late-bottled vintage belong to the wider ruby-style
family because they also begin with deeply coloured, relatively protected red
wines.

**Tawny** is red Port whose maturation has moved it toward tawny colour and
wood-aged character. A basic tawny need not have the long cask history implied
by an age-indicated wine. Reserve tawny requires at least six years in wood;
age-indicated tawny is a multi-year blend approved as showing the character of
10, 20, 30, 40, 50, or 80 years in cask. That number is a sensory indication,
not a statement that every component—or the mathematical average of the
blend—has exactly that age. Colheita is a different tawny-style path: one
harvest matured in wood for at least seven years.[^3]

**Vintage** is red Port from one harvest, approved by the IVDP in its second
year and bottled by the end of July in the third. Its relatively short period
before bottling leaves much of the slow development to the bottle, so sediment
and substantial evolution with age are normal. The category identifies an
approved lot, not every Port made in that year, and producers can make different
blending and extraction choices within the rules.

**Late-bottled vintage (LBV)** is also red Port from one harvest, but it remains
in pre-bottling maturation longer: approval begins in the fourth year and final
bottling is permitted through the sixth. That extra time generally makes it
more developed on release than vintage Port. Filtered, unfiltered, and
explicitly bottle-matured examples can nevertheless behave differently after
bottling; the regulation allows the bottle-matured indication only after at
least three years in bottle.[^3]

**White Port** is made from white grapes and spans several sweetness levels and
maturation approaches. A young wine protected from oxygen can remain pale and
immediate, while extended wood ageing can produce a much deeper, oxidative
wine. Reserve, age-indicated, and single-harvest Colheita forms extend the same
logic used for mature tawny Port. Colour alone can become an unreliable guide:
an old white Port may approach an old tawny in appearance.

**Rosé Port** is made from red grapes with limited skin contact and restricted
oxidation before bottling. It is generally intended to retain pink colour and a
young fruit-led character. Legally and technically it is its own Port category,
not simply a mixture of ruby and white or a point halfway between them.

## What the label does not settle

Port law controls origin, production, analytical limits, maturation claims,
and approval, and it defines sensory expectations for each traditional term.
Those safeguards make the categories meaningful without making wines within a
category identical. A ruby blend can differ in grape mix, extraction, sweetness,
and time in large vessels. Two LBVs can differ through selection, wood exposure,
filtration, and bottle age. Age-indicated tawnies are judged for an age-related
character rather than assembled to one fixed formula, while white Port crosses
both youthful and long wood-aged forms.

The label should therefore be read as a set of boundaries and a maturation
direction. Producer decisions, harvest conditions, blending material, and time
within those boundaries determine the particular sensory profile.
