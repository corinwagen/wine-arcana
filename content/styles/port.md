# Port

Port is a fortified wine made from grapes grown in Portugal's
[Douro](../regions/douro.md) Demarcated Region and protected under the Porto
denomination of origin. The names *Port*, *Port wine*, *Porto*, and *vinho do
Porto* are reserved for wine that meets the region's production and
certification rules.[^1]

Most Port is sweet and relatively high in alcohol because grape spirit is added
while alcoholic fermentation is still under way. Yet this common method leads
to markedly different wines. Grape colour and composition, the timing of
fortification, extraction, blending, vessel, oxygen exposure, filtration, and
bottle age all matter. Ruby, tawny, vintage, late-bottled vintage, white, and
rosé therefore mark legal and production categories. Quality and flavour still
depend on the individual wine.

## How it is made

Port begins as fermenting grape must. Red Port commonly uses blends that may
include [Touriga Franca](../grapes/touriga-franca.md),
[Touriga Nacional](../grapes/touriga-nacional.md),
[Tinta Roriz](../grapes/tempranillo.md), and many other authorized varieties.
Because fortification follows only a short fermentation, colour and tannin must
be extracted quickly. Traditional foot treading in shallow stone *lagares* is
one method. Mechanical treading and pump-overs pursue the same goal but extract
differently.

At the intended balance of alcohol and remaining sugar, winemakers add certified
grape spirit and normally run red Port off its solids at the same time. The
higher alcohol inhibits the yeast and stops fermentation, preserving sugar that
the yeast would otherwise convert to alcohol. Adding the spirit earlier
generally leaves more sugar; adding it later leaves less. Current rules require
spirit of viticultural origin approved by the Instituto dos Vinhos do Douro e
do Porto (IVDP), and most Port falls between 18 and 22 percent alcohol by
volume, with special provisions for light dry white Port.[^1] Research indicates
that volatile compounds in the spirit can shape the young wine, although grape
material, fermentation, and subsequent maturation remain major influences.[^2]

## Blending and maturation

After the first winter, tasting and analysis help determine a lot's likely
destination. Blends may draw from different grapes, parcels, fermentation
vessels, and maturation lots. Most basic ruby and tawny Ports also combine
harvest years. Vintage and late-bottled vintage must come from one harvest, but
may still draw from several vineyards, grapes, or vats from that year. Blending
can build balance and maintain continuity across a producer's wines, so a
category name cannot serve as a complete recipe.

Maturation creates the broadest divide among styles. Large vats or stainless
steel limit the wine's contact with oxygen and tend to preserve deeper colour
and more immediate fruit. Smaller wooden casks, transfers, and longer storage
expose wine to more oxygen relative to its volume. Pigments and tannins change,
colour lightens, and fruit-led aromas give way to more developed, oxidative
character. Vessel size and condition, temperature, ullage, racking, time, and
the starting wine all change its course.
Because oxygen availability is much more restricted, bottle maturation follows
a different course.

## Principal categories

**Ruby** is usually a blend of relatively young red Ports matured to limit
oxidative development. Its name describes the intended colour and youthful
direction, not a grape or vintage. Reserve ruby is a separately approved
category, while vintage and late-bottled vintage belong to the wider ruby-style
family because they also begin with deeply coloured, relatively protected red
wines.

**Tawny** is red Port whose maturation produces tawny colour and wood-aged
character. A basic tawny need not have the long cask history implied by an
age-indicated wine. Reserve tawny requires at least six years in wood.
Age-indicated tawny is a multi-year blend approved as showing the character of
10, 20, 30, 40, 50, or 80 years in cask. That number is a sensory indication,
not a statement that every component—or the mathematical average of the
blend—has exactly that age. Colheita follows a different tawny-style path, with
one harvest matured in wood for at least seven years.[^3]

**Vintage** is red Port from one harvest, approved by the IVDP in its second
year and bottled by the end of July in the third. This early bottling leaves
much of the slow development to the bottle, so sediment and substantial
evolution with age are normal. The category identifies an approved lot, not
every Port made in that year, and producers can make different blending and
extraction choices within the rules.

**Late-bottled vintage (LBV)** is also red Port from one harvest, but it matures
longer before bottling. Approval begins in the fourth year, and final bottling
is permitted through the sixth. That extra time generally makes it more
developed on release than vintage Port. Filtered, unfiltered, and explicitly
bottle-matured examples can nevertheless behave differently after bottling.
The regulation allows the bottle-matured indication only after at least three
years in bottle.[^3]

**White Port** is made from white grapes and spans several sweetness levels and
maturation approaches. A young wine protected from oxygen can remain pale and
immediate, while extended wood ageing can produce a much deeper, oxidative
wine. Reserve, age-indicated, and single-harvest Colheita forms extend the same
logic used for mature tawny Port. Colour alone can become an unreliable guide:
an old white Port may approach an old tawny in appearance.

**Rosé Port** is made from red grapes with limited skin contact and restricted
oxidation before bottling. It is generally intended to retain pink colour and a
young fruit-led character. Legally and technically, it is a distinct Port
category.

## What the label does not settle

Port law controls origin, production, analytical limits, maturation claims,
and approval, and it defines sensory expectations for each traditional term.
The rules leave room for variation within each term. A ruby blend can differ in
grape mix, extraction, sweetness, and time in large vessels. Two LBVs can differ
through selection, wood exposure, filtration, and bottle age. Age-indicated
tawnies are judged for an age-related character rather than assembled to one
fixed formula, while white Port crosses both youthful and long wood-aged forms.

The label sets boundaries and a direction of maturation. Within those
boundaries, producer decisions, harvest conditions, blending material, and time
determine the particular sensory profile.
