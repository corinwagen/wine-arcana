# Rosé

Rosé is a family of pink wines usually made by limiting the contact between
dark grape skins and their juice. Most dark-skinned wine grapes have pale
flesh, so the winemaker can draw off lightly coloured juice before extracting
the pigment and [tannin](../concepts/tannin.md) associated with red winemaking.
The range extends from pale, fresh still wines to fuller examples and
sparkling rosé.

## Extracting colour

In direct pressing, grapes enter the press without a preceding soak. Contact
during pressing supplies some colour, and the separated juice then ferments.
In a short maceration, crushed berries first soak together so that skins
release more pigment and other compounds. The winemaker drains and presses
the fruit when the desired extraction has been reached. Provence uses both
methods extensively.

The result depends on the grape's pigment supply, ripeness, temperature, and
the handling of the fruit. A longer or warmer soak generally extracts more
colour. Fermentation, clarification, and maturation subsequently change that
colour, so the shade of freshly pressed juice is only an intermediate stage.

*Saignée*, meaning bleeding, draws juice from a vat of dark grapes after some
skin contact. When the remaining contents become red wine, removing juice
raises the proportion of skins to liquid in that vat. The drawn juice ferments
separately as rosé. Harvest decisions made for the red wine also determine
the rosé's starting sugar, acidity, and ripeness.

Grapes grown specifically for rosé give the producer more freedom to pick for
a lighter style. Earlier harvesting can retain acidity and limit potential
alcohol; riper fruit can give greater weight. This vineyard decision helps
explain differences between wines made with similar periods of skin contact.

## Fermentation and variation

Once the juice has been separated, winemakers can manage [fermentation](../concepts/alcoholic-fermentation.md) much as
they would for white wine. Protection from [oxidation](../concepts/oxidation.md),
juice clarification, yeast choice, and fermentation temperature shape the
aroma. Fermenting to dryness or retaining residual sugar determines sweetness.
[Lees aging](../concepts/lees-aging.md) can add texture to wines intended for
a fuller expression.

Rosé [Champagne](../regions/champagne.md) provides another route to pink wine.
Producers can blend red and white base wines, or obtain a rosé base through
skin maceration. Bottle fermentation and maturation then add the effects of
the [traditional sparkling method](traditional-method-sparkling-wine.md).
Examples range from light and fresh to substantial wines whose fruit develops
toward dried-fruit and spicy aromas with age.
