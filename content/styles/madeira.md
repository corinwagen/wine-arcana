# Madeira

Madeira is a fortified wine whose maturation uses heat and oxidative ageing
to develop its character. The protected origin covers Portugal's Madeira
archipelago, including Madeira and Porto Santo. Wines range from relatively
dry to richly sweet, with grape variety, fortification timing, and maturation
all contributing to the result.

Its combination of acidity, retained sugar, and developed aroma makes Madeira
distinctive among fortified wines. The terms used for sweetness belong to
this particular tradition: even a Madeira described as dry can contain
appreciable [residual sugar](../concepts/residual-sugar.md).

## Grapes and the island

Madeira's steep agricultural slopes are often arranged in small terraces
called *poios*. The island has volcanic soils, a maritime climate, and a
network of irrigation channels called *levadas*. Traditional *latada*
training spreads vines horizontally overhead. Fragmented holdings and steep
terrain make vineyard work and harvesting labour-intensive.

The familiar white-grape names also help orient a reader to sweetness.
Sercial is associated with dry Madeira and particularly marked acidity.
Verdelho generally gives a somewhat fuller, medium-dry style. Boal is
associated with medium-rich wine, while Malvasia produces rich, sweet styles.
The island grows both Malvasia-Cândida and Malvasia de São Jorge, so Malvasia
on a Madeira label should be understood in its local varietal context.

Tinta Negra is central to production and spans the sweetness range. The grape
alone consequently gives limited guidance about whether its wine will be dry
or sweet. The producer's chosen fermentation endpoint supplies that
information. Grape names, sweetness terms, and ageing designations work
together when reading the label.

## Fermentation, fortification, and heat

Producers ferment the pressed juice and add neutral vinous alcohol at a point
chosen for the intended sweetness. Interrupting fermentation while substantial
sugar remains yields a sweeter wine. Allowing yeast to consume more first
produces a drier result. The Instituto do Vinho, do Bordado e do Artesanato da
Madeira (IVBAM), the regional regulatory institute, describes
Madeira as a fortified wine within a 17–22% alcohol range.[^1]

In *estufagem*, the wine receives controlled heating, commonly in stainless
steel tanks warmed by circulating hot water. IVBAM describes a heating period
of at least three months followed by a resting period. The combination makes
heat a planned stage of production.[^2]

In *canteiro*, wine matures in casks in warmer parts of the cellar. The name
comes from the wooden beams that support the casks. Ambient warmth and
[oxidative ageing](../concepts/oxidation.md) progressively develop the wine.
Time in cask permits a slower course of maturation, and producers can retain
stocks for extended ageing and later blending.

## Sweetness and age designations

Age indications such as 10 or 20 years appear on blends whose sensory
character must meet the institute's standards for that age. Colheita and
Frasqueira carry a harvest year. Under the 2015 labelling regulation identified
by IVBAM, Colheita requires at least five years of continuous wood ageing.
Frasqueira requires canteiro production and at least twenty years of continuous
wood ageing, together with a recommended grape-variety designation. Both
categories require sensory assessment and a bottling year.[^4] The latter
helps show how long a harvest-dated wine remained in the producer's care
before bottling.

Sweetness provides another independent distinction. The institute lists
extra dry, dry, medium dry, medium rich, and rich styles, while marking its
published sugar figures as indicative.[^3] These words are most useful for
comparing Madeiras with one another. Sercial's acid structure, for example,
helps explain how a sugar-containing fortified wine can occupy the dry end
of this range. Richer wines retain more sweetness while maturation supplies
the developed character shared across the style.
