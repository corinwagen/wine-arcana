# Irrigation and vine water stress

Irrigation supplies water to a vineyard when rain and stored soil moisture
cannot meet the intended needs of the vines. Water stress develops when a
vine has difficulty supplying enough water to its tissues. Its effects
depend on severity, timing, soil, plant material, and the crop being grown.

Growers may use a controlled water deficit to limit excessive shoot growth.
They also need enough water to support photosynthesis, ripening, and the
vine's longer-term health. Managing that balance helps explain wines from
arid regions and the decisions facing vineyards during a dry spell in an
otherwise rainy climate.

## Soil supply and atmospheric demand

Water enters the soil through rain or irrigation. Some runs off, some drains
below active roots, and some remains accessible to the plant. The vine loses
water mainly through transpiration from its leaves; soil and ground cover
also lose moisture. Together, evaporation and transpiration form
*evapotranspiration*.

Annual rainfall is an incomplete guide. A downpour may supply less usable
water than slower rain, and a long dry interval can exhaust reserves during
rapid growth. The North Carolina extension guide discusses exactly this
problem in a region with substantial annual precipitation. Its example
shows why a humid climate can still benefit from supplemental irrigation.

The soil profile matters as much as the surface. University of California
adviser Mark Battany explains that a deep rooting zone may store a large
reserve, while shallow rock or a restrictive layer limits access. Irrigation
frequency and emitter placement also change the volume of soil wetted.
Small, frequent applications and larger, less frequent ones can therefore
produce different rooting and nutrient-access conditions on the same site.

## What the vine does under stress

Stomata are small pores that allow leaves to take in carbon dioxide while
releasing water vapour. As water becomes harder to supply, stomatal closure
helps conserve it. That response also restricts photosynthesis, reducing
the vine's capacity to make the sugars needed for growth and ripening.

Moderate water limitation can reduce shoot growth and produce a more open
canopy. Severe or prolonged stress can instead slow sugar accumulation,
damage leaves, and expose fruit when foliage is lost. Berry shrivelling
concentrates the remaining juice, so a rising sugar concentration can reflect
water loss as well as continued ripening. A thirsty vine does not reliably
produce a more balanced wine.

The timing matters. Growth, fruit development, and the preparation of buds
for the following year's crop place different demands on the plant. Water
management belongs with [crop load](crop-load.md) and
[canopy management](canopy-management.md), because the fruit and leaf area
alter how much the available root system must support.

## Measuring before irrigating

Growers can combine soil observations, weather-based estimates of water use,
and measurements of the vine itself. A pressure chamber measures leaf water
potential by determining the pressure needed to bring water back to the cut
surface of a sampled leaf. More negative values indicate greater tension in
the plant's water system.

Measurements require a consistent method and appropriate interpretation.
University of California guidance identifies different irrigation thresholds
for red and white wine grapes, with further variation by variety and other
conditions. A numerical threshold developed for one vineyard should not be
treated as a universal definition of ideal wine-growing stress.

## Deficit irrigation and its limits

Deficit irrigation applies less water than the crop's estimated full use.
*Regulated deficit irrigation* changes the planned deficit according to the
stage of the season. Both require monitoring: a chosen reduction in water
delivery does not guarantee a chosen degree of plant stress when weather or
soil conditions change.

A UC Davis experiment reported in 2021 compared irrigation treatments in
a Napa Cabernet Sauvignon vineyard during two contrasting seasons. Replacing
part of estimated crop water use maintained useful fruit composition and
yield under the tested conditions.[^trial] That finding establishes an
opportunity for that system, rather than a percentage prescription for
every grape, rootstock, or region.

Water quality adds another constraint. Salts can become more concentrated as
soil water is depleted, making it harder for roots to take up what remains.
Battany's account explains why irrigation frequency and the wetted soil
volume require attention alongside the total water delivered.

## Regional meaning

In the [Calchaquí Valleys](../regions/calchaqui-valleys.md), Colomé documents
mountain-water collection and reservoir-fed irrigation in its arid
vineyards. This makes infrastructure part of the regional wine-growing
landscape. [Dry farming](dry-farming.md) uses a different water strategy,
relying on rainfall stored within reach of the roots.

For the drinker, irrigation describes part of how the fruit was grown. Its
effect on the wine follows the resulting canopy, crop, ripeness, and berry
condition, then the choices made in the cellar. The useful question is how
the water supply suits the vineyard and season.
