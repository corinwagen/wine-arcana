# Grape ripeness and harvest decisions

Grape ripeness is the condition of the fruit in relation to the wine a grower
and winemaker intend to produce. Sugar, acids, aroma compounds, skins, seeds,
and fruit health change together, but at different rates. Harvest selects a
point in that changing balance.

Fruit suitable for a sparkling base wine may be picked before fruit destined for
a fuller still wine from the same variety. Grapes intended for a [botrytized
sweet wine](../styles/botrytized-sweet-wine.md) follow another path. There is
consequently no universal harvest date or sugar reading that defines ripeness
for all wines.

## What changes after véraison

At [véraison](veraison.md), berries soften and begin the main ripening phase;
dark-skinned varieties develop colour. Sugar accumulates as the vine supplies
the fruit, while malic acid is consumed in metabolism. Tartaric acid behaves
differently, so the changing acid balance depends on more than the loss of one
compound.

Aroma compounds and their precursors also develop. Some green-smelling
components decline, while other compounds accumulate or become available for
transformation during fermentation. The direction and timing depend on the
variety and growing conditions. For red wines, growers also assess colour
development, skin properties, and seed maturation.

## Measurements and tasting

Degrees Brix measures soluble solids and is commonly used to estimate sugar and
potential alcohol. It is an estimate: Oregon State University notes that Brix is
not identical to fermentable glucose plus fructose, and that sample preparation
can change the reading.[^chemistry]

Growers also measure pH and titratable acidity, which describe different aspects
of [acidity](acidity.md). Tasting berries adds information about flavour,
texture, and uneven development. Representative sampling is needed for both
tasting and analysis.

A useful sample includes different parts of the block, both sides of the row,
and berries exposed to different conditions. Repeating the sampling shows the
pace of change. Picking only appealing berries can make a vineyard seem more
advanced than the fruit that will actually arrive at the press.

## Water, heat, and fruit health

A rising sugar concentration can reflect both sugar accumulation and loss of
berry water. Shrivelled fruit therefore needs interpretation. Severe [water
stress](irrigation-and-vine-water-stress.md) can restrict photosynthesis, while
high temperatures can accelerate acid loss or damage exposed berries. Additional
time on the vine does not guarantee continued improvement.

Waiting also changes exposure to rain, rot, birds, and autumn frost. A sound
crop picked before a storm presents a different starting point from damaged
fruit harvested later at a higher Brix. Vineyard access, available labour,
transport, and winery capacity influence what can be picked and processed
promptly.

## Different endpoints for different wines

[Chenin Blanc](../grapes/chenin-blanc.md) makes the range of objectives easy to
see: earlier fruit can supply sparkling wine, later fruit still wine, and
concentrated berries sweet wine. For [Cabernet
Sauvignon](../grapes/cabernet-sauvignon.md), a grower may weigh herbal aroma,
tannin extraction, alcohol potential, and forecast weather together.

These decisions continue into the cellar. [Pressing](pressing.md), skin contact,
fermentation, and [blending](blending.md) shape the material harvest provides.
