# Fortification

Fortification raises a wine's alcohol content by adding spirit. The timing of
that addition has a major effect on sweetness. Added during
[alcoholic fermentation](alcoholic-fermentation.md), enough spirit interrupts
yeast activity and leaves some grape sugar unfermented. Added after a wine has
fermented dry, spirit raises alcohol without restoring the sugar already
consumed.

[Port](../styles/port.md), [Madeira](../styles/madeira.md), and
[Sherry](../styles/sherry.md) illustrate how this one operation serves different
purposes. It can preserve sweetness, prepare wine for long maturation, or help
select the biological conditions under which it ages.

## Timing and retained sugar

In Port production, the winemaker adds grape spirit while fermentation is
underway. The Instituto dos Vinhos do Douro e do Porto, the regional regulatory
institute, describes using certified spirit at 77% alcohol by volume. The
sudden increase in alcohol interrupts fermentation at the chosen sweetness.[^1]
The resulting wine contains alcohol made by the yeast and alcohol supplied by
the spirit, together with [residual sugar](residual-sugar.md) from the grapes.

For red Port, colour and tannin extraction must fit into this shortened
fermentation. Contact with skins and techniques such as treading or pumping
juice over the solids help extract material before fortification. Subsequent
maturation then separates the styles: more protected storage preserves the
fruit-led character associated with ruby wines, while prolonged cask ageing
supports the oxidative development associated with tawny.

Madeira producers also use fermentation timing to obtain their intended
sweetness. The island's regulatory institute describes fortification with
vinous alcohol at 96% alcohol by volume.[^2] Allowing more sugar to ferment
before the addition moves the wine toward the drier end of Madeira's range.
Controlled heating or warm cask maturation follows, giving heat and oxygen a
further role in developing the wine.

## Alcohol and biological ageing

Dry Sherry offers a different sequence. Producers first ferment the base wine
and assess the resulting batches. In the traditional fortification route,
wines selected for fino or manzanilla are brought to about 15% alcohol. This
supports *flor*, the film of yeast that grows on the surface and modifies the
wine during biological ageing. Wines selected for oloroso are fortified to at
least 17%, a level that prevents sustained flor development under these cellar
conditions.[^3]

Without that surface film, the oloroso follows an
[oxidative maturation](oxidation.md). Fortification thus helps determine which
organisms remain active and which ageing process follows. The result also
depends on the starting wine and the cellar environment. These traditional
Sherry strengths describe a particular production system, not universal
survival limits for every wine microorganism.

## Spirit and the finished wine

Spirit strength and final wine strength are different measurements. A highly
concentrated spirit enters a much larger volume of fermenting juice or wine,
so the blend has a lower alcohol content than the spirit itself. The amount
needed depends on both starting strengths and the intended result.

Adding spirit also changes the proportions of sugar, acids, and other grape
constituents in the blend. Sweetness therefore depends on both how much sugar
remained when fermentation stopped and the composition after blending. A
fortified wine's final balance brings together those concentrations, alcohol,
and the changes that occur during maturation.
