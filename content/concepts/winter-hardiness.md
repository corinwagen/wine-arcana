# Winter hardiness

Winter hardiness is a grapevine's ability to survive cold during its dormant
season. It helps determine which varieties can sustain vineyards in places
such as Minnesota, Québec, and the
[Finger Lakes](../regions/finger-lakes.md). Hardiness varies with the variety,
the tissue being considered, and the vine's changing physiological condition.
A published minimum temperature is consequently an observation under specified
conditions, not a permanent survival guarantee.

## Hardiness changes through the season

As autumn progresses, vines acclimate: their tissues become better able to
tolerate freezing temperatures. After the vine's chilling requirement has
been met, sufficiently warm weather can begin reversing that protection.
This loss of hardiness is called *deacclimation*.

A sudden freeze after a warm interval can therefore cause damage at a
temperature that a fully acclimated vine survived earlier in winter. Gradual
cooling may allow some reacclimation. Joseph Fiola's University of Maryland
account emphasizes this sequence of weather, which explains why a winter's
lowest thermometer reading alone is an incomplete measure of danger.

## Surviving roots, injured shoots

Winter injury may kill buds, sections of wood, or an entire trunk while leaving
the roots alive. In 2019, University of Minnesota Extension documented severe
injury in some vineyards and survival in others, with differences among sites
and varieties. Surviving shoots from the base could be trained into replacement
trunks. The account also cautioned that the possible contribution of trunk
disease required investigation rather than diagnosis from appearance alone.

For own-rooted varieties such as
[Frontenac](../grapes/frontenac.md) and
[Marquette](../grapes/marquette.md), renewal from the base can retain the desired
grape. On a [grafted vine](grafting.md), the origin of a replacement shoot
matters: a shoot below the union belongs to the rootstock.

Winter survival and [spring frost](spring-frost.md) tolerance are separate
questions. Once growth begins, tender green shoots can be damaged by
comparatively mild freezing conditions even on a very winter-hardy variety.
