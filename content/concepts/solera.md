# Solera

A solera is a system of maturation in which part of an older wine is withdrawn
and replaced with younger wine. Repeated withdrawals and replenishments blend
harvests over time. The method is central to [Sherry](../styles/sherry.md) and
Montilla-Moriles, where it helps maintain continuity of style across successive
releases.

The word also names the oldest stage within the system: the wine from which
the producer draws the portion destined for bottling or further blending.
Younger stages, called *criaderas*, feed it in sequence. Each stage already
contains a mixture of ages.

## Moving through the stages

In the classic arrangement, the first criadera replenishes the solera, the
second replenishes the first, and so on. Younger incoming wine refreshes the
last criadera. The withdrawal is a *saca*; replenishment is a *rocío*. Only a
fraction moves at each operation, leaving wine behind to mix with the next
addition.[^1]

A simplified example shows why this matters. Suppose a single, thoroughly
mixed cask contains 100 litres, and 20 litres are removed and replaced. It now
contains 80 litres of the previous blend and 20 litres of incoming wine. If
the operation is repeated after mixing, 64 litres of the original 100 remain:
four-fifths of the earlier four-fifths. The original component dwindles through
successive withdrawals while younger additions acquire their own histories
in the cask. This is an illustration of fractional blending, with evaporation
and other losses omitted.

A system with several criaderas repeats that mixing across stages. The
producer chooses the number of stages, the proportion withdrawn, and the
frequency of transfers. Together these decisions determine how quickly wine
moves through the stock and how strongly each new addition affects the
outgoing blend.[^2]

## Time and the age of the wine

A solera's founding date identifies when the system began. It cannot by itself
establish the age of a later bottling, because replenishment has continually
changed the blend. A small surviving contribution from early wine and a large
contribution from recent wine have very different effects on its average age.

Even a volume-weighted average requires the ages and proportions of the
components. Mixing equal volumes of wines aged two and ten years produces a
six-year average at that moment; the liquid still contains both age groups.
The example describes arithmetic, independently of any regional labelling
category.

The Sherry regulatory council uses total stock divided by annual withdrawals
as a turnover measure associated with average ageing. That ratio describes
how much wine is retained relative to the rate at which it leaves. It is most
informative for a system operating steadily. Establishment, changes in
withdrawals, the age of incoming wine, and losses require additional accounting
before it can describe the actual age distribution of a particular release.[^2]

## Maturation continues between transfers

A solera can contain biologically or oxidatively aged wine. In biological
ageing, [flor](flor.md) consumes compounds in the wine; younger additions help
replenish nutrients that support the yeast. In oxidative ageing, cask storage
and oxygen introduced during handling contribute to a different course of
chemical development.[^3]

Blending and maturation consequently work together. Transfers change the
proportions of the component wines, while the time between transfers allows
those wines to keep changing. A producer seeking a consistent release must
manage the character of incoming wine as well as the rate of withdrawal.
