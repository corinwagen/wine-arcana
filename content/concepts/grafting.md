# Grafting

Grafting joins living parts of two plants so that they grow as one vine. The
*scion* supplies the fruit-bearing shoots of the desired grape variety; the
*rootstock* supplies the roots. This combination lets growers retain a variety
such as [Cabernet Sauvignon](../grapes/cabernet-sauvignon.md) while choosing a
root system suited to vineyard pests and growing conditions.

The scion retains its varietal identity while the rootstock influences its
growth. Breeding a cross produces genetically new offspring; grafting connects
existing plants.

## Making the union

Successful joining requires contact between the partners' vascular cambium,
the thin growth tissue between the wood and inner bark. Healing tissue forms
at the cut surfaces and connects the graft. The union must develop sufficiently
to support the growing scion.[^1]

Nurseries commonly bench-graft dormant pieces of scion and rootstock, then
allow the union to heal and the rootstock to form roots. Field grafting joins
new scion material to an established vine. Growers can use this second method,
also called topworking, to change the fruiting variety while retaining an
existing root system.

## Why resistant roots matter

Phylloxera is a small insect that feeds on grapevine roots. On susceptible
vines, feeding damage and secondary infection can destroy parts of the root
system, reducing growth and yield. *Vitis vinifera*, the species containing
most familiar wine grapes, is vulnerable. Rootstocks derived from resistant
American grape species supplied a practical response to the vineyard losses
caused by the pest.

Resistance protects the vine while phylloxera may remain present. University
of California pest-management guidance notes that resistant young rootstocks
can still support small populations and suffer stunting in heavily infested
replant sites. Its warning about the historical failure of AXR#1 in California
also shows why protection must be evaluated for the particular rootstock and
pest population.[^2]

Other root pests pose different problems. Plant-parasitic nematodes are tiny
roundworms, and a rootstock resistant to one species may be susceptible to
another. Some nematodes transmit viruses as they feed. Resistance to the
nematode does not necessarily prevent virus transmission, because even brief
feeding can transfer the pathogen.[^3]

## Matching roots to the site

Rootstocks also differ in drought tolerance, vigour, and adaptation to soil
conditions. Their performance depends on the scion and the vineyard environment.
A root system supporting vigorous growth may be useful where the soil limits
the vine, but require different irrigation and
[canopy management](canopy-management.md) on a more generous site.

A Coonawarra trial summarized by viticulturist Nick Dry illustrates the
interaction. The drought-tolerant rootstocks 1103 Paulsen and 140 Ruggeri
produced vigorous vines, and the trial raised questions about managing that
growth during wetter conditions. Applying the same management across rootstocks
can influence their apparent performance.[^4] The practical choice must
consider water supply and the intended vine size alongside pest resistance.

## Plant health and establishment

A healthy graft also depends on the planting material. Propagation can carry
pathogens already inside the plant tissues. Nursery certification programmes
use testing and inspection to reduce this risk; the scope of testing matters
to what that certification establishes. Oregon State University's vineyard
establishment guidance emphasizes certified sources for both cultivars and
rootstocks and specifically identifies leafroll, red blotch, and fanleaf among
the viruses of concern.[^5]

A suitable rootstock can offer durable protection against a soil pest while
an infected scion introduces a different problem.
