# Residual sugar

Residual sugar is the sugar remaining in finished wine. In winery analysis,
the most useful measurement is usually the combined concentration of glucose
and fructose, the principal fermentable grape sugars, expressed in grams per
litre. The result helps explain sweetness and indicates how much substrate
remains available to microorganisms.

Sugar concentration and perceived sweetness are closely related, but acidity
and the kinds of sugar present alter the impression. Knowing the analytical
value helps most when the wine's style and composition are also known.

## How sugar remains

During [alcoholic fermentation](alcoholic-fermentation.md), yeast consume
glucose and fructose and produce alcohol and carbon dioxide. Yeast commonly
consume glucose more readily, leaving a greater proportion of fructose late
in fermentation. The balance depends on strain and conditions. Fructose also
tastes sweeter than glucose at comparable concentrations, so the proportions
can influence the sweetness of the finished wine.[^1]

Fermentation may stop deliberately or through difficulty. High alcohol,
nutritional limitations, and other stresses can prevent yeast from finishing
the available sugar. A fermentation that has stopped changing can therefore
still contain appreciable fermentable sugar. Monitoring sugar consumption
helps distinguish completion from a stalled fermentation.

In fortified wine, the timing of spirit addition offers deliberate control.
[Madeira](../styles/madeira.md) producers, for example, interrupt fermentation
according to the desired sweetness. Earlier interruption preserves more of the
grape sugar; longer
fermentation consumes more before fortification. Finished wine can also be
sweetened with grape juice or concentrate where the applicable rules permit.
These different routes can produce different glucose-to-fructose balances.

## Measuring sweetness and dryness

Glucose and fructose measurements are particularly useful because both sugars
affect taste and can support further microbial activity. Enzymatic assays use
reactions specific to the sugars being measured, allowing laboratories to
determine small quantities in otherwise dry wine.

Older “reducing sugar” methods also respond to other sugars and can yield
different results. Small quantities of pentoses, sugars that ordinary wine
yeast leave largely unfermented, may remain even when glucose and fructose are
nearly exhausted. They contribute relatively little sweetness. The analytical
method therefore matters when interpreting a claim about how dry a wine is.

The [acidity](acidity.md) provides another part of the tasting context. A wine
with substantial sugar can retain a strongly acid impression, as Madeira
illustrates. Its local terms for dry and sweet belong to a distinctive
fortified style. The Madeira regulatory institute's published sugar ranges are
explicitly indicative; “dry” Madeira can contain appreciable sugar.[^2]
Sweetness words on labels need
to be read within the relevant wine category.

## Stability after fermentation

Residual glucose and fructose can feed surviving yeasts and bacteria. Sugar
analysis therefore serves a practical purpose beyond describing flavour: it
helps assess the possibility of further fermentation or spoilage. A sweet wine
requires a preservation strategy suited to its alcohol, acidity, microbial
population, and intended storage. Suitable [filtration](wine-filtration.md)
can remove microorganisms, while [sulfur dioxide](sulfur-dioxide.md) can inhibit
their growth. The remaining sugar must be considered alongside these controls.
