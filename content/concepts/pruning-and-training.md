# Pruning and training systems

Pruning determines which parts of a grapevine remain to grow the next crop.
Training arranges those parts in space. Together they establish the framework
for [canopy management](canopy-management.md): where shoots emerge, how much
room leaves have, where bunches hang, and how workers or machines reach them.

A trellis is the supporting hardware—posts, wires, anchors, and stakes.
Different vine shapes can occupy similar trellises. Looking at the wires alone
will therefore tell you less than looking at the wood attached to them.

## Read the wood before naming the system

A green shoot becomes a woody **cane** as it matures. During dormancy, that
one-year-old cane carries buds from which the next season's shoots can grow.
Those new shoots bear the bunches. Older trunks and arms support this annual
renewal and connect it to the roots.

A **spur** is a cane cut back to a short section with a few buds. A **cordon**
is an older arm retained over successive years. This distinction explains
why a spur-pruned vine still needs new fruiting wood annually: the cordon
stays, while the short pieces growing from it are renewed.

In **cane pruning**, the grower selects longer one-year-old canes and removes
much of the previous fruiting framework. Selected canes are tied into place;
short renewal spurs can provide shoots from which future canes are chosen.
In **spur pruning**, many short fruiting units are distributed along retained
wood. Both methods leave buds that produce new shoots; they differ in where
those buds are retained and how the framework is renewed.

## Bud position and the next crop

The main pruning choices can be compared before considering the trellis:

| Approach | Wood retained for the next crop | Recurring work and constraints |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cane pruning | Selected longer canes from the previous season, usually with renewal positions near the head. | Choose sound canes, remove the old fruiting canes, and tie replacements. Useful where productive buds occur farther from the base. |
| Spur pruning | Short pieces of last season's wood on retained arms or a head. | Renew and space the spurs, remove unwanted growth, and maintain productive positions. Depends on suitable basal-bud fruitfulness. |

Neither choice establishes the final crop by itself. The number of retained
buds, their survival and fruitfulness, and subsequent thinning all matter.

Buds near the base of a cane are less fruitful in some varieties than buds
farther along it. Such a vine may need longer canes to retain enough productive
positions. Varietal behaviour, the previous summer's light exposure, and
winter injury all influence what the grower finds at pruning.

Bud number also sets the starting point for [crop load](crop-load.md).
Pruning very severely can leave too few growing points for a vigorous vine,
encouraging strong growth from those remaining. Leaving numerous buds on a
weak vine can create more shoots and fruit than it supports adequately.
The useful number depends on vine size and condition; it cannot be read from
the pruning system's name.

Pruning weight, measured from the removed one-year-old wood, can help assess
the previous season's growth. Balanced-pruning approaches use that observation
to guide bud retention, with adjustments for variety and injury. The appropriate
formula depends on the growing system. Yield also depends on budbreak, bunch
number, fruit set, and
berry growth after pruning.[^yield]

## Guyot, cordons, and head-trained vines

**Single Guyot** retains one longer fruiting cane; **double Guyot** retains
two. Each year, replacement canes are selected and tied into the fruiting
zone. The one-year-old cane laid along the wire is different from a permanent
cordon, even when the dormant vines look superficially similar.[^guyot]

A **spur-pruned cordon**, including cordon de Royat arrangements, retains
older horizontal wood with short fruiting units distributed along it. The
grower renews those units while keeping the arm. Gaps, dead spurs, or an
unproductive arm may require a replacement shoot and a longer renewal plan.

In **head-trained** vines, short arms arise around the upper trunk rather
than extending along a long horizontal cordon. A spur-pruned bush or
**gobelet** vine is one familiar form. Head training can also support longer
fruiting canes: the location of the framework and the length of the annual
fruiting wood are separate decisions. Regional names often describe a
combination of those features.

## Arranging the canopy

**Vertical shoot positioning**, usually shortened to **VSP**, guides shoots
upward between wires. It creates an accessible fruiting zone beneath a wall
of leaves. VSP describes shoot arrangement: it can accompany cane pruning
or spur pruning. A **high-cordon** system instead places the arms higher and
allows shoots to hang down. Trailing varieties generally fit this arrangement
more readily than strongly upright ones.

Vigorous vines may benefit from divided canopies. **Geneva double curtain**
separates two hanging curtains sideways; **Scott Henry** separates upward-
and downward-growing foliage vertically. The extra space can expose leaves
that would otherwise crowd one another, but keeping the curtains separate
requires suitable support and seasonal work.[^systems]

Hardware makes these choices possible. The fruiting wire supports the crop
and its bearing wood; catch wires guide the growing shoots. Posts and anchors
must withstand their combined weight and wire tension. Row spacing, machine
access, and the height at which people prune and pick are part of the design,
alongside the vine's growth habit.

## Establishment and renewal

Young vines first need roots and a usable trunk, followed by the arms or
renewal positions required by the chosen system. Their progress depends on
growth, so a weak vine may need another season to establish the structure that
a stronger neighbour has already formed. Early cropping is adjusted to that
capacity.

Cold climates make renewal especially important. Growers may retain multiple
trunks or suitable replacement shoots so that damage to one part does not
require rebuilding the entire vine. Cane pruning renews the horizontal
fruiting wood each year, while injured permanent cordons may require a more
substantial replacement. Such renewal plans help growers recover from
[winter injury](winter-hardiness.md).

## Choosing when to cut

Late pruning allows assessment of winter bud damage. Under some conditions,
delaying the final cut also delays development of the buds that will be kept,
which can help manage [spring frost](spring-frost.md) exposure. Labour and the
risk of breaking swelling buds constrain how late a vineyard can finish.

Pruning also opens wounds through which wood-infecting fungi can enter.
University of California guidance describes delayed pruning, staged pruning,
and wound protection as preventive tools against trunk diseases under
California conditions. Their timing follows local rainfall and infection risk;
a calendar recommendation from one region should not become a universal
pruning date.[^wounds]

In staged pruning, an early preliminary cut leaves extra wood for removal
at the final pass. This can delay development of the lower buds or, in
trunk-disease management, move the final cuts away from wetter periods with
higher infection risk.[^wounds]

## Hand work, pre-pruning, and minimal pruning

A mechanical pre-pruner removes surplus wood before workers select the final
spurs or canes. The preliminary pass and the finished pruning are different
operations. Spur-pruned cordons lend themselves to regular machine passes,
while selecting and tying replacement canes still requires more individual
handling.

Mechanical hedge pruning or minimal-pruning systems retain a different bud
population from carefully selected short spurs. That changes shoot density,
bunch development, and the work needed later in the season. Their suitability
depends on variety, vine capacity, growing conditions, and the intended crop;
labour savings must be assessed alongside fruit development and canopy access.
Mechanical harvesting is another separate decision and can accompany more
than one pruning approach.[^mechanization]

A three-year study of 20 vineyards at five German estates found labour and
cost savings from minimal and semi-minimal systems. Work shifted across the
season, so the comparison included summer canopy work as well as winter
pruning.[^minimal]

Summer shoot thinning, hedging, and leaf removal act on the growing canopy.
They complement dormant pruning but solve different problems, as explained
under [canopy management](canopy-management.md).
