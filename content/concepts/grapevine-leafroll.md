# Grapevine leafroll

Grapevine leafroll is a disease associated with a group of grapevine viruses.
Infection can delay ripening, reduce yield, and alter fruit composition.
The practical consequences extend beyond the curled or discoloured leaves
that give the disease its name: growers need healthy plant material and a
strategy to prevent further spread through the vineyard.

## Symptoms and ripening

Symptoms commonly become clearer toward the end of the growing season. Red
varieties may develop red areas between veins that remain green, sometimes
with downward-curled leaf edges. White varieties can show faint yellowing or
few visible symptoms. Variety, rootstock, weather, and the particular infection
affect what a grower sees.

South African guidance identifies disruption of transport through the phloem,
the tissue carrying sugars and other products of photosynthesis. Delayed and
uneven maturity, poorer colour development, and reduced crops can follow.
Waiting longer for infected fruit to ripen also exposes it to whatever heat,
rain, or disease risks accompany the end of the season.

A symptom-free appearance does not establish a virus-free vine. University
of California guidance emphasizes laboratory testing and appropriate sample
collection. A visual observation, a test for a particular virus, and the
performance of the crop supply different kinds of evidence.

## How infection moves

Infected cuttings and budwood can introduce viruses into a new planting or
a grafted block. Propagation preserves a vine's variety while potentially
carrying its infection with it. This makes health testing part of
[massal and clonal selection](massal-and-clonal-selection.md).

Some leafroll-associated viruses also spread through insect vectors. Mealybugs
and soft scale insects transmit grapevine leafroll-associated virus 3,
abbreviated GLRaV-3. South African guidance identifies the vine mealybug as
an especially important local vector. Removing an infected vine and managing
the insects address separate parts of the transmission problem.

## Managing the vineyard

An established infected vine cannot be cured simply by controlling its
insect vectors. Growers assess infection levels, the pattern of affected
plants, vineyard age, and economic performance. Decisions can include removing
individual infected plants, controlling vectors, or replacing a heavily
affected block. Monitoring nearby vineyards matters because clean plantings
can be reinfected.

The South African integrated control guide combines inspection, testing,
removal of infected material, and vector management. Its decision framework
shows why one response cannot fit every block. A young planting with a few
affected vines presents a different problem from an older, widely infected
vineyard.

Foundation Plant Services at UC Davis provides a documented example of
prevention through propagation. Its foundation material undergoes pathogen
testing, with tissue-culture treatment used to establish clean source plants.
Health status concerns the pathogens tested under that programme; it is
separate from a clone's performance or a guarantee against future infection.
