# Terroir

Terroir describes the relationship between a wine-growing place, the vines
cultivated there, and the practices through which people turn their fruit into
wine. Climate, topography, soils, water, and living organisms interact with
choices about planting, farming, and production.

The International Organisation of Vine and Wine's definition explicitly includes
collective knowledge and viticultural practices alongside the physical and
biological environment. It also distinguishes this descriptive concept from the
legal definition of a geographical indication.[^definition]

## How a place affects the vine

Temperature governs the pace of development and the growing season available to
a variety. Slope and aspect change the sunlight a vineyard receives; wind and
nearby water can modify heat and humidity. [Altitude](altitude.md) acts through
several of these routes at once. These conditions help determine whether grapes
reach the intended [ripeness](grape-ripeness.md) while retaining acidity and
healthy skins.

Soil influences rooting, water storage, drainage, nutrient supply, and the
temperature around roots. A shallow, freely draining soil may help control
vigour in a wet year but leave a vine short of water during drought. A deeper
soil can sustain growth through summer, although excessive vigour may require
more [canopy management](canopy-management.md). The consequences depend on the
weather, variety, and rootstock as well as the soil itself.

Van Leeuwen, Roby, and de Rességuier's review organizes soil effects around
water, mineral supply, and root-zone temperature. Each affects how a vine grows
and ripens its fruit.[^soil]

## The role of cultivation

Growers choose varieties and rootstocks, arrange rows and canopies, manage the
ground, and decide when to harvest. The same hillside planted with
early-ripening [Pinot Noir](../grapes/pinot-noir.md) or later-ripening [Cabernet
Sauvignon](../grapes/cabernet-sauvignon.md) presents different problems.
[Pruning](pruning-and-training.md) and crop decisions then change the demands
placed on the vine.

In [Burgundy](../regions/burgundy.md), the named vineyard parcels called
*climats* illustrate the historical relationship between observation,
cultivation, ownership, and the recognition of particular places. Their
boundaries and reputations developed through human institutions as well as
differences in natural conditions.

Extraction, fermentation, blending, and oak can preserve, alter, or obscure
differences established in the vineyard. Comparing wines made by similar methods
can help investigate place, while comparisons between unrelated bottles include
many other variables.

## Evidence and interpretation

A difference in wine composition is measurable; deciding whether it expresses a
place convincingly also involves sensory interpretation. Repeated observations
across vintages provide stronger evidence for a stable site pattern than one
successful bottle. Experiments that control variety, harvest, and winemaking can
test particular mechanisms, although controlling everything may also remove
parts of the cultivation tradition being studied.

Descriptors such as *stony*, *chalky*, or *mineral* record sensory impressions,
not a chemical identification of material transferred from rock to wine.
Likewise, an [appellation](appellations-and-wine-labels.md) establishes origin
and applicable rules; its boundary does not eliminate variation within it.
