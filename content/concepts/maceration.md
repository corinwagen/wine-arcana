# Maceration

Maceration is the contact between grape solids and juice or wine during which
substances pass into the liquid. Skins supply pigments, aroma compounds and
their precursors, and [tannin](tannin.md); seeds and any retained stems supply
additional phenolics. The duration and conditions of contact help determine
wine's colour, texture, and flavour.

Maceration can happen before, during, or after
[alcoholic fermentation](alcoholic-fermentation.md). These stages offer
different extraction conditions because the liquid changes from sugary juice
to an increasingly alcoholic wine, while temperature and the condition of the
solids change too.

## Extraction during red fermentation

Red winemaking commonly ferments juice together with skins and seeds. Carbon
dioxide lifts solids into a floating cap. Cap management brings this material
back into contact with the liquid and redistributes heat. In a punch-down,
the cap is pushed beneath the surface. A pump-over draws liquid from the
fermenter and distributes it over the cap. Rack-and-return drains wine away
from the solids and then returns it.

These operations change extraction as well as oxygen exposure. Their effects
depend on frequency, force, temperature, and the grapes themselves.

Colour and tannin also follow different courses. Skin anthocyanins, the main
red grape pigments, extract early. Later, measured free anthocyanins can fall
as pigments react or attach to solids. Rising ethanol promotes extraction of
seed tannins.[^1]

## Before and after fermentation

A *cold soak* holds crushed red grapes at a low temperature before alcoholic
fermentation. This extends contact in a largely aqueous liquid. The intended
effects commonly include colour and aroma extraction, but the Australian Wine
Research Institute's review finds that outcomes vary with grape variety and
growing conditions. A cold soak also extends the time during which [oxidation](oxidation.md)
and unwanted microbial activity must be controlled.[^2]

*Extended maceration* usually refers to retaining skins and seeds with the
wine after fermentation. Alcohol is now present, and both extraction and
phenolic reactions continue. The resulting texture depends on which compounds
are available and how readily the grapes release them. The same duration can
produce different results in different varieties or vintages.

Prolonged contact can increase bitterness or astringency. Exposed cap material
also creates opportunities for spoilage, including acetic acid and ethyl
acetate production when oxygen and suitable microorganisms are present.
The decision to press ends this phase of contact and separates wine from much
of the remaining solid material.[^3]

## White grapes and shorter contact

White-grape skin contact can add aroma and body before fermentation. Some
contact already occurs when mechanically harvested or crushed fruit waits for
pressing. Deliberately extending it changes how much the juice extracts.

Skins also release potassium, which can raise pH and reduce titratable [acidity](acidity.md).
Longer or warmer contact generally increases extraction, including phenolics
that contribute bitterness. Fruit condition matters: damaged or botrytized
fruit can introduce enzymes that accelerate oxidation. The useful duration
therefore depends on the grapes and the intended wine.[^4]

Fermenting white grapes with their skins produces the much longer contact
associated with [skin-contact white wine](../styles/skin-contact-white-wine.md).
Such wines can acquire substantial phenolic texture despite having no red
skin pigments. [Whole-cluster fermentation](whole-cluster-fermentation.md)
adds the further variables of intact berries and stems;
[carbonic maceration](carbonic-maceration.md) involves metabolism within intact
berries as well as the extraction that occurs as they break down.
