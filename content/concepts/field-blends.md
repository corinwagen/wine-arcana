# Field blends

A field blend begins with different grape varieties planted together in a
vineyard. In its familiar cellar form, their fruit is picked together and
fermented together, making the mixture a harvest decision as well as a
planting pattern. The term is also used for wines from mixed vineyards whose
components receive some separate handling.

Two choices therefore need distinguishing. **Mixed planting** describes where
the vines grow. **Co-fermentation** describes different grapes fermenting in
the same vessel. Fruit from separate vineyards can be co-fermented, and fruit
from a mixed vineyard can be separated. A field-blend description is most
informative when the producer explains both vineyard and cellar practice.

## Choosing one harvest for several grapes

Varieties do not necessarily reach comparable ripeness together. With a
single harvest, earlier-ripening grapes can bring more developed fruit while
later grapes retain more acidity or less mature skins. The grower chooses
a workable combination across the vineyard, rather than an independent
ideal date for every variety.

Changing yields also change the mixture. Missing vines, replacement planting,
fruit losses, and differences in fertility can alter each variety's share
of the crop. A planted proportion is consequently different from an exact
percentage of the harvested fruit or finished wine.

Fermenting the grapes together commits the winemaker to that combination
before the individual wines exist. With separate fermentation, the cellar
can taste components and adjust their proportions later. The practical
trade-off is between preserving a combined harvest and keeping more control
over individual ripeness, extraction, and the final blend.

## California: planting and cellar terminology

Older California vineyards often put [Zinfandel](../grapes/zinfandel.md)
alongside [Carignan](../grapes/carignan.md),
[Petite Sirah](../grapes/durif.md), and other grapes. These companion
varieties bring different fruit, acidity, colour, and tannin into a wine
whose leading variety may still appear on the label.

Ridge's general account defines a field-blend wine through mixed planting,
joint harvesting, and co-fermentation. Its more specific Lytton Springs
estate account describes interplanted old vines and fermentation of varieties
and lots separately. Reading those accounts together prevents a technical
assumption from the name alone. The vineyard in
[Dry Creek Valley](../regions/dry-creek-valley.md) preserves a mixed-variety
history while the cellar retains choices among components.[^ridge]

## Douro and Vienna

The [Douro](../regions/douro.md) supplies another continuing mixed-vineyard
tradition. Quinta do Crasto describes old parcels containing dozens of
varieties and uses their fruit in its Reserva Old Vines. A wine can therefore
combine the harvests of several mixed parcels, making both planting diversity
and site selection important to understanding its composition.

Vienna's white Gemischter Satz gives the practice a different regional form.
The regional wine body describes Wiener Gemischter Satz DAC as a wine from
at least three varieties grown in one vineyard, picked together, and
processed together.[^vienna] Wieninger's 2025 bottling provides a current
producer example: its description identifies 12 varieties growing together
and sharing the harvest. The aim includes combining different ripening
stages and aromatic contributions within one wine.

Mixed vineyards thus support red and white wines with quite different
methods. Their planting history explains something about the blend, while
harvest, fermentation, and [maturation](oak-maturation.md) explain how that
mixture becomes a particular wine.
