# Powdery mildew

Powdery mildew is a grapevine disease caused by the fungus *Erysiphe necator*.
Its pale, powder-like growth can affect leaves, shoots, and berries. Infection
can reduce the crop and change the fruit entering the winery, making it a
concern for wine quality as well as vineyard productivity.

## How an epidemic develops

The fungus can survive winter in dormant buds or in small fruiting bodies on
the vine. These provide routes for the first infections of the season.
Subsequent cycles spread spores through the vineyard when conditions favour
growth. Temperature is particularly important; hot extremes and favourable
moderate warmth have different effects on the organism.

University of California guidance uses weather-based risk assessment to help
judge disease pressure. [Canopy management](canopy-management.md) also matters:
opening dense growth improves access for protective treatments and changes
the shaded environment around susceptible tissue. Early infections deserve
attention because they can supply spores for later cycles.

## Why infected grapes change wine

The Australian Wine Research Institute describes effects extending beyond the
visible coating. Infected fruit can have altered composition, greater
susceptibility to other microbes, and different fermentation behaviour.
Reported wine effects include earthy or mushroom-like aromas, increased
bitterness, and changes in texture; their extent depends on the fruit and
severity of infection.

This creates practical consequences for sorting and
[maceration](maceration.md). Removing affected fruit limits what enters the
fermenter, while shorter contact with skins may reduce extraction of unwanted
components. The appropriate response depends on whether the intended wine is
white or red and on the condition of the lot.

Powdery mildew and [downy mildew](downy-mildew.md) are separate diseases with
different causal organisms and infection biology. A grape's resistance to one
therefore needs to be evaluated separately from its resistance to the other.
