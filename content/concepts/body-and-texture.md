# Body and texture

Body is a wine's perceived weight or fullness in the mouth. Texture describes
how it feels: smooth, rough, drying, viscous, prickly, or otherwise tactile.
These impressions overlap with taste and aroma.

A deeply coloured wine can feel relatively light; a pale wine can have
substantial weight. Likewise, a wine that dries the gums is not necessarily low
in sugar. Several different sensations contribute to what is often described
loosely as a wine's structure.

## Separate the sensations first

[Acidity](acidity.md) supplies sourness and influences salivary response.
[Residual sugar](residual-sugar.md) supplies sweetness and can affect perceived
fullness. [Tannin](tannin.md) contributes especially to astringency, the drying
or rough sensation associated with changes in lubrication in the mouth.
Bitterness is a taste and can accompany astringency without being the same
sensation.

Alcohol contributes its own sensory effects, including warmth. Dissolved carbon
dioxide can give a prickling sensation and alter the impression of freshness. A
wine's [aroma](wine-aroma.md) can be intense while it remains light in body.

## Composition and interaction

There is no single laboratory value for body. Ethanol, sugars, phenolics,
polysaccharides, acids, and dissolved gas interact within the wine and with the
taster. Physical viscosity is measurable, but it does not capture every
sensation included in a judgement of fullness.

A review of white-wine mouthfeel by Gawel and colleagues identifies alcohol and
pH as important parts of the sensory framework, with other constituents
modifying the response. Glycerol has not consistently increased perceived
viscosity within the range normally found in white wine.[^white]

The vocabulary itself needs care. Paissoni and colleagues' review finds body to
be widely used but incompletely defined across the research literature. A term
such as *silky* may communicate an impression, while a comparison such as “less
drying than the other wine” gives a clearer account of the observed
difference.[^terms]

## What winemaking changes

[Maceration](maceration.md) and [pressing](pressing.md) determine which skin and
seed components enter the liquid. A white grape fermented on its skins can
therefore acquire a drying texture that a rapidly pressed wine from the same
variety lacks. The effect depends on the fruit and extraction conditions.

[Lees aging](lees-aging.md) introduces yeast-derived material and can modify
mouthfeel. Oak contact and oxygen exposure change phenolics and other
constituents during maturation. [Fining](wine-fining.md) can remove or alter
some components, with trials needed to assess the result in the particular wine.

Adding a small proportion of another lot during [blending](blending.md) may
change texture more or less than its volume alone would suggest. The mixture
needs tasting as a whole.

## Comparing wines usefully

Compare at similar [serving temperatures](serving-temperature.md), and notice
when the sensation occurs. Astringency can build over repeated sips; alcoholic
warmth may be clearer after swallowing or spitting. Carbonation and temperature
can change as a wine stands in the glass.
