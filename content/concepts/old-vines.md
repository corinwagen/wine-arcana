# Old vines

Old vines are grapevines that have remained in production for many years.
Their value can include established roots, accumulated knowledge of a site,
distinctive planting material, and a record of regional farming. Age is useful
information about a vineyard, while grape quality still depends on health,
water, crop, weather, and management.

## Defining and documenting age

In 2024, the International Organisation of Vine and Wine adopted a definition
of an old grapevine as an officially documented plant at least thirty-five
years old. For grafted vines, the rootstock–scion connection must have remained
undisturbed for that period. Its definition of an old vineyard requires at
least 85% of the plants to qualify.[^definition] This is an international
standard and recommendation; the wording on a bottle also needs to be read
within the relevant country's rules or certification programme.

South Africa's Old Vine Project recognizes vineyards at least thirty-five
years old through its Certified Heritage Vineyard programme. Documenting
planting history lets growers identify fruit from a particular surviving
parcel. A vineyard can contain replacement plants, so the date of the original
planting and the age of every vine are different questions.

## What time can change

A vine's roots and permanent framework develop within the conditions of its
site. Soil depth, restrictive layers, water supply, and rootstock determine
where roots can grow. Additional years do not guarantee deeper roots where
the soil profile prevents them. [Dry farming](dry-farming.md) and
[irrigation](irrigation-and-vine-water-stress.md) also influence the water
available to an established plant.

Repeated propagation can preserve differences within a variety. Old parcels
may contain plant material selected by earlier generations of growers,
making them useful sources for [massal and clonal
selection](massal-and-clonal-selection.md). That value depends on identifying
the material and its health, as well as retaining diversity.

The OIV's resolution explicitly identifies gaps in research on vine longevity,
yield, and wine quality. Comparisons need to account for site, variety,
rootstock, planting density, management, and missing vines. A lower yield per
hectare can reflect gaps in a block rather than a desirable change in every
surviving plant. [Crop load](crop-load.md) remains the relationship between
the fruit carried and the vine's capacity to ripen it.

## Keeping a vineyard productive

Preservation requires continuing work.
[Pruning](pruning-and-training.md) must renew fruiting wood while
maintaining a usable framework. Disease, damage, and lost plants can make
replacement necessary. [Grapevine leafroll](grapevine-leafroll.md) illustrates
why conserving planting material and propagating healthy vines must be
considered together.

The Old Vine Project combines recognition with research and training for the
people who care for these vineyards. Commercial recognition can also give a
grower a reason to retain a parcel whose grapes previously entered an
anonymous blend. This connects preservation with livelihoods and farming
decisions, rather than treating old vines solely as picturesque objects.

## A wine-growing example

[Franschhoek](../regions/franschhoek.md) provides a documented case.
Boekenhoutskloof's Sémillon 2023 drew on vineyards with different planting
dates, soils, and exposures. The resulting wine also reflected pressing,
fermentation, and maturation choices. Its heritage vineyards identify the
source material; the cellar still helps explain the finished texture.

For a drinker, an old-vine claim is most informative when it gives a verified
planting date, a location, and a clear account of the source vineyard. It
supplies context without assigning a fixed flavour or a rank of quality.
