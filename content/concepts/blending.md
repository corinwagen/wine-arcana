# Blending

Blending combines grapes, musts, or wines to make a chosen final wine. The
components may differ in variety, vineyard, harvest date, vintage, extraction,
or maturation vessel. Even a wine labeled with one grape and one year can be
assembled from several separately fermented lots.

The purpose may be to combine complementary properties, maintain a recognizable
house style, express a larger geographical origin, or select the lots that
belong in a particular bottling.

## What is combined, and when

A [field blend](field-blends.md) begins with varieties planted together, often
picked and fermented together. Cofermentation also brings components together
before their separate wines exist: a producer may put grapes from different
varieties into the same fermenter. The subsequent extraction and fermentation
occur in a shared environment.

Separately fermented lots leave another set of options open. A winemaker can
compare individual parcels, free-run wine and press wine, or barrels with
different histories before selecting proportions. Keeping the lots apart
preserves flexibility, but requires cellar space, tracking, and repeated
tasting.

A varietal wine can involve extensive blending within its named grape. The
label's wording is governed by [origin and labeling
rules](appellations-and-wine-labels.md); it does not disclose every cellar lot
used to make the wine.

## Regional examples

[Bordeaux](../regions/bordeaux.md) illustrates blending between grapes with
different ripening behaviour and structural contributions. Merlot, Cabernet
Sauvignon, and Cabernet Franc can all contribute, with proportions changing by
site, estate, and vintage. The winemaker selects proportions after assessing the
components from that season.

[Champagne](../regions/champagne.md) extends the decision across vineyards,
grapes, and harvest years. Reserve wines give a producer material already
developed in the cellar to combine with younger wine. A vintage Champagne still
involves selection and can combine several parcels or varieties.[^champagne]

## Testing a possible blend

Bench trials compare small, measured mixtures before a cellar-scale decision.
The unblended components and several candidate proportions provide useful
reference points. Recording the volumes makes a preferred mixture reproducible;
tasting without seeing the recipe can reduce expectations about which component
ought to dominate.

Composition and perception require different kinds of checking. Alcohol or sugar
can be estimated by weighted quantities when measurements and units are
consistent. pH is affected by buffering and should be measured in the mixture.
Aroma, bitterness, and astringency also interact, so tasting one component alone
cannot establish its effect at a small proportion in another wine.

## Stability and the final wine

Combining two clear wines does not establish that the mixture will remain
stable. The final blend still needs appropriate analytical, microbial, and
stability checks before bottling. [Fining](wine-fining.md),
[filtration](wine-filtration.md), and [tartrate
stabilization](tartrate-stabilization.md) address different problems and are
considered for the finished composition.

[^champagne]: Comité Champagne, “Blending,” description of grape, vineyard,
    vintage, and reserve-wine choices.
