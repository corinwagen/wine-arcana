# Wine aroma

Wine aroma is the smell produced by volatile compounds reaching receptors in the
nose. Some arrive when a person smells the glass; others travel from the mouth
to the nose during drinking. This second route helps explain why a wine's
flavour includes more than the tongue's sensations of sweetness, sourness, and
bitterness.

Grapes supply both aromatic molecules and precursors that become aromatic later.
Yeast, bacteria, maturation vessels, oxygen exposure, and bottle age add or
transform further compounds.

## Grapes and precursors

Varieties differ in the compounds they can produce. Monoterpenes help explain
the floral expression of [Muscat](../grapes/muscat-blanc-a-petits-grains.md),
while methoxypyrazines can contribute green or herbal notes to Cabernet grapes.
Their concentrations depend on the vine's genetics and its growing conditions.

Some precursors have little smell until enzymes or fermentation transform them.
Consequently, tasting a berry and smelling its finished wine can give quite
different impressions. Harvest date and fruit exposure change the available
material, while extraction and microorganisms influence what becomes
perceptible. Lin and colleagues review these relationships between grape
genetics, biosynthetic pathways, and wine aroma.[^grape]

## Fermentation and maturation

During [alcoholic fermentation](alcoholic-fermentation.md), yeast produce
esters, higher alcohols, and other volatile compounds. The yeast population,
temperature, nutrition, oxygen, and juice composition affect the result.
Fruit-like smells can therefore arise through fermentation without the
corresponding fruit being an ingredient.

[Malolactic fermentation](malolactic-fermentation.md) can contribute buttery
character through diacetyl, although its perception depends on production and
subsequent transformations. [Oak maturation](oak-maturation.md) supplies another
source of aroma and changes oxygen exposure. Barrel toast, size, age, and the
duration of contact influence how much wood character enters the wine.

In [bottle aging](bottle-aging.md), some compounds decline while others form or
become easier to perceive. The familiar terms *primary*, *secondary*, and
*tertiary* refer broadly to grape, fermentation, and maturation aromas, although
their chemistry overlaps.

## Why the same compound can smell different

Concentration matters relative to a compound's detection threshold, but the
threshold itself depends on the surrounding mixture and the person smelling it.
Compounds can reinforce, mask, or change the interpretation of one another. The
smell of the whole wine is therefore not a simple inventory of every molecule
detected by an instrument.

[Serving temperature](serving-temperature.md), glass handling, and time after
opening also change the material reaching the nose. Comparisons become easier
when wines are served under similar conditions and revisited as they warm.

## Describing an impression

A specific, familiar comparison can communicate an aroma efficiently. One reader
may identify a flower or fruit where another notices only a broader floral or
fruity impression.

Aroma intensity is also separate from [body and texture](body-and-texture.md).
An expressive wine can feel light, and a substantial wine can smell restrained.
[Oxidation](oxidation.md), [reduction](reduction.md), and
[Brettanomyces](brettanomyces.md) provide further context when a particular
smell needs investigation.
