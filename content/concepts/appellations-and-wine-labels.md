# Appellations and wine labels

An appellation identifies a wine's geographical origin under a particular set of
rules. A wine label brings that information together with other kinds of
description: a producer, grape variety, harvest year, sweetness term, or
maturation category. Each describes a different aspect of the wine.

A place name can identify a broad region, a village, or a defined vineyard. The
relationship between those scales depends on the jurisdiction. An appellation
may also restrict varieties, yields, production methods, and release dates;
another system may concentrate principally on grape origin.

## Origin and production rules

The European Union distinguishes protected designations of origin, or PDOs, from
protected geographical indications, or PGIs. The European Commission's overview
explains that PDO wine uses grapes exclusively from its geographical area, while
PGI wine requires at least 85% from the area. Each wine's product specification
supplies the detailed conditions and any relevant exceptions.[^eu]

National terms such as France's AOP and Italy's DOC and DOCG appear within this
wider framework. Their production requirements vary by denomination.

In the United States, an American Viticultural Area, or AVA, identifies a
bounded grape-growing area distinguished by geographical or climatic features.
The federal framework differs from an appellation that prescribes a particular
blend or winemaking tradition. State requirements can add further
conditions.[^ava]

## Place, grape, and producer

A label headed [Chablis](../regions/chablis.md) identifies an appellation whose
white wines use Chardonnay. A [Willamette
Valley](../regions/willamette-valley.md) label may state Pinot Noir separately.
In both cases, knowing the geographical name helps, but the information is
arranged differently.

The producer's name supplies another essential distinction. Several growers can
make wine from the same named place, using different harvest dates, fermentation
methods, and maturation vessels. A vineyard name narrows the origin while
allowing those choices to vary. [Terroir](terroir.md) provides context for the
relationship between place and cultivation.

## Time and sweetness answer different questions

A vintage identifies a harvest year under the applicable labeling rules. It is
separate from a bottling date or disgorgement date. A non-vintage wine can
include several harvests, an important possibility in sparkling-wine
[blending](blending.md).

Maturation terms also require local interpretation. [Rioja](../regions/rioja.md)
uses Crianza, Reserva, and Gran Reserva for defined ageing categories. Those
terms should not be carried into another country as though they had identical
requirements everywhere.

Sweetness needs its own attention. The German Wine Institute distinguishes
*trocken*, meaning dry, from the Prädikat categories associated with grape
maturity at harvest. Its guidance also notes that *feinherb* has no precisely
defined legal sweetness range.[^sweetness] A harvest designation and the
finished wine's residual sugar describe different stages of production.

## A practical reading order

Start with the producer and the complete geographical designation. Identify the
grape if it is stated, then the vintage and any method, sweetness, or maturation
terms. Consult the relevant regional article for the meaning of unfamiliar
names.
