# Encruzado

Encruzado is a pale-skinned Portuguese grape whose identity is closely bound
to Dão. It can be blended with other local white varieties, but varietal
bottlings have made it an increasingly visible name in its own right. The
grape's particular interest lies less in an extravagant aroma than in its
ability to combine weight, acidity, and a firm shape that can remain clear in
both unoaked and more elaborately matured wines.

That balance helps explain Encruzado's association with lees and oak. Lees
contact can add breadth, while fermentation or maturation in wood changes
aroma, phenolics, and oxygen exposure. Neither treatment is required, and
neither is automatically beneficial: a lean, tank-made wine may emphasize
freshness, while conspicuous new oak can obscure the grape. Texture in an
Encruzado wine is therefore an interaction among fruit maturity, acidity, lees,
vessel, and time rather than a property of the variety alone.

## History and identity

Encruzado is generally treated as a Dão variety, but its exact birthplace is
not proved. A maintained Portuguese varietal reference finds no mention in
nineteenth-century literature and identifies a 1942 technical description that
associates the grape with the Viseu area. This is evidence of cultivation and
recognition in Dão, not proof that the original seedling arose there. The same
reference records *Salgueirinho* as a historical regional name but lists no
current official synonym.[^1]

A specialist account traces a more deliberate evaluation of the variety to
experimental plantings and separate vinifications by Alberto Vilhena at the
Dão viticultural research centre in Nelas from the late 1950s. Small lots from
that work demonstrated what the grape could do alone, but Dão remained largely
a region of blended wines. Broader recognition came with the growth of
varietal bottling in the 1990s. It is safer to describe that period as the
beginning of Encruzado's modern public identity than to claim one undisputed
first commercial bottle.[^1]

## Viticulture and Dão

The vine usually gives a medium, reasonably regular crop. Its bunches are
small to medium and fairly compact, which can make bunch rot a concern when
moisture persists. Portuguese records also note moderate susceptibility to
downy and powdery mildew, shoots that can break in wind, and relatively good
tolerance of dry conditions. These traits fit Dão imperfectly rather than by
destiny: the region's mountain barriers reduce direct Atlantic influence, but
winters can be wet and summers hot and dry. Many vineyards sit on low-fertility
granite-derived soils at moderate elevation, with substantial differences in
exposure and altitude from site to site.

Ripening classifications differ between Portuguese references: the current
Institute of Vine and Wine profile places Encruzado in the middle of the
season, while the Vine to Wine Circle record calls it early. Such labels depend
on the reference variety and trial site. More important in practice is the
harvest balance. The grape can accumulate ample sugar without necessarily
losing all useful acidity, but picking later for greater weight can still raise
potential alcohol and soften the acid line.

Technical sources likewise differ over whether its must acidity should be
classed as medium or high. That disagreement is a warning against a rigid
ranking. Encruzado's reputation rests on the proportion between alcohol,
acidity, and extract in a finished wine, not on acidity alone. Site, season,
crop level, and harvest date can move that proportion considerably.

## Wine character

Encruzado commonly gives dry whites with more tactile presence than their
restrained aroma might suggest. Young wines may show citrus and quiet floral
character, sometimes with a resinous or nut-like aspect emerging during
maturation. These are tendencies rather than a tasting grid. Warmer sites,
riper fruit, skin contact, and barrel work can make a broader wine; earlier
harvesting and inert vessels can preserve a tighter, more direct expression.

Acidity provides contrast to that breadth and can support bottle development,
but longevity is not guaranteed by the grape name. Fruit condition, protection
from oxidation, sulfur dioxide management, closure, and storage all matter.
This is especially relevant because one Portuguese varietal record describes
both must and wine as oxidation-sensitive. The fact that some examples mature
well should not be turned into the opposite claim that Encruzado is naturally
resistant to oxygen.

## Lees and oak in the cellar

Keeping wine on its fine [lees](../concepts/lees-aging.md), sometimes with
stirring called *bâtonnage*, allows yeast-derived polysaccharides and other
material to enter the wine and can change its tactile impression. A study of
one Encruzado wine compared four months of stirred lees contact in French-oak
barrels with contact in a steel tank fitted with oak staves. The barrel wine
finished with more measured polysaccharides and was judged rounder in body,
although the two treatments did not differ significantly in overall panel
preference.[^2] The experiment helps explain how the familiar broad texture can
be built; it does not show that every Encruzado needs lees or that barrel and
lees effects can be separated cleanly.

[Oak](../concepts/oak-maturation.md) may be used for fermentation, maturation,
or both. A separate 180-day experiment with Encruzado found that new barrels
transferred more phenolic material than one-year-old barrels. The clearest
sensory difference concerned wood aroma, strongest in the wine held in new
225-litre barrels.[^3] Barrel age, size, toast, contact time, and the proportion
of a blend treated in wood therefore matter more than a simple claim that the
grape “takes oak.” Used or larger vessels can give less overt wood character,
and blending a barrel component with tank wine can retain a fresher outline.

## Regional role and recognition

Encruzado is among the white varieties authorized by the current Dão
specification, but the rules do not require every white Dão to contain it or
require varietal bottling.[^4] It continues to work alongside grapes
such as Bical, Malvasia Fina, and Cerceal Branco. Small cultivation outside Dão
does not undo the association: the region remains both its historical reference
and the place where varietal bottling established its modern reputation.

Its emergence as one of Portugal's notable white grapes is thus relatively
recent and easy to overstate. Encruzado is still limited in area and closely
regional, not a broadly planted national counterpart to an international
variety. What changed from the late twentieth century was visibility: separate
vinification made its balance of texture and acidity legible, while restrained
use of lees and oak supplied producers with several credible styles rather
than one formula.
