# Montepulciano

Montepulciano is a dark-skinned grape strongly associated with [Abruzzo](../regions/abruzzo.md) and the
Adriatic side of central Italy. It can produce deeply coloured, substantial
reds, but also the region's cherry-coloured Cerasuolo rosés. The contrast makes
it a useful example of how extracting different amounts from the same grape
changes a wine's purpose.

The name also belongs to a town in Tuscany. Vino Nobile di Montepulciano is
based on [Sangiovese](sangiovese.md), locally called Prugnolo Gentile; its
place name does not identify the Montepulciano grape.[^1]

## Ripening and structure

Montepulciano needs a site capable of completing a relatively long season.
Rauscedo's technical account places budbreak late and ripening in the middle
to later part of the season, favouring warm, dry, well-exposed sites. Its thick
skins provide substantial colour and [tannin](../concepts/tannin.md).

Those properties leave room for markedly different cellar decisions. Extended
skin contact can build a firm red intended for maturation; a gentler extraction
can retain fruit while making the young wine easier to approach. Clonal
material adds another variable: the nursery records differences in acidity
and structure among its selections. A dark colour alone tells the drinker
little about how tannic a particular bottle will feel.

## Abruzzo and the rosé connection

Montepulciano d'Abruzzo gives the grape an established regional red-wine
identity. Cerasuolo d'Abruzzo offers another route, using limited extraction
to make rosé from the same variety. Its often substantial colour is a useful
reminder that [rosé](../styles/rose.md) covers a range of approaches, and that
paleness is not a measure of quality.

The practical decision is when to separate juice from skins. A red continues
fermenting with the solids; a rosé producer removes them earlier, or presses
the grapes with little prior skin contact. Colour, aroma and texture respond
to the details of that process, not just to the word on the label.

## A contemporary Australian setting

Montepulciano also has a meaningful varietal presence in McLaren Vale. Hither
& Yon describes its local expression in the context of the region's warm
maritime climate and the grape's later ripening. Its current bottlings give
readers a concrete comparison with the Italian home: a familiar grape grown
within a different viticultural and drinking culture.

The producer's accounts emphasize dark fruit and tannin. Those broad traits
connect the wines, while harvest maturity and maturation choices determine
whether their weight comes across as supple, fresh or more imposing. The
Australian example is established production, rather than a prediction that
the variety will solve every warm-region growing problem.
