# Pedro Ximénez

Pedro Ximénez is a white *Vitis vinifera* grape most closely associated with
Andalusia, especially the inland Montilla-Moriles appellation in Córdoba and,
to a lesser extent, [Jerez](../regions/jerez.md) in Cádiz. It can make dry
white wine and contributes to the dry, biologically aged wines of
Montilla-Moriles. Its defining role, however, is as the raw material for the
very sweet wines also called Pedro Ximénez, commonly shortened to PX.

Those wines begin with ripe bunches dried after harvest. Removing water
concentrates sugar and acid before pressing, so the resulting must is far more
concentrated than one from fresh grapes. Fermentation is then stopped or
prevented by the addition of grape spirit, leaving grape-derived sweetness;
oxidative ageing may add further colour and savoury complexity. Its dense
texture and dried-fruit character arise from post-harvest drying and cellar
work as well as the variety itself.

## A name that needs care

Pedro Ximénez is a Spanish white wine grape, documented in the Madrid regional
grape collection as a distinct, characterized *Vitis vinifera* accession.[^1]
The origin of its name and the stories often offered to explain it are much
less secure. No direct evidence establishes an origin in a particular country
or a known historical namesake.

Spelling is consequential here. **Pedro Giménez**, the Argentine white criolla
variety, is not Pedro Ximénez. A microsatellite study found the two to be
different cultivars and found no genetic relationship between them in the
markers examined; it instead supported Pedro Giménez as offspring of Muscat of
Alexandria and Criolla Chica.[^2] A label or planting record therefore needs
its place and genetic identity, not just a near-identical name.

On a label, Pedro Ximénez may name a naturally sweet fortified wine under a
regional appellation as well as the grape. Pedro Ximénez can also be made dry:
Montilla-Moriles permits it in unaged and aged white wines, as well as in its
Fino style. The legal category and production method explain the finished wine.

## Drying grapes for sweetness

In the traditional *asoleo* process, growers lay sound harvested bunches in
the sun. Water evaporates while sugar, acid, skin material, and other dissolved
components become more concentrated. This is drying, or raisining, rather than
the concentration caused by *Botrytis cinerea* in a botrytized sweet wine. Its
practical success depends on ripe, healthy fruit and dry enough weather to
avoid damaging the berries before pressing.

Montilla-Moriles defines *asoleo* as post-harvest sun exposure on gently
sloping ground, designed to dehydrate the berries while preserving their skins
and sanitary condition. Its current specification requires PX sweet wine to be
made from at least 95% Pedro Ximénez; the dried-grape must must contain at least
450 grams of sugar per litre.[^3]

After pressing, the concentrated must is fortified early enough that much of
its sugar remains. Some Montilla-Moriles PX is sold without ageing; other lots
age oxidatively in cask, either as a vintage wine or through fractional
*criaderas y solera* blending. Oxidative maturation darkens the wine and can
move it beyond fresh-grape notes toward raisin, caramelized, roasted, or
coffee-like impressions. Duration, cask history, oxygen exposure, strength,
and the starting fruit all affect the balance.

## Montilla-Moriles and Jerez

Montilla-Moriles is the principal inland Andalusian home of Pedro Ximénez. Its
vineyards occupy the rolling country south of Córdoba, where the protected zone
includes Montilla and Moriles as well as neighbouring municipalities. The
grape is central enough to the appellation that it supplies both dry and sweet
styles; warm growing conditions can help fruit accumulate the sugar needed for
the dried-grape route, but the sweet wine remains the result of a deliberate
post-harvest process.[^3]

In Jerez, [Palomino Fino](palomino-fino.md) is the main grape for the region's
dry wines. Pedro Ximénez is authorized there for the naturally sweet category
explained in [Sherry](../styles/sherry.md), but is a much smaller local
planting. The Jerez specification requires a wine sold as Pedro Ximénez to use
at least 85% must from raisined grapes of that variety and to age
oxidatively.[^4] It also allows fortified raisined PX must from Montilla-Moriles
when it is unavailable from the Jerez production area. That provision helps
explain why a Jerez PX name can encompass fruit grown in Montilla-Moriles.

The two regional versions resemble one another because both build sweetness
from concentrated Pedro Ximénez must and commonly use oxidative maturation.
Their legal definitions differ: Montilla-Moriles sets a 95% threshold for the
grape, while Jerez sets 85%; each appellation also has its own permitted methods
and ageing options. In either place, the deeper colour and more developed
character of an old example are partly the work of time in cask.
