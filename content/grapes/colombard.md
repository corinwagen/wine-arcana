# Colombard

Colombard is a white grape associated with the Charentes and Gascony in western
France. Historically used for distilling wine, it also produces fresh,
aromatic table wines, particularly in Côtes de Gascogne. Its fertility,
productivity, and useful acidity explain part of that versatility. The aroma
of the finished wine depends strongly on vineyard and cellar choices.

## Identity and changing uses

Plantgrape reports genetic evidence suggesting that Colombard is a cross
between Gouais Blanc and [Chenin Blanc](chenin-blanc.md).[^1] It belongs to
the historical group of Charentais distilling grapes alongside
[Folle Blanche](folle-blanche.md) and [Ugni Blanc](ugni-blanc.md), but is
typically more overtly aromatic as a table wine than Ugni Blanc.

The revival of Gascon table wine gave those aromas a new commercial purpose.
Plaimont dates its own renewed interest to a tasting of American “French
Colombard” in the late 1970s. That producer account illustrates an exchange
between regions rather than a new discovery of the variety: Colombard was
already present in Gascony for distillation.

Its productive habit has also suited larger-scale winegrowing outside France.
A 2019 University of California study treated Colombard as one of four
varieties for modelling largely mechanized vineyards in the southern San
Joaquin Valley. That is a different production setting from a Gascon aromatic
white, even when both draw on the same grape's capacity to yield useful fruit.

## Viticulture

Colombard is vigorous and fertile, with pruning adaptable to the intended
crop. Plantgrape places its ripening in the middle part of the season rather
than among the latest white varieties. Medium-sized bunches and berries can
give substantial yields, but productivity alone says little about the
balance or concentration of the resulting wine.

The IFV's comparison of Colombard clones in the Gers found differences in
productivity, potential alcohol, and suitability for table or distilling
wine.[^2] Site, planting material, and production target therefore deserve
attention before making a general claim about its quality. Powdery mildew
on leaves and grey rot at ripeness can be problems. Some berry shedding at
maturity also makes harvest timing and handling relevant.

## Aroma and winemaking

Young Colombard can show grapefruit, tropical fruit, and boxwood-like aromas
over a lively acid structure. Volatile thiols contribute to this family of
aromas. They are released during fermentation from odourless grape precursors,
so yeast activity helps create the wine's aromatic expression rather than
merely preserving a perfume already present in the berries.

Handling juice and wine with protection from excessive
[oxidation](../concepts/oxidation.md) helps retain that expression. Aroma can
change with yeast choice and storage, and a wine designed around fresh fruit
need not benefit from prolonged ageing. In blends with
[Gros Manseng](gros-manseng.md) or
[Sauvignon Blanc](sauvignon-blanc.md), the proportions and harvest decisions
alter both the aromatic emphasis and the balance of alcohol, acidity, and
any [residual sugar](../concepts/residual-sugar.md).
