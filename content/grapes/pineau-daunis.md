# Pineau d’Aunis

Pineau d’Aunis is a dark-skinned Loire grape used for light-coloured red and
rosé wines, and sometimes for sparkling-wine base. The name also carries an
unusually tangled history: for part of the late nineteenth century, the
variety was more often called *Chenin noir*.

## History and identity

Plantgrape lists Pineau d’Aunis as a French wine grape of the Loire Valley and
records no officially recognized synonym in France or elsewhere in the
European Union. The historical name *Chenin noir* is still useful when reading
older sources, but it has fallen out of ordinary use. It should not be confused
with [Chenin Blanc](chenin-blanc.md), whose name became the enduring one for the
white Loire variety.

The surviving nineteenth-century record is difficult partly because *pinot* and
*pineau* were then written for both Burgundy and Loire grapes. Henri Galinié
traces the name Pineau d’Aunis through references from the early nineteenth
century, then follows the late-century preference for *Chenin noir* as
ampelographers tried to separate Loire plants from Burgundian Pinots. The
earlier name returned during the twentieth century as that terminology became
more settled.[^1]

That history does not establish the popular stories about a vine selected at a
priory near Dampierre, or about Henry III Plantagenet sending its wine to
England. Galinié rejects those accounts. His own proposal—that the variety came
to the Loire from or through Aunis near La Rochelle, with its deeper origin to
be sought in southwestern France—is explicitly a hypothesis, not a settled
birthplace.[^1]

## Viticulture

Pineau d’Aunis breaks bud about eight days after Chasselas and reaches
mid-season maturity roughly two and a half weeks after it, in Plantgrape’s
comparative system. It is moderately vigorous and fertile when pruned short,
but production can alternate from one year to the next. Compact bunches make
grey rot a concern; the variety can also be susceptible to browning and, in
some sites, chlorosis.

The small berries and medium bunches do not automatically produce a dark wine.
Plantgrape describes the finished wines as light and not very coloured, and
warns that quality falls quickly as yields rise. Crop level is therefore an
important practical choice, since excessive production risks lowering quality.

## Wine character and cellar choices

Pineau d’Aunis is associated with pale reds, rosés, and base wines for
sparkling wine.

## Where it is grown and its modern return

The grape’s historic Loire range includes the Vendômois and parts of the
central Loire. Galinié’s review describes it as a minor presence in the
nineteenth-century Saumurois, where [Cabernet Franc](cabernet-franc.md) was more important. In the
“Aire géographique” passage he reproduces, Alfred Bouchard’s 1901 notice
attributes the disappearance of Chenin noir from some Anjou and Chinonais
vineyards to the Cabernets’ more full-bodied wines.[^1]

Plantgrape records 448 hectares in France in 2018, down from the 1,741 hectares
recorded in 1958. It also records a 150-clone conservatory planted in the
Vendômois in 1997.
Galinié notes renewed interest among attentive growers late in the twentieth
century.[^1]

[^1]: Henri Galinié, *Le Pineau d’Aunis, Recherches sur l’histoire des cépages
    de Loire, 1*, 2014, especially the résumé, abbreviated version, and
    chronological discussion of nineteenth-century names and provenance.
