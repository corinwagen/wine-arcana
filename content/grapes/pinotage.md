# Pinotage

Pinotage is a South African red grape created from [Pinot Noir](pinot-noir.md)
and [Cinsault](cinsault.md). Its name joins Pinot to Hermitage, the former
South African name for Cinsault. Both parents belong to *Vitis vinifera*;
Pinotage is a crossing within that species.

The wines span bright, comparatively light reds and concentrated wines with
firm structure and substantial oak influence. That range is useful context
for a variety whose reputation has sometimes been dominated by a narrow set
of powerful or awkward examples.

## History

Abraham Izak Perold made the cross at Stellenbosch. The Pinotage Association's
current historical account dates the crossing to 1924 and the planting of its
seeds to 1925. Older accounts commonly give 1925 as the year of the cross;
the distinction between pollination and seed planting explains the chronology
used here.[^1]

Cinsault was the seed parent and Pinot Noir the pollen parent. DNA analysis
later confirmed the parentage. Commercial planting followed in the early
1940s, and a wine labelled Pinotage appeared in 1961.[^2] Its establishment
as a wine category thus followed the original breeding work by decades.

## Viticulture

Pinotage ripens early and generally has moderate vigour, erect growth, and
compact bunches of small, thick-skinned berries. Its fertility permits spur
pruning. These characteristics help explain its usefulness as an early red
harvest, though ripening dates still vary with site and season.

The Pinotage Association emphasizes soils with adequate water-holding
capacity and matching rootstocks to the site's conditions. Vine health also
matters: the
association notes that virus infection can delay maturity, complicating a
simple comparison of harvest dates between vineyards.

South Africa remains the grape's principal home, with important associations
in Stellenbosch, Paarl, and the area around Malmesbury. Small plantings
elsewhere, including Australia and New Zealand, have not displaced that
regional identity.

## Wine character and reputation

Pinotage can give substantial colour and firm [tannin](../concepts/tannin.md). Plum and cherry
characters occur across different styles; oak can add coffee, chocolate, or
smoky impressions. Recent reporting on South African producers also describes
fresher, more lightly handled wines whose appeal rests on fruit and
drinkability.
Those examples broaden the picture formed by heavily extracted, strongly
wooded bottlings.

Burnt-rubber and solvent-like descriptions have played a part in Pinotage's
uneven reputation. They should not be treated as obligatory varietal
characters. A wine's condition and the choices made in growing and fermentation
need to be considered before using an unattractive example to describe the
whole grape.

## In the cellar

The Pinotage Association gives different fermentation guidance for fruity
wines intended for early drinking and fuller wines destined for wood
maturation. Temperature, the proportion of intact berries, and skin contact
can shift the balance between aromatic fruit and extraction.

Pressing before fermentation finishes is one established approach. Longer
skin contact is a separate decision that requires suitable fruit and regular
tasting, rather than following automatically from the variety's colour.
[Oak maturation](../concepts/oak-maturation.md) can then change both texture
and aroma; its duration and the wood used should fit the intended style.
