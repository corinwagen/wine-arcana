# Gros Manseng

Gros Manseng is a white grape from the Pyrenean foothills of southwestern
France. Its ability to combine substantial sugar with retained
[acidity](../concepts/acidity.md) supports both dry and sweet wine. It is
especially useful in regional blends, where it can contribute freshness and
weight together, rather than functioning simply as a lighter substitute for
[Petit Manseng](petit-manseng.md).

## Identity and regional roles

Gros Manseng and Petit Manseng are separate varieties. The size distinction
in their names is relative: Gros Manseng has larger berries than Petit,
but Plantgrape still describes its berries as very small. Gros Manseng is
also more productive. These differences help explain its wider practical
role in dry wine, while Petit Manseng is particularly associated with
concentrated late-harvest styles.

Jurançon sec provides a familiar expression, often blending Gros Manseng with
other local white grapes. It also appears in the whites of Pacherenc du
Vic-Bilh, Saint Mont, and Irouléguy. In Côtes de Gascogne, varietal wines and
blends with [Colombard](colombard.md) or
[Sauvignon Blanc](sauvignon-blanc.md) give it a different setting, with an
emphasis on readily accessible fruit. Sweet Gascon examples are important
too; neither a regional name nor the grape itself establishes dryness.

## Viticulture and ripening

Plantgrape describes a vigorous, fertile vine that can be pruned either long
or short. Its productivity makes crop management important to the ripening
target. A large crop destined for fresh dry wine and a smaller crop left for
concentration impose different demands on the vine and season.

Gros Manseng ripens late. It is relatively resistant to grey rot but
susceptible to powdery mildew, so suitability for a long growing season does
not remove the need to protect the canopy and fruit. Thick skins can permit
on-vine drying in favourable autumn weather. Even before deliberate drying,
however, sugar may become high enough to complicate dry winemaking.

## Fermentation and wine character

High sugar and high acidity create a choice rather than a fixed style.
Fermenting the available sugar fully can yield a powerful dry wine. Retaining
some sugar produces a softer or distinctly sweet balance, depending on its
amount and the remaining acidity. The IFV's work on Gros Manseng identifies
this sugar–acid combination as a practical challenge when the intended wine
is dry.[^1]

Citrus and tropical-fruit expression also depends on winemaking. The IFV
identifies volatile thiols among the compounds relevant to Gros Manseng's
aroma. Yeast can release these from odourless precursors during fermentation;
juice handling, yeast choice, and subsequent [oxidation](../concepts/oxidation.md) therefore affect how
strongly they appear. This helps explain why two Gros Manseng wines can differ
even before their blending partners or sweetness are considered.

The useful comparison with Petit Manseng is consequently one of crop,
concentration, and intended wine. Gros Manseng can make a brisk young blend,
a fuller dry white, or a sweet wine. Treating it only as the less prestigious
Manseng obscures the choices that make those wines different.

[^1]: Institut Français de la Vigne et du Vin, “Influence de l'itinéraire de
    vinification sur le potentiel aromatique des vins blancs de Gros Manseng,”
    *La Grappe d'Autan* 92, December 2012, pp. 2–4.
