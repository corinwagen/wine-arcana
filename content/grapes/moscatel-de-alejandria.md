# Muscat of Alexandria

Muscat of Alexandria is a white *Vitis vinifera* grape with large berries, an
evident muscat aroma, and an unusually broad role: it is eaten fresh, dried as
raisins, and made into wine. In Spanish it is Moscatel de Alejandría; in Italy,
especially on Pantelleria, it is Zibibbo. All are names for one variety whose
large berries and capacity to accumulate sugar have suited it to warm
Mediterranean viticulture.

The Muscat family contains several distinct, sometimes related varieties.
Muscat of Alexandria and [Muscat Blanc à Petits
Grains](muscat-blanc-a-petits-grains.md) are closely related and important
parents within the wider group. Their pronounced grape-and-flower perfume comes
largely from monoterpenes,
aroma compounds already present in the fruit. Dryness, sweetness, fortification,
and bottle age can nevertheless make their wines seem very different.

## History and identity

The variety is often called ancient, which reasonably describes its long
Mediterranean cultivation but does not prove that a modern vine is the grape of
classical texts. Its name has encouraged an Egyptian origin story. SSR analysis
identifies it as the offspring of Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains and Axina de Tres
Bias, a variety also catalogued as Heptakilo.[^1] That parentage points to a
Mediterranean family history, but it cannot by itself establish the place or
date at which the cross first occurred.

Its regional names record a wide circulation. French varietal authorities list
Zibibbo in Italy and Malta, Moscatel Graúdo in Portugal, and Damaszener Muskat
in Austria as official names in those countries and recognize them for
propagation material in France.[^2] Names require care on a bottle or in a
vineyard record: “Muscat” alone can denote several
unrelated or more distant varieties, and local wine names can refer to a style
as well as a grape.

## A grape for heat and for the table

Muscat of Alexandria ripens late in French reference conditions and needs a
substantial accumulation of heat for its fruit and wood to mature well. Its
growth habit is drooping and its vigour relatively low; it is generally
short-pruned and trained as a goblet. Plantgrape also describes it as suited to
drought and gravelly, acidic, or decalcified soils.[^2] Water availability, crop
level, sun exposure, and harvest date govern the balance among sugar
accumulation, freshness, and berry condition.

The large bunches and very large berries help explain the grape's table-grape
and raisin uses. The European specification for Pasas de Málaga identifies
Moscatel de Alejandría as the variety for those sun-dried raisins and calls out
its large berries.[^3] Wine production asks for a different balance. Fully ripe
fruit can supply sugar for sweet or fortified wines. Plantgrape reports
susceptibility to powdery mildew, grey rot, and insect damage.[^2]

## From aromatic fruit to sweet wine

Linalool, geraniol, nerol, and related monoterpenes are important to the grape's
characteristic muscat profile. Their concentration and form vary with ripeness,
and many are concentrated in the skins. Fermentation and ageing then alter what
remains perceptible.[^4] A dry Moscatel can therefore remain strongly aromatic while
containing little residual sugar; a sweet version may gain weight from retained
grape sugar without losing all sense of freshness.

In southern Spain, Moscatel de Alejandría is associated with sweet wines and
with the sandy coastal ground around Chipiona in [Jerez](../regions/jerez.md).
For naturally sweet [Sherry](../styles/sherry.md) sold as Moscatel, must from
overripe or raisined grapes is fortified before or during fermentation,
preserving grape sugar, then aged oxidatively for at least two years. The Jerez
specification requires at least 85% Moscatel must but permits it with or without
raisining, so *asoleo* is common rather than universal.[^5] These cellar choices
drive the change from fresh fruit toward darker, developed aromas.

Pantelleria offers a useful contrast. Under the island's PDO, Zibibbo is the
permitted grape for Moscato di Pantelleria, Passito di Pantelleria, Bianco and
Frizzante, sparkling Moscato, and the *liquoroso* wines.[^6] Passito is made
from dried grapes, concentrating their sugars before pressing; the *liquoroso*
categories follow a fortified route. These wines share aromatic fruit, but
their sweetness, alcohol, and texture follow separate choices about drying and
fermentation. Muscat of Alexandria consequently supports a broad range of
warm-climate wines as well as table grapes and raisins.
