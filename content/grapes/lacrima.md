# Lacrima

Lacrima is a dark-skinned grape associated with Morro d'Alba and the surrounding
hills near Ancona in Marche. Its conspicuous floral character, often described
in terms of rose and violet, makes it distinctive among local reds.
Its local identity is expressed most directly in
Lacrima di Morro d'Alba, while contemporary producers also use it for rosé,
sparkling wine and sweet styles.

Morro d'Alba is in central Italy, a different place from Alba in Piedmont.
Keeping the full place name in view helps distinguish this small regional
tradition from the better-known wines of the Langhe.

## Vineyard choices

Vivai Cooperativi Rauscedo describes an early-budding, vigorous vine with
mid-season ripening and variable production. It also records susceptibility
to bunch rot. Growers therefore have to consider both the spring risks that
come with early growth and the health of the fruit as harvest approaches.
[Canopy management](../concepts/canopy-management.md) connects those practical
decisions with the eventual wine by changing shade and ventilation around
the bunches.

The nursery's account emphasizes the local red-wine use of the grape. Present
producer ranges show a wider set of possibilities, making the cellar an
important part of understanding Lacrima's current relevance.

## One grape, several cellar routes

Marotti Campi's range offers a particularly clear comparison. Rubico matures
in steel; its current technical sheet also describes a small contribution
fermented as whole bunches with [carbonic maceration](../concepts/carbonic-maceration.md).
Orgiolo, its Superiore, spends time in used barriques. Both
are Lacrima di Morro d'Alba wines, but the second receives a different
maturation treatment. Used barrels can affect development and texture while
contributing less conspicuous wood flavour than new ones; vessel choice and
age are separate questions in [oak maturation](../concepts/oak-maturation.md).

The same producer makes a Lacrima rosé and a tank-fermented Brut Rosé.
Producing bubbles in a closed tank captures the carbon dioxide of
fermentation, giving the grape another expression without requiring the
bottle-fermentation process of traditional-method sparkling wine. Its Xyris,
described as partially fermented Lacrima must, represents a further departure
from dry red wine.

These are specific production choices, not interchangeable versions of the
same finished drink. They give Lacrima a contemporary role beyond a single
appellation style, while keeping the grape's cultivation and commercial
identity rooted in Marche.
