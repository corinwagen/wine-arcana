# Sousón

Sousón is a dark-skinned *Vitis vinifera* variety of northwestern Iberia,
grown in Galicia under its Spanish name and in northern Portugal principally
as Vinhão. It combines two properties that do not always travel together in a
red grape: abundant colour and pronounced acidity. This makes it especially
useful in the multi-grape reds of Atlantic-influenced Galicia and the Vinho
Verde region, where it can deepen a blend without sacrificing freshness.

Its colour is more than a visual curiosity. Sousón can give a relatively pale,
fragrant partner such as [Brancellao](brancellao.md) a darker frame, or add
both pigment and tension to a blend led by [Mencía](mencia.md). Varietal wines
also exist, but they expose the same balance directly: fruit must ripen far
enough for tannin and acidity to feel integrated, while extraction must be
controlled so that colour does not arrive with unnecessary hardness.

## One vine, several names

Genetic comparison identifies Galician Sousón as Vinhão, the prime name used
by the Vitis International Variety Catalogue.[^1] The two names therefore
describe regional identities of one cultivar rather than similar Spanish and
Portuguese grapes. Older Galician names include Retinto, Tintilla, Pazao, and
Viñón.

Portuguese terminology needs more care. Current Vinho Verde rules list Vinhão
under code PRT51902 and recognize Sousão as its synonym, while listing Sezão
separately under PRT51901.[^2] The spelling Souzão is also widespread,
particularly outside Portugal and in writing about the Douro. A familiar name
on its own is consequently weaker evidence of identity than a genetic record
or a modern official variety code.

The exact birthplace is not documented. A maintained Portuguese reference
finds names associated with Vinhão in Minho sources from 1790 and the present
name in the nineteenth century. Galicia's viticultural station traces Sousón,
Retinto, and Viñón in several Galician districts to a 1914 survey. These
records establish a long history on both sides of the modern border, with
earlier documentation presently known in Portugal; they cannot locate the
original seedling.

## Vine and Atlantic season

Sousón ripens late. That long cycle helps explain its acidity, because the
grapes can remain relatively acid as sugars and skin compounds develop. It
also creates risk. In a cool site or difficult autumn, harvest may be forced
before tannins settle; in a warmer, well-exposed parcel, the same variety can
reach greater sugar and phenolic maturity while retaining a firm acid line.
“High acid” is therefore a useful tendency, not a promise of identical
balance in every wine.

The vine has medium to strong growth and can crop regularly, depending on
plant material and site. Galician observations report moderate sensitivity to
downy mildew, powdery mildew, and botrytis, while Portuguese records describe
some tolerance to the two mildews. These accounts are not necessarily
incompatible: disease pressure, clone, canopy density, and the standard of
comparison differ. In the humid northwest, airflow and sound fruit still
matter for a late grape that may remain exposed to autumn rain.

Vinhão is also reported as vulnerable to drought stress and sunburn. Its
association with rainy Atlantic districts should not be converted into a
general claim of climatic toughness. Moving inland can assist ripening, but
hot exposure and limited water introduce different constraints.

## Why the wines are so dark

Anthocyanins are the red, purple, and blue pigments extracted principally from
grape skins during fermentation. Sousón accumulates a large quantity of them
in its dark blue-black skins, and Portuguese descriptions also record some
pigment in the pulp. It is not uniformly red-fleshed like a classic teinturier
grape, but the combination gives it unusual colouring capacity. A two-year
comparison of 27 red varieties grown at one Portuguese site placed Vinhão
among the high-anthocyanin varieties, while also finding that its total changed
substantially between years.[^3] Genetic potential is strong; season still
changes what reaches the cellar.

Colour and acidity arise from different parts of berry development, so one
does not cancel the other. Galician and Portuguese institutional records both
describe wines with intense violet or red-purple colour and high natural
acidity. Less mature fruit can make the acidity and tannin feel severe, while
warmer conditions or a later harvest may bring more alcohol and breadth.
Selective references to dark fruit, herbs, or balsamic aromas are useful, but
no fixed descriptor set follows from the grape name.

Winemaking determines how much of the available pigment and tannin appears in
the wine. Longer or more active skin contact generally extracts more, while
gentler handling can preserve colour without maximizing grip. Malolactic
fermentation softens the sharper malic component of acidity, and maturation
changes both tannin and pigment. Deep colour is therefore a capacity of the
fruit, not proof that every Sousón wine was heavily extracted or will retain
the same purple hue with age.

## Blending across the border

In a Galician blend, Sousón often acts as a structural component rather than a
neutral darkener. Alongside Mencía, Brancellao, [Caíño Tinto](caino-tinto.md),
and other local grapes, it can supply colour, acidity, and tannic definition;
its partners may contribute a different aromatic emphasis, softer texture, or
earlier ripeness. The proportions cannot be reduced to a standard recipe,
because a small addition of very pigmented fruit may be enough in one vintage
while a cooler year may make its acidity more conspicuous.

The same logic operates in northern Portugal. A Portuguese varietal reference
specifically recommends Vinhão for blending with lower-colour Borraçal, the
Portuguese name for Caíño Tinto. Vinhão is also capable of traditional red
Vinho Verde as a varietal wine, so “blending grape” describes a common
function, not a biological limitation or a lower tier of wine.

Its legal geography reflects this cross-border role. As of July 2026, Spain's
agriculture ministry listed Sousón as a principal variety in all five Galician
protected denominations of origin: Monterrei, Rías Baixas, Ribeira Sacra,
Ribeiro, and Valdeorras.[^4] Portugal's consolidated Vinho Verde rules
authorize Vinhão throughout the denomination and in each of its nine named
subregions. Authorization indicates where the grape may be used; it does not
require it, prescribe a single blend, or guarantee that plantings are equally
important across those regions.
