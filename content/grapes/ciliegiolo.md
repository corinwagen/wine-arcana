# Ciliegiolo

Ciliegiolo is a dark-skinned *Vitis vinifera* grape associated with central
Italy, especially Tuscany's Maremma and the Narni area of
[Umbria](../regions/umbria.md). It has a long role in blends with
[Sangiovese](sangiovese.md), contributing fruit and roundness. Varietal wines
now give it a more visible identity, from youthful reds and rosato to wines
selected for extended maturation.

Cherry is a familiar reference for its fruit character. The more useful
structural distinction is its tendency toward relatively modest acidity,
although site, vine material, harvest timing, and extraction can produce
markedly different balances.

## Identity and origins

The Italian Vitis Database lists Morettone as an official synonym. It
questions the identification with Soderini's *Ciregiuolo dolce* of 1590 and
regards the story of introduction by pilgrims returning from Santiago de
Compostela as unconfirmed.[^history]

Genetic studies establish a close relationship with Sangiovese. A large 2021
analysis using single-nucleotide polymorphisms, or SNPs, identified Ciliegiolo
as an offspring of Sangiovese and Moscato Violetto. It rejected earlier
proposals that placed Ciliegiolo among Sangiovese's parents.[^genetics] This
provides a specific modern pedigree interpretation while leaving the place
and date of the original seedling unresolved.

## Growing and picking

Ciliegiolo is vigorous and can crop heavily. Rauscedo describes large bunches
and berries, early ripening, and a preference for warm, relatively dry sites
with soils that do not encourage excessive growth. Longer pruning is commonly
recommended. The Italian Vitis Database connects that recommendation with
limited fertility in the basal buds, the buds closest to the base of a shoot.

Ciliegiolo generally ripens before Sangiovese, making separate picking useful
where they grow together. The Pisa database also notes that waiting for seed
phenolic maturity can leave Ciliegiolo short of acidity. Harvest timing thus
balances freshness against the development of material extracted during
[maceration](../concepts/maceration.md).

## Maremma and the range of red wines

The Maremma around Grosseto remains an important Tuscan home, including areas
around Scansano, Manciano, and Pitigliano. The regional consortium describes
wines with red-fruit character and a rounded palate, with more spice in some
mature examples. Liguria and other parts of central Italy also retain the
grape, but Maremma and Narni offer particularly useful comparisons of its
contemporary varietal wines.

Sassotondo's San Lorenzo comes from a single vineyard near Pitigliano, on
volcanic soils. The estate reports preserving its older vine material through
mass selection: propagating from selected vines within the vineyard. Its
published method uses fermentation and skin contact in steel followed by
18–24 months in large Slavonian oak and a further period in bottle. That
approach gives extraction and maturation a substantial role in the wine.

Ciliegiolo's fruit and relatively supple profile can also suit earlier
release. Rauscedo's general description favours young consumption, while its
clone notes describe differences in acidity and structure. The contrast with
San Lorenzo illustrates why a grape's usual profile is a starting point for
understanding a wine, with the vineyard and cellar determining the result.

## Narni: red wine and rosato

Around Narni and San Gemini in southern Umbria, Ciliegiolo is the focus of
varietal bottlings that broaden the region's red-wine identity beyond
Sangiovese and [Sagrantino](sagrantino.md). Leonardo Bussoletti's Brecciaro
provides a concrete example. Made entirely from Ciliegiolo, it combines fruit
from rocky and clay-limestone sites. The producer matures most of the wine
in steel on its fine lees and the remainder in large French oak before
blending and bottling.

This divides the maturation between vessels with different effects. Steel
adds no wood flavour, while [oak maturation](../concepts/oak-maturation.md)
can contribute both wood compounds and gradual oxygen exposure. Keeping most
of a wine in steel allows oak to play a supporting role.

Bussoletti also makes Asla Ciliegiolo Rosé from vineyards at San Gemini,
fermenting the wine and then keeping it on its fine lees in steel. It offers
another local expression of the grape through [rosato](../styles/rose.md).
A producer's red and rosato bottlings are useful companions for understanding
how the treatment of a single variety changes the wine's colour and texture.
