# Xinomavro

Xinomavro is a dark-skinned *Vitis vinifera* grape central to northern
Greek wine. Its name combines the Greek words for sour and black. The
wines often have marked [acidity](../concepts/acidity.md) and tannin, with relatively modest colour,
and can develop for years in bottle. Naoussa and Amyndeon are important
centres for varietal wines; Goumenissa and Rapsani have established blending
traditions.

The grape's uses extend to [rosé](../styles/rose.md), sparkling wine, and white wine pressed
from dark grapes. These styles draw on its acidity while extracting less
of the skins' and seeds' tannin than a full red-wine fermentation.

## Colour and tannin develop differently

Xinomavro's skins and seeds contain substantial tannin, while its supply
of red pigments is often more limited. [Tannin](../concepts/tannin.md)
contributes drying grip; anthocyanins supply the red colour. Their different
concentrations help explain why a relatively pale Xinomavro can feel
formidable when young.

During ripening, pigments accumulate and the composition and extractability
of tannins change. Growers seeking a structured red therefore need to
assess the skins and seeds alongside sugar and acidity. A ripe sugar
reading alone gives an incomplete view of the fruit's readiness for
that style.

Water supply affects these processes. A two-year study near Thessaloniki
found that the timing of irrigation after berries began ripening altered
both pigment levels and tannin composition. Water supplied later in
ripening produced a different balance from irrigation begun immediately
after colour change, with fewer reductions in pigment concentration.[^1]
The result illustrates why irrigation timing matters alongside the amount
of water supplied.

## Site and intended wine

Xinomavro ripens late, and its performance varies with planting material,
soil, and climate. In warmer sites with enough season to ripen the fruit,
it can produce substantial reds. Cooler, elevated vineyards can retain
the freshness useful for rosé and sparkling wine.

Amyndeon shows how these uses coexist. Domaine Karanika draws fruit from
several villages and describes marked differences among its sites. It
uses some warmer vineyards for red Xinomavro, while fruit from the colder
Pedino area contributes to sparkling cuvées. Around Lake Petron, it also
uses Xinomavro fruit for blanc de noirs. The intended wine gives these
vineyards different harvest goals.

For [traditional-method sparkling wine](../styles/traditional-method-sparkling-wine.md),
the base wine needs enough acidity to remain fresh
after a second fermentation and maturation.
[Lees ageing](../concepts/lees-aging.md) in bottle can then develop texture
and soften the impression of the young wine.

## Aroma, age, and blends

Red-fruit aromas often accompany savoury impressions of tomato and olive.
As a concentrated red matures, dried-fruit and tobacco-like notes can
emerge. [Oak maturation](../concepts/oak-maturation.md) adds its own spice,
while changes in tannin and aroma gradually alter the wine's balance.

In Goumenissa, Xinomavro is blended with Negoska, whose fuller fruit and
softer structure can round out the wine. Rapsani combines it with Krassato
and Stavroto. These local partners produce a different balance from a
varietal Naoussa, where Xinomavro's own fruit, acidity, and tannin carry
the wine.

    regimes in 2015 and 2016; its findings concern grape composition.
