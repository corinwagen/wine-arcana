# Touriga Nacional

Touriga Nacional is a dark-skinned Portuguese grape central to both Port and
dry red wine. It is strongly associated with the Douro, but its older connection
with Dão and its present distribution across Portugal make it misleading to
treat it as a Douro variety alone. Small berries, relatively thick skins, and
marked aromatic potential allow it to contribute colour, tannin, and perfume to
a blend without necessarily providing most of the volume.

Those traits do not produce one fixed style. Site and water supply affect berry
size and ripening; maceration determines how much material is extracted from
skins and seeds; and fortification changes the balance entirely. Touriga
Nacional can therefore appear as one component of a sweet fortified Port, the
structural and aromatic centre of a dry blend, or a dry varietal wine.

## History and identity

Touriga Nacional's precise birthplace is not established. A maintained
Portuguese varietal reference traces the name *Touriga* to accounts published
in 1790 that place it in both the Douro and the Beiras, the broad area that
includes Dão.[^1] Historic names preserve both associations: Touriga Fina was
used in the Douro, while Tourigo and Preto Mortágua were used in Dão. This
evidence supports a northern Portuguese origin, but a record in one region does
not prove that the original seedling arose there.

The variety was historically known for low, irregular crops, partly because
some old vine material set fruit poorly. Selection within its genetically
varied population produced clones capable of more regular yields. Modern
Touriga Nacional is consequently not uniform: clone, crop level, and vineyard
conditions help explain why older descriptions of an exceptionally
unproductive vine do not fit every planting today.

## Viticulture

The vine is vigorous and tends to make a spreading or trailing canopy, so
training, pruning, and shoot positioning must keep growth from shading the
fruit. It buds relatively early. Ripening is usually classed in the middle of
the season in reference collections, although growers in the Douro may wait
until late in the local harvest for seeds and skins to mature. Calendar labels
are therefore less useful than the relationship among sugar, acidity, and
phenolic maturity in a particular site.

Clusters are small to medium and the berries are small, with thick skins. A
small berry has more skin relative to its juice than a large berry of similar
shape, all else being equal. Because pigments and much of a red grape's tannin
are located in the skins, with further tannin in the seeds, this anatomy gives
the winemaker considerable extractive material. It does not guarantee a dark or
severe wine: berry size varies with clone and water supply, while extraction
depends on crushing, temperature, time, and cap management.

Touriga Nacional generally tolerates bunch rot and the major mildews reasonably
well, but it is susceptible to excoriose, a cane and shoot disease. It needs
enough sun and warmth to ripen fully, yet that should not be confused with
unlimited heat tolerance. Severe water stress and exposed fruit can lead to
sunburn and stalled or uneven development. Canopy shade and water availability
therefore remain important even in warm, dry regions.

## Aroma, tannin, and wine character

Touriga Nacional's floral reputation has chemical support, but no single
compound or aroma defines every wine. A three-year comparison of eight Douro
varieties found Touriga Nacional wines highest in total free terpenols,
including linalool, alpha-terpineol, nerol, and geraniol. The study also found
comparatively abundant beta-ionone, a norisoprenoid associated with violet-like
aroma.[^2] Fermentation, blending, oxygen exposure, and maturation can amplify,
transform, or obscure that grape-derived potential.

Young wines can consequently be intensely aromatic, often with a floral lift
over dark-fruit character, but structure is just as important. Thick skins and
seeds can supply substantial tannin, while the berries can accumulate ample
sugar and colour. Earlier picking may retain more acidity but leave firmer,
less-developed tannin; waiting can improve phenolic maturity while increasing
potential alcohol and the risk of heat or dehydration. Gentle extraction can
make a more immediately open wine, whereas longer maceration may build a firmer
frame for maturation. Oak can alter aroma and texture, but it is a cellar choice
rather than a property of the grape.

## Port and dry Portuguese reds

Touriga Nacional is one of the recommended varieties for Port, not a required
single-variety formula. In the Douro it commonly joins Touriga Franca,
[Tinta Roriz](tempranillo.md), Tinta Barroca, Tinto Cão, and other grapes. Its
colour, tannin, and aroma are useful in that blend, while other components may
bring more volume, acidity, or a different ripening pattern. The distinction
matters: official 2023 figures put Touriga Nacional at 10.3% of Douro vineyard
area, behind both Touriga Franca and Tinta Roriz.[^3]

Port production also changes how the raw material is expressed. Red grapes are
worked to extract colour, tannin, and aroma during a short fermentation; grape
spirit is then added to stop fermentation while sugar remains. The same grapes
fermented dry have more time for alcohol and extraction to develop before the
wine is separated from its solids. A dry Touriga Nacional is therefore not Port
without fortification, nor is a Port simply a sweeter version of the same dry
wine.

Dry reds now give the variety an identity independent of fortified wine. In the
Douro it may lead a blend or be bottled alone. Dão, with generally higher,
granite-based vineyards sheltered by mountains, is an equally important
reference for dry wines; its cooler nights and different water conditions can
shift the balance toward firmer acidity and less immediate weight. Neither
region guarantees a style, because elevation, exposure, crop, harvest date, and
cellar practice vary within both.

## Beyond the Douro

The scale of Touriga Nacional outside the Douro is not merely symbolic. In
2023 the Institute of Vine and Wine recorded 12,567 hectares on mainland
Portugal, or 7.1% of the vineyard, making it the third-most-planted variety.
The Douro accounted for 4,432 hectares in the same year, so most mainland
Touriga Nacional lay elsewhere.[^4] Beyond Dão, it is now encountered in regions
including Alentejo, Lisboa, Tejo, Beira Interior, and Trás-os-Montes, usually in
dry blends or varietal wines. Warmer sites can emphasize sugar and breadth;
higher or more exposed sites may preserve more acidity, subject to water and
wind.

International plantings remain small beside Portugal's, so their importance
should not be overstated. They nevertheless show a widening role. France added
Touriga Nacional to its official vine catalogue in 2012; recorded French area
rose from 1 hectare in 2008 to 11.7 hectares in 2018, still a very limited
base. Wine Australia's 2025 vintage report records Touriga Nacional crushes
across several regions in South Australia, Victoria, and Western
Australia.[^5] South Africa, California, and Brazil provide further
warm-climate examples. These sites are testing a useful combination of colour,
aroma, and heat-season ripening, but local results, rather than Portuguese
reputation, determine whether the variety is well suited.
