# Casavecchia

Casavecchia is a red grape associated with the hills around Pontelatone in
the province of Caserta, northern Campania. Its revival forms part of a wider
effort to bottle local varieties individually, alongside
[Pallagrello Nero](pallagrello-nero.md) and
[Pallagrello Bianco](pallagrello-bianco.md).

## A recovery story with uncertain beginnings

A frequently repeated account traces the surviving grape to a vine growing
by an abandoned house near Pontelatone, explaining the name *casa vecchia*,
“old house.” Alois presents this as a local legend. Its further association
with an ancient Roman wine should also be treated as a historical hypothesis,
not as an established identification of the modern cultivar.[^1]

The contemporary recovery is more concrete. A 2026 account of Vestini
Campagnano describes its early commitment to Casavecchia and Pallagrello,
with a first harvest in 1998, and lists Casavecchia Riserva among its wines.
The grape also has a specific appellation context in Casavecchia di
Pontelatone, documented by Regione Campania.

## Understanding the wine through maturation

Alois's Trebulanum supplies a detailed example of Casavecchia intended for
extended maturation. The producer describes October harvesting in the Monti
Trebulani, fermentation with skin contact in large wooden vessels,
[malolactic fermentation](../concepts/malolactic-fermentation.md), and two
years in wood followed by two in bottle.

Each stage does different work. [Maceration](../concepts/maceration.md)
extracts colour and tannin from the fruit; malolactic conversion changes the
acid balance; the subsequent time in wood and bottle develops the wine after
fermentation. A bottle produced this way reflects both the grape and a long
series of cellar choices. The schedule belongs to Trebulanum, rather than
defining a universal minimum for Casavecchia.

These wines make the revived variety visible as a substantial local red.
They also put its cultural significance in perspective: the present story
rests on growers propagating, cultivating and selling the grape, regardless
of how far back its ancestry can eventually be traced.

[^1]: Alois, “Casavecchia,” explicitly presenting the ruined-house account
    as legend and the Roman connection as an attribution.
