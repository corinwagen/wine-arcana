# Krakhuna

Krakhuna is a white grape associated with central Imereti in western
Georgia, including Sviri, Obcha, and Dimi. It can produce broad, ripe-fruited
whites and textured amber wines, with vinification making a substantial
difference to how that weight is expressed.

## Ripeness in a humid region

The National Wine Agency describes Krakhuna as accumulating sugar readily
while retaining acidity. Its thin-skinned berries and susceptibility to
powdery mildew also make it a demanding crop in western Georgia's humid
conditions. The agency associates its wines with ripe apricot flavours and
notes its use alongside [Tsolikouri](tsolikouri.md) and [Tsitska](tsitska.md)
in regional blends.

Sugar accumulation and [acidity](../concepts/acidity.md) describe different
parts of the harvest decision. Sugar supplies the potential alcohol of a
dry wine; acid affects its balance. Neither alone determines how heavy the
finished wine will feel, especially when extraction and maturation add
further sources of texture.

## Qvevri and oak

Glekhuri's Krakhuna is a documented example of a modern Imeretian bottling
that combines Georgian and barrel-based cellar practices. The producer
lists six months in qvevri followed by twelve months in French oak, and
describes the result as a full-bodied dry wine.[^1] Its account does not
specify the proportion of skins retained, so the vessel alone cannot tell
us the extent of extraction.

In [skin-contact white wines](../styles/skin-contact-white-wine.md),
[maceration](../concepts/maceration.md) supplies phenolic compounds from the
grape solids, including tannins. Subsequent
[oak maturation](../concepts/oak-maturation.md) introduces another set of
choices about oxygen exposure, texture, and flavour. Glekhuri therefore
provides a useful example of how Krakhuna's contemporary identity in
Imereti includes wines shaped by successive vessels, as well as the
region's blending traditions.

[^1]: Glekhuri, “Krakhuna,” producer's wine description, accessed
    21 September 2026. The stated durations describe time in the vessels;
    they should not be read as six months of active fermentation.
