# Greco

Greco is a white *Vitis vinifera* grape associated especially with Campania
and the dry wines of Greco di Tufo. It combines freshness with enough
substance to make a firm, persistent white. Its inland vineyards around
Tufo give it a different setting from the coastal plain where the name
Asprinio belongs to a separate wine tradition.

Naming requires care. The Campanian grape discussed here is distinct from
the Calabrian variety catalogued as Greco Bianco, although some literature
also uses the words *Greco bianco* descriptively for the Tufo grape.[^1]

## Greco and Asprinio

DNA profiling has established a synonymy between Greco di Tufo and Asprinio.
In a Campanian study published in 2005, the sampled vines under those names
matched at the sixteen microsatellite markers examined. The Italian Vitis
Database also records the relationship.[^2] Asprinio and Greco remain useful
regional names because the growing conditions and wines associated with
them differ.

The wider Italian use of *greco* and *grechetto* has encompassed several
varieties and historical wine names. Those words alone cannot identify a
vine or establish its precise place of origin. The distinction matters
especially when reading old accounts of southern Italian wines.

## A long growing season

Greco ripens late. Its compact bunches sometimes have a large wing that
makes them look doubled. Budbreak classifications differ between technical
accounts, while both Rauscedo and the Italian Vitis Database describe a
late harvest. Exposure and autumn weather are important to completing
ripening.

Around Tufo, winters are cold and summer nights markedly cooler than days.
The vineyards include clay-rich ground, with different proportions of sand
and silt. Water held in the soil can support ripening through dry summer
weather. The denomination's rules favour well-exposed hillsides and exclude
damp, insufficiently sunny valley floors.[^3]

## Wine character

Greco can give floral and citrus impressions with an almond-like finish.
Clonal trials reported by Vivai Cooperativi Rauscedo show variation in fruit
aroma, acidity, and suitability for maturation. Some selections make
particularly fresh wines; others provide more body. The result in a bottle
also reflects harvest maturity and the producer's handling of the fruit.

Greco di Tufo requires at least 85% Greco, with up to 15% Coda di Volpe.
The defined area covers Tufo and seven neighbouring communes in the province
of Avellino. Alongside still wines, its rules permit sparkling wines made
by [second fermentation in bottle](../styles/traditional-method-sparkling-wine.md).
Those wines spend time on their yeast sediment, adding a distinct stage of
maturation to the grape's fresh base wine.[^3]
