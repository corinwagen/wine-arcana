# Fer Servadou

Fer Servadou is a southwestern French red grape encountered under several
regional names. It is Braucol in Gaillac, Mansois in Marcillac, and Pinenc
in the Pyrenean foothills. Its wines combine freshness and firm tannin with
aromas that can move between green pepper and blackcurrant as fruit ripens.

## One grape, several regional roles

The names describe the same variety, but they place it in different wine
traditions.[^1] Marcillac, in Aveyron, gives Mansois a central role. A report
from the Institut national de l'origine et de la qualité (INAO)
describes the grape's long association with the valley's vineyards
and the revival of local winegrowing from the 1960s. Its account emphasizes
both red wine and a smaller [rosé](../styles/rose.md) tradition.

In Gaillac, Braucol is part of a more varied local repertoire. Farther west,
Plaimont uses the name Pinenc for the grape in Saint Mont and specifically
describes its use in fresh, spicy rosé. These contexts make the regional
name useful information, even when the biological identity is unchanged.

The variety belongs to southwestern France's established vineyard heritage.
Its precise birthplace is less secure: the Institut français de la vigne
et du vin (IFV) varietal profile proposes
the Gironde as a possibility. A more exact migration story would require
stronger evidence than the modern distribution of its names.

## Irregular fertility and harvest decisions

Fer Servadou can have missing or non-growing buds, making its fertility
irregular. Long pruning is commonly advised to retain enough productive
positions. Poor flowering conditions and excessive vigour or crop can also
lead to *coulure*, when flowers fail to set fruit.

The grape ripens toward the later part of the mid-season group in French
comparative observations. Resistance to grey rot is generally useful, though
leafhopper damage and trunk disease remain concerns. Clonal observations
reported by the IFV distinguish earlier- and later-ripening material, with
consequences for harvest composition and rot susceptibility.

## Why the herbal character changes

The green-pepper note has a specific contributor: isobutyl methoxypyrazine,
usually shortened to IBMP. The IFV describes this compound in the skins and
seeds and reports that its concentration declines as grapes mature.[^2]
Harvest ripeness can therefore change the balance between vegetal and
fruit-led aromas. Sugar concentration alone does not describe that aromatic
development.

Research has also detected eucalyptol in some Fer Servadou wines, a possible
contributor to mint-like impressions. Its interaction with IBMP helps explain
why a fresh herbal aroma is not always perceived simply as green pepper.
These findings describe contributors within a mixture, rather than a fixed
aroma formula for every bottle.

Fer's [tannin](../concepts/tannin.md) and [acidity](../concepts/acidity.md) provide structure around
those aromas. The INAO's Marcillac account describes young reds with present
but unaggressive tannin, alongside wines capable of developing with age.
This regional example is useful beside the IFV's broader description of a
firm, sometimes rustic grape: ripeness and production choices materially
change how the structure feels.
