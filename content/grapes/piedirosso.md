# Piedirosso

Piedirosso is a red grape associated with Naples, the Campi Flegrei, Vesuvius
and the islands of coastal Campania. Its name refers to the reddish stalks
that support the berries; the local name Per'e Palummo invokes a pigeon's foot.
The wines often offer a relatively supple, fresh expression of southern
Italian red wine, with less imposing tannin than a heavily extracted
[Aglianico](aglianico.md).

## Growing the grape

Vivai Cooperativi Rauscedo describes a vigorous vine with a drooping growth
habit, suited to long pruning. Its profile places ripening in the middle to
later part of the season. Managing that growth means arranging enough useful
leaf area to ripen the crop while avoiding an excessively shaded interior.
[Pruning and training](../concepts/pruning-and-training.md) establish the
framework for that work.

The nursery records differences in colour and structure among Piedirosso
selections. Its moderate tannin and useful acidity help explain both varietal
wines and blends with Aglianico. Blending changes the proportions of these
contributions; it does not make the two varieties interchangeable.

## Vineyards within metropolitan Naples

The Campi Flegrei provide a particularly revealing contemporary setting.
Agnanum, the Moccia family's estate near Agnano and the Astroni, farms steep
terraces within the wider city landscape. The winery describes manual work
and the shaping of small basins around vines to retain water. Here the
physical demands of cultivation are part of keeping a local wine tradition
alive amid urban development.

The Naples branch of the Associazione Italiana Sommeliers documented the
estate's Piedirosso wines in 2013, including Pèr 'e Palumm and Vigna delle
Volpi. The family's continuing estate gives that account a present-day
context. It is a useful reference for understanding Piedirosso as a wine of
specific vineyards, alongside its role in broader coastal blends.

For the drinker, the grape offers a way into Campania beyond its more famous
inland reds. Acidity, extraction and the producer's maturation choices govern
how light or substantial a particular wine feels. The combination of a
specific coastal landscape and a relatively supple local red gives these
vineyards an identity of their own.
