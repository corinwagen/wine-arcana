# Pinot Blanc

Pinot Blanc is a white-skinned member of the Pinot family, known as Pinot
Bianco in Italy and Weißburgunder in Germany and Austria. Its place in that
family explains both its resemblance to [Pinot Gris](pinot-gris.md) and the
confusion that sometimes surrounds it: it is a colour mutation related to
[Pinot Noir](pinot-noir.md).

The grape is used in more than one way. [Alsace](../regions/alsace.md) uses it for fresh, dry white
wines and for Crémant; Germany has made it a substantial still-wine variety
with examples ranging from light dry wines to richer and sweet bottlings; and
[Alto Adige](../regions/alto-adige.md) treats Pinot Bianco as an important high-elevation white grape.

## Identity and regional names

Plantgrape identifies Pinot Blanc as the white mutation of Pinot Noir. In the
clones studied, genetic research supports a parallel model in which Pinot Blanc
and Pinot Gris arose as independent somatic mutations from ancestral Pinot
Noir.[^1] Ripe Pinot Blanc berries are greenish-yellow, while Pinot Gris berries
retain gray-pink or coppery colour; the vines remain similar enough that their
identity is easiest to establish from the whole set of varietal and genetic
features rather than berry colour alone.

[Chardonnay](chardonnay.md) is a separate grape, and a Pinot Blanc or Pinot Bianco label does
not identify it. In Alsace, the current AOC rules permit “Pinot blanc” for
either pinot blanc B or [auxerrois](auxerrois-blanc.md) B; “Pinot” or “Klevner” likewise includes
both. The label can therefore describe a regulated regional category rather
than Pinot Blanc alone.[^2]

## Viticulture and wine character

The French varietal record describes small to medium bunches, small berries,
vigorous growth, and regular production. Pinot Blanc is reported as somewhat
more productive than Pinot Gris or Pinot Noir and relatively resistant to cold,
but relatively sensitive to major vine diseases and slightly susceptible to
chlorosis. It buds early and ripens in the early part of the season, with deep
soils listed among suitable sites.

Plantgrape characterizes the wine broadly as light-bodied and moderately
acidic. Earlier harvest can preserve a pale, fresh style, while ripe fruit and
maturation can give more weight and texture. German examples are often
described as discreetly aromatic, with apple, pear, citrus, or nutty notes.

## Four regional roles

In **Alsace**, Pinot Blanc is one of the permitted white grapes of the AOC
Alsace. Its associated wines are generally dry and fresh, and the appellation's
rules distinguish the varietal designations from the broader blends sold as
Edelzwicker. The legal distinction between Pinot Blanc and Auxerrois is
important because they are different varieties.

In **Germany**, Weißburgunder has become an important planting, especially in
Baden, Rheinhessen, and the Pfalz. The German Wine Institute describes a wide
range: dry wines with moderate body and fine [acidity](../concepts/acidity.md),
more extract-rich or barrel-aged versions, and occasional late-harvest, sweet,
and sparkling wines.

In **Italy**, the name is Pinot Bianco. Alto Adige is a useful documented
example: the regional wine body places it among the area's leading white
varieties and associates it with higher sites, including vineyards reaching
about 800 metres. Its local presentation emphasizes fresh, dry wines with lively
acidity and restrained fruit.

For **sparkling wine**, Pinot Blanc's pale juice and moderate profile make it
useful as a base-wine grape. Plantgrape explicitly records this use, and the
Crémant d'Alsace category is made by the [traditional
method](../styles/traditional-method-sparkling-wine.md), principally from
Pinot Blanc but also from Pinot Gris, Pinot Noir, [Riesling](riesling.md), Auxerrois, or
Chardonnay.
