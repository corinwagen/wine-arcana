# Pinot Meunier

Pinot Meunier is a dark-skinned *Vitis vinifera* variety used above all in
[Champagne](../regions/champagne.md). It is a somatic variant of [Pinot
Noir](pinot-noir.md), not a seedling or cross descended from it. Meunier
typically begins growth later in spring and completes ripening slightly earlier
than Pinot Noir. That short growing cycle helps explain its importance in cool,
frost-prone vineyards, especially in the western Vallée de la Marne.

Its public profile has nevertheless been smaller than its place in the vineyard.
Because Meunier commonly enters blends whose labels do not emphasize each
component, the grape has often been presented merely as a dependable,
early-drinking support for Pinot Noir and [Chardonnay](chardonnay.md). The
Comité Champagne's current overview assigns Meunier 31% of the region's
plantings—the same share as Chardonnay and only moderately less than Pinot
Noir's 38%.[^1] Meunier has thus been a substantial part of Champagne, even
when it was not the most visible part.

## Identity and name

A grapevine propagated by cuttings can preserve a mutation that arose in one
growing point of an older vine. Such a plant is called a bud sport. In Meunier,
the mutation occurs in the outer, epidermal cell layer while inner layers retain
the Pinot genotype, a layered arrangement known as a periclinal chimera. Genetic
work associates the distinctive outer layer with a mutation in *VvGAI*, a gene
involved in the vine's response to the growth hormone gibberellin.[^2] Meunier
is therefore extraordinarily close to Pinot Noir biologically, but its stable,
clonally retained growth traits justify treating it as a distinct cultivated
variety.

The most visible difference is hairiness. Young shoot tips and the undersides of
leaves can look as though they have been dusted with flour. *Meunier* means
“miller” in French, an allusion to that floury appearance rather than to the
wine. Many growers now use the shorter name Meunier, which is also the official
French varietal name. In Germany it is better known as Schwarzriesling or
Müllerrebe. Despite the first of those names, it is not related to
[Riesling](riesling.md).

## Viticulture and the short season

“Later budding and earlier ripening” is a comparison, not a fixed timetable.
The French national reference records budbreak for Meunier three days after
Chasselas, compared with one day after for Pinot Noir; it places both in the
early maturity group, with Meunier about half a week after Chasselas and Pinot
Noir between half a week and one week after.[^3] Site, clone, crop, season, and
the intended wine can narrow or alter that difference. The useful point is that
Meunier often has a slightly shorter period of exposed green growth than Pinot
Noir under comparable conditions.

Later budbreak reduces the chance that tender shoots will be present during an
early spring frost. It does not make the vine frost-proof: a later cold event
can still damage emerged growth. Plantgrape also reports good winter-frost
resistance and relatively good fruitfulness after spring-frost injury. At the
other end of the season, early maturity can help fruit reach a workable balance
before autumn weather deteriorates. Together these traits gave growers a way to
use cooler, frost-exposed sites where an earlier-budding or longer-season vine
carried more risk.

That reliability has costs and limits. Meunier is fairly productive, readily
forms side shoots and suckers, and needs canopy and crop management rather than
being left simply to yield. Its small bunches and berries remain susceptible to
grey rot and grape moths. Earlier harvest may shorten exposure to autumn rain,
but it cannot compensate for compact, damaged fruit or a wet season. Nor does a
historical association with difficult sites mean that Meunier expresses itself
only on them; current varietal and single-vineyard wines also come from
mid-slope and chalk-influenced parcels.

## Its role in Champagne

The current Champagne specification lists Meunier among the appellation's
principal permitted grapes.[^4] In practice, Meunier, Pinot Noir, and Chardonnay
account for almost the entire vineyard. Meunier is especially important toward
the west of the Vallée de la Marne, where continental frost risk combines with
greater oceanic influence and many clay-rich or sandy sites. Its later start and
early finish provide practical insurance, but “insurance grape” is an incomplete
description of material that regularly forms a large share of finished blends.

Like Pinot Noir, Meunier has dark skins and pale pulp. Prompt, controlled
pressing can therefore yield a light base wine; longer skin contact can produce
rosé or red wine. It may be blended across grapes, places, and years before the
[traditional method](../styles/traditional-method-sparkling-wine.md), or used
alone for a white *blanc de noirs*, rosé Champagne, or still Coteaux Champenois.
Single-variety bottlings make Meunier explicit, but they are not a more authentic
use than blending: both show established parts of Champagne practice.

Meunier base wines are often described as relatively supple, fruity, and quick
to become approachable. Those tendencies helped produce the old assumption
that the grape was useful mainly for wines intended to show well young. They do
not set a universal ageing limit. Fruit maturity, yield, site, pressing,
malolactic fermentation, oxygen exposure, reserve-wine use, vessel, and time on
lees all change the result. Specialist accounts of long-aged and
single-vineyard Meunier Champagnes have challenged the simple hierarchy in
which Chardonnay and Pinot Noir provide seriousness while Meunier supplies only
immediate fruit.[^5] The safer conclusion is that Meunier can evolve relatively
quickly, not that every Meunier-based wine must be short-lived.

## Beyond Champagne

Meunier remains much less widespread than Pinot Noir, but Champagne is not its
only setting. Germany provides the clearest still-wine counterexample. Under the
names Schwarzriesling and Müllerrebe, it is concentrated in Württemberg and is
usually made as red wine. The German Wine Institute recorded 1,664 hectares in
2023, including 1,175 hectares in Württemberg.[^6] Smaller plantings elsewhere
are used for both sparkling and still wine. These examples matter less for
volume than for perspective: pale sparkling wine is Meunier's principal role,
not an inherent property of a dark-skinned grape.
