# Pinot Meunier

Pinot Meunier is a dark-skinned *Vitis vinifera* variety used above all in
[Champagne](../regions/champagne.md). It is a somatic variant of [Pinot
Noir](pinot-noir.md), not a seedling or cross descended from it. Meunier
typically begins growth later in spring and completes ripening slightly earlier
than Pinot Noir. That short growing cycle helps explain its importance in cool,
frost-prone vineyards, especially in the western Vallée de la Marne.

The Comité Champagne's current overview assigns Meunier 31% of the region's
plantings—the same share as Chardonnay and only moderately less than Pinot
Noir's 38%.[^1]

## Identity and name

A grapevine propagated by cuttings can preserve a mutation that arose in one
growing point of an older vine. Such a plant is called a bud sport. In Meunier,
the mutation occurs in the outer, epidermal cell layer while inner layers retain
the Pinot genotype, a layered arrangement known as a periclinal chimera. Genetic
work associates the distinctive outer layer with a mutation in *VvGAI*, a gene
involved in the vine's response to the growth hormone gibberellin.[^2] Meunier
is therefore extraordinarily close to Pinot Noir biologically, but its stable,
clonally retained growth traits justify treating it as a distinct cultivated
variety.

The most visible difference is hairiness. Young shoot tips and the undersides of
leaves can look as though they have been dusted with flour. *Meunier* means
“miller” in French, an allusion to that floury appearance. Many growers now use
the shorter name Meunier, which is also the official French varietal name. In
Germany it is better known as Schwarzriesling or Müllerrebe. Despite the first
of those names, it is not related to [Riesling](riesling.md).

## Viticulture and the short season

The French national reference records budbreak for Meunier three days after
Chasselas, compared with one day after for Pinot Noir; it places both in the
early maturity group, with Meunier about half a week after Chasselas and Pinot
Noir between half a week and one week after.[^3] Site, clone, crop, season, and
the intended wine can narrow or alter that difference.

Later budbreak reduces the chance that tender shoots will be present during an
early spring frost. A later cold event can still damage emerged growth.
Plantgrape also reports good winter-frost resistance and relatively good
fruitfulness after spring-frost injury. At the other end of the season, early
maturity can help fruit reach a workable balance before autumn weather
deteriorates. Together these traits gave growers a way to use cooler,
frost-exposed sites where an earlier-budding or longer-season vine carried more
risk.

Meunier is fairly productive, readily forms side shoots and suckers, and needs
canopy and crop management. Its small bunches and berries remain susceptible to
grey rot and grape moths. Current varietal and single-vineyard wines also come
from mid-slope and chalk-influenced parcels.

## Its role in Champagne

The current Champagne specification lists Meunier among the appellation's
principal permitted grapes.[^4] In practice, Meunier, Pinot Noir, and Chardonnay
account for almost the entire vineyard. Meunier is especially important toward
the west of the Vallée de la Marne, where continental frost risk combines with
greater oceanic influence and many clay-rich or sandy sites.

Like Pinot Noir, Meunier has dark skins and pale pulp. Prompt, controlled
pressing can therefore yield a light base wine; longer skin contact can produce
[rosé](../styles/rose.md) or red wine. It may be blended across grapes, places,
and years before the
[traditional method](../styles/traditional-method-sparkling-wine.md), or used
alone for a white *blanc de noirs*, rosé Champagne, or still Coteaux Champenois.

Meunier base wines are often described as relatively supple, fruity, and quick
to become approachable. Fruit maturity, yield, site, pressing, [malolactic
fermentation](../concepts/malolactic-fermentation.md), oxygen exposure,
reserve-wine use, vessel, and time on lees all change the result. Specialist
accounts also document long-aged and single-vineyard Meunier Champagnes.[^5]

## Beyond Champagne

Meunier remains much less widespread than Pinot Noir. Germany is an important
centre for still wines. Under the names Schwarzriesling and Müllerrebe, it is
concentrated in Württemberg and is usually made as red wine. The German Wine
Institute recorded 1,664 hectares in 2023, including 1,175 hectares in
Württemberg.[^6] Smaller plantings elsewhere are used for both sparkling and
still wine.

England and Wales also use Meunier alongside Chardonnay and Pinot Noir for
traditional-method sparkling wine. The variety's later budbreak and early
maturity offer useful options in a cool climate, while the site and season
still determine frost exposure and fruit health. Here its contemporary role
is chiefly as a sparkling-wine grape, contrasting with the still-red tradition
of Württemberg.[^7]
