# Treixadura

Treixadura is a pale-skinned *Vitis vinifera* variety of Atlantic northwest
Iberia. The Galician name is Treixadura; in Portugal the same grape is called
Trajadura. Its principal associations are Ribeiro in Galicia and Vinho Verde
in northern Portugal, where it is commonly part of multi-grape white blends
rather than the sole variety.

The grape is useful in those blends because it can contribute aroma, relatively
full body, and a soft texture. It commonly gives less of an acid edge than
[Albariño](albarino.md), [Loureiro](loureiro.md), or [Arinto](arinto.md),
especially when fully ripe. That does not make every Treixadura wine low in
acidity: site, season, crop, and harvest date determine whether its body feels
balanced or heavy, and its partners are often chosen precisely to adjust that
balance.

## History and identity

Treixadura is long established on both sides of the modern Spanish-Portuguese
border, but its exact birthplace has not been demonstrated. A Portuguese
varietal reference records the grape by the Minho River in 1822 and the related
name *Treijaduro* in Spain in 1843; Galicia's viticultural research station
describes Treixadura as traditional to Ribeiro and cultivated along the Miño
valley.[^1] These records support a regional history, not a proven national
origin.

Trajadura is therefore a regional name, not a separate Portuguese variety.
The current specifications for both Ribeiro and Vinho Verde treat Treixadura
and Trajadura consistently with that shared identity. Other older names exist,
but some resemble names used for unrelated grapes, so Trajadura is the useful
alias for discovery rather than a reason to reproduce every historical form.

## Viticulture and harvest balance

Ripening descriptions vary with place and comparison variety. Galician records
class the vine as late by a general reference scale but place it after Albariño
and [Godello](godello.md) and before Loureiro under local conditions. Portuguese
records describe a middle-season maturity, while the Vinho Verde specification
calls Trajadura early relative to Arinto and Loureiro in several maritime
subregions. It is most useful to treat it as mid- to later-ripening across its
range: it needs sufficient warmth and exposure, but the practical harvest date
changes with region.

The vine can be vigorous and productive. Fertile ground and excessive vigour
can enlarge and crowd its bunches, slowing even ripening and reducing airflow.
The compact fruit is vulnerable to bunch rot in wet conditions, and heavy rain
near harvest can split berries. Canopy and crop management therefore serve two
purposes: allowing enough light for maturity while keeping the fruit zone open
and healthy.

Site choice is especially important near the cooler limits of cultivation.
Warm, sheltered slopes can help the variety complete ripening, whereas a cool
or high site may leave sugar and aroma development behind. Yet simply waiting
longer is not cost-free. As the grapes mature, sugar rises and acidity tends to
soften; in warm conditions a late harvest can produce ample body but a loose
finish. Earlier picking preserves more acidity but may sacrifice the breadth
and aromatic maturity that Treixadura brings to a blend.

## Wine character and blending

Treixadura commonly produces a broader, rounder wine than the sharp, very
light style sometimes associated with Atlantic whites. Young wines can suggest
flowers and orchard fruit, but the more reliable distinction is structural:
moderate acidity, moderate to ample alcohol and extract, and enough aroma to
remain recognizable beside other varieties. Riper fruit generally pushes the
wine toward greater body and softer balance; a cooler season or earlier harvest
can give a firmer, less expansive result.

This combination explains the grape's place in aromatic white blends. In
Ribeiro it may be joined by Albariño, Godello, Loureiro, Torrontés, Lado, or
other local varieties. Treixadura can provide volume and a central register of
fruit and flowers, while a higher-acid partner can tighten the finish or a more
distinctly perfumed grape can lift the aroma. The roles are tendencies, not a
fixed recipe, and proportions vary by site, vintage, and intended style.

In Vinho Verde, Trajadura is often blended with Loureiro, Alvarinho, and
Arinto. The current denomination specification explicitly describes blends in
the Ave, Cávado, and Sousa subregions as combining middle-ripening Arinto and
Loureiro with earlier Trajadura, alongside varieties that make different
ripening demands.[^2] Here Trajadura's softer, earlier-ripening contribution can
round an acid-driven blend without requiring the other grapes to remain on the
vine until the same date.

Treixadura can also be bottled alone. Varietal examples make its body and
moderate acidity easier to see, but they place more pressure on harvest balance.
Cool fermentation can preserve direct fruit and floral aromas; brief skin
contact may increase aroma and phenolic texture; contact with fine
[lees](../concepts/lees-aging.md) can add further breadth. These methods can
support a substantial wine, while excessive extraction or conspicuous
[oak](../concepts/oak-maturation.md) can obscure the grape's relatively gentle
aromatic detail.

## Ribeiro and Vinho Verde

Ribeiro is Treixadura's strongest Galician association. The denomination lies
in an inland transition zone where river valleys admit Atlantic influence but
warmer, drier conditions than the coast can help the grape mature. Vineyards
vary in elevation, slope, exposure, and water availability, so a sheltered
slope and a cooler valley floor do not produce the same balance.

The current Ribeiro specification identifies Treixadura as a principal white
variety and singles it out among the local grapes most characteristic of the
denomination. It does not require Treixadura in every white Ribeiro. Wines
labelled *Ribeiro castes*, however, must be made entirely from the listed
principal varieties, a group that also includes Albariño, Godello, Loureira,
Lado, and Torrontés.[^3] This legal framework fits the regional tradition of
multi-variety whites without prescribing one blend.

Across the border, Trajadura belongs to the larger and wetter Vinho Verde
denomination. Current rules authorize it for Vinho Verde generally and for
eight of the nine named subregions; Baião is the exception.[^4] Its function
changes within that broad area. In mild, maritime sectors it can reach useful
ripeness earlier than some blending partners, while in warmer or more sheltered
sites it can add still more weight. Vinho Verde is therefore not a single light,
sprightly style, and the presence of Trajadura does not by itself determine a
wine's alcohol, acidity, or degree of carbonation.

Ribeiro and Vinho Verde show the same grape solving related problems in
different settings. In Ribeiro, Treixadura often forms the broad aromatic core
of a local blend and can also stand alone. In Vinho Verde, Trajadura is commonly
a complementary source of ripeness and softness beside more acid-driven or
perfumed grapes. In both places, its value lies less in dominating the blend
than in connecting aroma, body, and acidity.
