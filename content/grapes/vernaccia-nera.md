# Vernaccia Nera

Vernaccia Nera is a dark-skinned grape closely associated with Serrapetrona,
in the inland hills of Marche. It is particularly interesting because local
producers use it for both still reds and red sparkling wines, sometimes
bringing dried grapes into the process. The grape name alone therefore gives
only part of the information needed to imagine the wine.

## The sparkling tradition

Vernaccia di Serrapetrona DOCG is the local sparkling expression. The account
published by the municipality and regional wine institute for Appassimenti
Aperti describes a sequence of three fermentations: fresh grapes first make
a base wine, dried grapes are subsequently brought into production, and a
further fermentation creates the bubbles.[^1]

Drying, or [appassimento](../concepts/appassimento.md), removes water from the
berries before they enter the wine. That concentrates their constituents and
changes the raw material available to the winemaker. Fermentation then
determines how much sugar remains. A dried-grape contribution consequently
does not by itself tell the drinker whether the result will be sweet.

The current Terre di Serrapetrona range makes that distinction tangible:
it includes both dry and sweet Vernaccia di Serrapetrona. Comparing the two
is a way to separate the effects of bubbles and concentration from the effect
of [residual sugar](../concepts/residual-sugar.md).

## Still wines and contemporary range

Serrapetrona DOC provides a separate still-red context for the grape. Terre
di Serrapetrona describes its still wines as entirely Vernaccia Nera, with
some receiving extended maturation in large wooden vessels. Its Sommo uses
French-oak and cherry-wood barriques, while a sparkling rosé called Blink
adds another expression to the estate's range.

These examples matter because the best-known local sparkling tradition can
otherwise obscure the grape's ability to make a still red. Time in wood
changes the wine through maturation and, depending on the vessel, contributes
flavour. Sparkling production adds a different structural element: dissolved
carbon dioxide. Neither choice follows automatically from the variety's name.

For a reader approaching a bottle, the useful order is to identify the full
appellation, then whether it is still or sparkling and dry or sweet. Those
distinctions explain much more than treating every Vernaccia Nera wine as a
single traditional specialty.

[^1]: Appassimenti Aperti, “Il vino,” the local account of Vernaccia di
    Serrapetrona and its fermentation sequence. This describes the tradition;
    individual producers' proportions and cellar schedules vary.
