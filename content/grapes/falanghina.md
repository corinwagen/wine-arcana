# Falanghina

Falanghina is the shared name of two distinct white wine grapes associated
with Campania: Falanghina Beneventana and Falanghina Flegrea. Both contribute
to the region's fresh, fruit-scented whites, and the unqualified name remains
common on labels.

Their main centres of cultivation differ. Beneventana is associated with
Benevento and the inland Sannio hills; Flegrea with the area around Naples,
including the Campi Flegrei. Geography is therefore a useful first clue to the
plant material behind a wine.

## Two grapes under one name

A 2005 genetic survey of Campanian vines distinguished Falanghina Beneventana
from Falanghina Campi Flegrei. The same study identified Falanghina Pigna
Piccola with the Campi Flegrei material. These findings explain why older
accounts that treated all Falanghina as one variable grape can be confusing.[^1]

The distinction is visible as well as genetic. The technical account from
Vivai Cooperativi Rauscedo describes more elliptical berries and a winged,
conical-pyramidal bunch in Beneventana, compared with rounder berries and a
more cylindrical bunch in Flegrea. It places their ripening in a similar
part of the season. Those differences matter to the identification and
selection of vines, even when their wines share some aromatic features.

## Vineyard conditions

Falanghina is generally vigorous, making the balance between foliage and
fruit an important part of farming. The nursery's profile favours warm, dry
conditions and well-exposed hillsides. It also reports susceptibility to
bunch rot and sour rot, so growers must consider how quickly clusters dry
after rain. [Canopy management](../concepts/canopy-management.md) affects
both ventilation and the amount of sunlight reaching the fruit.

Nursery observations put budbreak early and ripening toward the middle to
later part of the season. Exposure, crop, and weather affect the picking
date. Commercial
technical accounts have also used the general name inconsistently, so
clone-specific observations are most useful when the plant material is
identified.

## Wines and labels

Falanghina commonly makes dry whites with floral and citrus impressions,
while riper examples can suggest tropical fruit and feel fuller. The
Benevento selections described by Rauscedo combine fruit aroma with enough
[acidity](../concepts/acidity.md) for fresh-tasting wines, and some are also suited to dried-grape
production. Harvest maturity and the intended style both matter to the
balance of fruit, alcohol, and acidity.

Falanghina del Sannio gives the inland expression a protected name. The
specification published by Regione Campania requires at least 85%
Falanghina and defines the grape-growing area as the province of Benevento.
Alongside the still wine it provides for sparkling, late-harvest, and passito
forms, with passito made using grapes dried on the vine or after picking.
It also defines the subzones Guardia Sanframondi or Guardiolo, Sant'Agata dei
Goti, Solopaca, and Taburno.[^2]
