# Mavrodaphne

Mavrodaphne is a dark-skinned Greek *Vitis vinifera* grape associated with
the northwestern Peloponnese, especially Patras, and the Ionian island of
Kefalonia. Its name is particularly familiar through sweet fortified wine,
but it also produces dry reds. The difference is fundamental to reading
the wine: ripeness, fermentation, and fortification determine sweetness,
not the grape's name alone.

The variety can give deep colour, ripe or dried-fruit impressions, herbal
notes, and a distinctive bitter edge. In a sweet wine that edge may
counterbalance sugar. In a dry wine, extraction and [tannin](../concepts/tannin.md) become more
conspicuous because sweetness is no longer softening their impression.

## Sweet wine and retained sugar

The Patras tradition is closely associated with Gustav Clauss and the
nineteenth-century development of commercial sweet Mavrodaphne. Kefalonia
has its own smaller sweet-wine tradition. These remain important parts of
the grape's identity alongside the newer attention to dry wine.

For a sweet fortified wine, adding spirit during fermentation raises
alcohol enough to interrupt yeast activity and leave grape sugar
unfermented. This [fortification](../concepts/fortification.md) changes
both strength and sweetness. Subsequent maturation alters the fruit and
texture further. A wine's dried-fruit aroma therefore needs to be read
alongside its harvest and ageing history.

## Dry reds on Kefalonia

Dry Mavrodaphne allows more of the grape's sugar to ferment into alcohol
without preserving the sweetness of the familiar dessert style. Wines of
Greece describes increasing interest in this use in both the Peloponnese
and Kefalonia.

Gentilini's Eclipse provides a specific island example. The producer uses
estate Mavrodaphne, with fermentation and post-fermentation
[maceration](../concepts/maceration.md) in open vats and barrels, followed
by a year in French and American oak. The wine is presented as a dry red,
with fruit and herbal aromas alongside the effects of
[oak maturation](../concepts/oak-maturation.md).

This contrast makes Mavrodaphne useful for understanding how a familiar
grape can acquire a different regional role through cellar choices.
Comparing a dry red with a sweet fortified version requires attention to
sugar and alcohol as well as fruit character.
