# Merlot

Merlot is a dark-skinned *Vitis vinifera* variety historically associated with
Bordeaux and now planted widely. It usually ripens sooner than [Cabernet
Sauvignon](cabernet-sauvignon.md), often lends a more supple texture to a blend,
and can make complete, structured wines on its own.

Those tendencies are shaped by water supply, crop level, weather, and harvest
date. Merlot begins its growing season fairly early. Fermentation and maturation
further shape whether the wine is fruit-led and made for early drinking or more
tannic and suited to bottle development.

## History and identity

DNA parentage analysis identified [Cabernet Franc](cabernet-franc.md) and
Magdeleine Noire des Charentes as Merlot's parents. A later phased assembly of
the Merlot genome used both parental genomes to separate the inherited
lineages.[^1] This establishes the relationship, but not the date or exact place
of the original seedling. Merlot is documented in French literature only from
the late eighteenth century. Its name is often connected to *merle*, the French
word for blackbird, but proposed explanations variously refer to the berries'
colour and to birds eating the early-ripening fruit; the etymology remains
unsettled.

The variety was established in Bordeaux by the nineteenth century and expanded
greatly there and abroad during the twentieth, producing many regional styles.

## Viticulture and ripening

Merlot normally reaches harvest before Cabernet Sauvignon in the same region.
French catalogue benchmarks place both in the broad mid-season ripening group,
with Merlot ahead; multi-year Bordeaux observations likewise found a shorter
ripening period and earlier harvest for Merlot.[^2] Its earlier harvest can help
it reach adequate maturity before autumn rain becomes pressing and gives
growers separate harvest windows when both grapes are planted.

Merlot also breaks bud earlier than Cabernet Sauvignon, exposing young growth to
more spring-frost risk. Weather around flowering can cause *coulure*, in which
flowers fail to become berries, making yields irregular. The vine is fertile and
moderately to strongly vigorous, so pruning, [canopy
management](../concepts/canopy-management.md), and crop level matter: excess
growth can shade clusters, while too much fruit can delay ripening.

Water supply is another important limit. Merlot is poorly suited to severe
drought, while persistent dampness can encourage downy mildew on flowers and
clusters and, to a lesser degree, grey rot. Clay-rich soils are often associated
with it in Bordeaux because they can retain useful water. Drainage still matters:
Pomerol's official specification favours sites with regulated water supply and
excludes waterlogged ground.

These sensitivities make simple soil rules unreliable. In a Bordeaux study of
Merlot and the two Cabernets across gravel, clay, and sand, year and soil both
altered vine growth and berry composition, with many effects mediated by vine
water status.[^3] A warm, dry season may advance sugar accumulation and reduce
acidity but can also impose damaging water stress; a cooler or wetter season may
slow development and increase disease pressure. Rootstock, rooting depth,
canopy, yield, and harvest decisions all modify the result.

## Wine character and cellar choices

Ripe Merlot commonly gives deeply coloured wine with moderate acidity and a
rounded, supple impression. Dark-fruit character is frequent, while cooler
conditions or an earlier harvest can preserve herbal notes and firmer acidity.
In hotter conditions or with a later harvest, the wine may become broader and
higher in alcohol as acidity falls.

Merlot skins and seeds contain ample phenolic material, and extraction determines
how much reaches the wine. In controlled Merlot trials, longer skin contact
increased seed-derived tannin and astringency; fruit maturity also changed
colour, aroma, and mouthfeel.[^4] Gentle extraction and earlier pressing can
emphasize roundness, while longer or more forceful extraction can build a firmer
frame. [Oak maturation](../concepts/oak-maturation.md) and bottle age can
further alter tannin perception.

## Bordeaux and varietal wines

In Bordeaux, Merlot is both a blending grape and the principal component of
many wines. It is especially prominent on the Right Bank: the Pomerol and
Saint-Émilion specifications describe it as very largely dominant.[^5] Cabernet
Franc and Cabernet Sauvignon can add a different tannic and aromatic shape. On
the Left Bank, the balance is often reversed, with Merlot contributing
earlier-ripening fruit and a rounder texture to Cabernet Sauvignon-led blends.
Estate, site, and vintage matter more than a fixed “Bordeaux blend” recipe.

Outside Bordeaux, Merlot is commonly bottled under its grape name, including in
parts of the United States and northern Italy. Rules vary by country; in the
United States, a single-variety label generally requires at least 75 percent of
the named grape and an appellation of origin.[^6] The remaining portion may
serve the same balancing role that blending does in Bordeaux.

Varietal Merlot ranges from wines made for early drinking to examples with
substantial structure and capacity to age. Restrained yields, a site with
adequate water and good drainage, appropriate harvest timing, and measured
extraction can support the latter. High yields, severe drought, delayed harvest
in a hot site, or aggressive extraction can each upset the balance differently.
