# Merlot

Merlot is a dark-skinned *Vitis vinifera* variety historically associated with
Bordeaux and now planted widely. It is often introduced as the softer partner
to [Cabernet Sauvignon](cabernet-sauvignon.md), but that comparison is most
useful when it is made specific. Merlot usually ripens sooner, often contributes
a more supple texture to a blend, and can also make complete, structured wines
on its own.

None of those tendencies is automatic. Merlot begins its growing season fairly
early, and its fruit responds markedly to water supply, crop level, weather, and
harvest date. Fermentation and maturation can then turn the same variety toward
either an accessible, fruit-led wine or a more tannic wine intended to develop
in bottle.

## History and identity

DNA parentage analysis identified [Cabernet Franc](cabernet-franc.md) and
Magdeleine Noire des Charentes as Merlot's parents; a later phased assembly of
the Merlot genome used
the genomes of both parents to separate the two inherited lineages.[^1] The
evidence establishes the relationship, but not the date or exact place of the
original seedling. Merlot is documented in French literature only from the late
eighteenth century. Its name is often connected to *merle*, the French word for
blackbird, although proposed explanations refer variously to the berries' colour
and to birds eating the early-ripening fruit, so the etymology is not settled.

The variety was established in Bordeaux by the nineteenth century and expanded
greatly there and abroad during the twentieth. That spread produced many
regional styles rather than one international Merlot formula.

## Viticulture and ripening

“Early” is relative: Merlot is not among the first wine grapes to mature, but it
normally reaches harvest before [Cabernet Sauvignon](cabernet-sauvignon.md) in
the same region. French catalogue benchmarks place the two in the same broad
mid-season ripening group,
with Merlot ahead; multi-year Bordeaux observations likewise found a shorter
ripening period and earlier harvest for Merlot.[^2] This can help it reach
adequate maturity in Bordeaux before autumn rain becomes pressing and gives a
grower two harvest windows when both grapes are planted.

The advantage at the end of the season has a cost at the beginning. Merlot
breaks bud earlier than Cabernet Sauvignon, exposing young growth to more spring
frost risk. Weather around flowering can cause *coulure*, in which flowers fail
to become berries, making yields irregular. The vine is fertile and moderately
to strongly vigorous, so pruning, canopy management, and crop level matter:
excess growth can shade clusters, while too much fruit can delay or dilute
ripening.

Water supply is another important limit. Merlot is poorly suited to severe
drought, yet persistent dampness can encourage downy mildew on flowers and
clusters and, to a lesser degree, grey rot. Cool clay-rich soils are often
associated with it in Bordeaux because they can retain useful water, not because
clay supplies a particular flavour. Drainage still matters. Pomerol's official
specification, for example, favours sites with regulated water supply but
excludes waterlogged ground.

These sensitivities make simple soil rules unreliable. In a Bordeaux study of
Merlot and the two Cabernets across gravel, clay, and sand, year and soil both
altered vine growth and berry composition, with many effects mediated by vine
water status.[^3] A warm, dry season may advance sugar accumulation and reduce
acidity but can also impose damaging water stress; a cooler or wetter season may
slow development and increase disease pressure. Rootstock, rooting depth,
canopy, yield, and harvest decisions all modify the result.

## Wine character and cellar choices

Ripe Merlot commonly gives deeply coloured wine with moderate acidity and a
rounded, supple impression. Dark-fruit character is frequent, while cooler
conditions or an earlier harvest can preserve more herbal notes and firmer
acidity. In hotter conditions or with a later harvest, the wine may become
broader and higher in alcohol as acidity falls. These are directions of change,
not a fixed set of flavours.

Supple does not mean tannin-free. Merlot skins and seeds contain ample phenolic
material, and extraction determines how much reaches the wine. In controlled
Merlot trials, extending skin contact increased seed-derived tannin and shifted
the wines toward greater astringency; fruit maturity also changed colour, aroma,
and mouthfeel.[^4] Gentle extraction and earlier pressing can emphasize
roundness, while longer or more forceful extraction can build a firmer frame.
Oak maturation and bottle age can further alter the perception of tannin, but
neither defines the variety.

## Bordeaux and varietal wines

In Bordeaux, Merlot is both a blending grape and the principal component of
many wines. It is especially prominent on the Right Bank: the current Pomerol
specification calls it overwhelmingly dominant, while the Saint-Émilion
specification describes it as the large majority variety among several
permitted principal grapes.[^5] Cabernet Franc and Cabernet Sauvignon can add a
different tannic and aromatic shape. On the Left Bank, the balance is often
reversed, with Merlot contributing earlier-ripening fruit and a rounder texture
to Cabernet Sauvignon-led blends. Estate, site, and vintage matter more than a
fixed “Bordeaux blend” recipe.

Outside Bordeaux, Merlot is commonly bottled under its grape name, including in
parts of the United States and northern Italy. A varietal label does not
necessarily mean a wine is pure Merlot: rules vary by country, and United States
regulations generally require at least 75 percent of the named grape along with
an appellation of origin.[^6] The remaining portion may therefore serve the same
balancing role that blending does in Bordeaux.

Varietal Merlot can be made for early drinking, but the category is much wider
than that. Restrained yields, a site with adequate water but good drainage,
appropriate harvest timing, and measured extraction can produce wines with
substantial structure and capacity to age. Conversely, high yields, severe
drought, delayed harvest in a hot site, or aggressive extraction can each upset
the balance in a different way. The grape's reputation for ease in the glass
should not be confused with indifference in the vineyard or cellar.
