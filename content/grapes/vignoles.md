# Vignoles

Vignoles is a French-bred white [hybrid grape](../concepts/hybrid-grapes.md)
with an established role in the American Midwest. Missouri's wine
organization describes a range extending from dry whites through sweet
and late-harvest wines. Its appeal to winemakers therefore involves both
fruit character and flexibility, rather than one prescribed sweetness level.

The vineyard presents a more difficult side of that versatility: compact
bunches are vulnerable to rot. Decisions about hanging fruit longer must
account for fruit condition as well as the style the winemaker hopes to make.

## Identity and a revised pedigree

Also known as Ravat 51, Vignoles was bred by J. F. Ravat. Its familiar
historical pedigree, Seibel 6905 crossed with Pinot Noir, should not be
repeated as settled fact. Foundation Plant Services reports that genetic
work challenged that account and records Plantet × Seibel 880 instead.
The catalogue attributes the replacement parentage to DNA results
communicated privately by Montpellier researchers.[^identity]

## Bunch structure and disease

USDA Agricultural Research Service reporting identifies tight clusters as
a particular problem: crowded berries favour Botrytis bunch rot and sour
rot, while making spray coverage within a bunch more difficult. This is a
physical feature with consequences beyond a general disease-resistance
rating.

ARS and Cornell researchers have evaluated looser-clustered selections,
including one called Vignoles 2. The work seeks to reduce rot through a
change in bunch architecture within the variety. Trial improvements should
not be read as freedom from vineyard disease.[^selection]

## Missouri and wine style

Missouri's regional account places Vignoles among its important white
grapes and describes pineapple, peach and apricot among the wine's possible
fruit associations. It also records its use in blends. A dry version and
a sweet one can consequently offer quite different introductions to the
same variety.

[Residual sugar](../concepts/residual-sugar.md) changes the balance of a wine,
but *late harvest* describes a vineyard decision rather than a guaranteed
taste. Leaving grapes longer exposes the crop to additional weather and
rot risk. With Vignoles, the condition of those compact bunches is part of
the explanation for what ultimately reaches the cellar.
