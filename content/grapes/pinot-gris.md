# Pinot Gris

Pinot Gris is a gray-skinned *Vitis vinifera* variety cultivated mainly for
white wine. Pinot Grigio is its Italian name, not a separate grape;
Grauburgunder and Ruländer are German names for the same variety. What makes it
unusual among familiar white-wine grapes is the skin: ripe berries can be
gray-pink, brownish pink, or blue-gray rather than green or yellow.

The two best-known names nevertheless suggest a useful, if imperfect,
stylistic divide. Alsace Pinot Gris is commonly made from riper fruit as a
broader, more concentrated wine and ranges from dry to sweet. Much northern
Italian Pinot Grigio is picked earlier and made as a lighter, dry wine that
emphasizes freshness. These are production traditions rather than properties
fixed by the name: substantial Italian Pinot Grigio and dry, restrained
Alsace Pinot Gris both exist.

## A gray member of the Pinot family

Pinot Gris is closely related to [Pinot Noir](pinot-noir.md), but calling it a
relative can obscure the nature of the connection. Vines are propagated by
cuttings or grafting, so a mutation in a shoot can be preserved as a new
cultivated form. Layer-specific genetic analysis found that Pinot Gris arose
from ancestral Pinot Noir through a somatic mutation affecting berry colour.
The mutation is present in one cell layer, making the vine a chimera: adjacent
layers of its growing tissue have different genetic constitutions. The same
research indicates that Pinot Blanc arose independently from Pinot Noir, not
through a simple sequence from Noir to Gris to Blanc.[^1]

This evidence explains identity, not a precise place or date of origin. It
also explains why the varieties remain so similar away from berry colour.
France's official catalogue describes Pinot Gris as matching Pinot Noir in its
main identifying features except for ripe skin colour, and notes that white
berries can sometimes appear on a Pinot Gris vine.

“Gray” is an ampelographic colour category, not a claim that every berry looks
neutral gray. The skins retain pigment, but much less uniformly than those of
Pinot Noir. The pulp and juice are pale, so prompt pressing with little skin
contact can produce a pale white wine. Longer contact between juice and skins
can draw out enough colour and phenolic material for a golden, pink, or copper
wine. The Italian term *ramato*, meaning copper-coloured, is used for one such
tradition; the Delle Venezie specification recognizes white, pink, and copper
forms of Pinot Grigio. Colour here is therefore partly a property of the fruit
and partly a cellar decision.

## Ripening and vineyard choices

Pinot Gris buds and ripens early. Its bunches and berries are usually small,
and the compact clusters can be vulnerable to grey rot in wet conditions.
Official French observations describe a strong capacity to accumulate sugar
while retaining low to moderate acidity, although site, crop, weather, and
harvest date can shift that balance. The early season helps it ripen in cool
and moderate regions, but it also makes the picking decision consequential.

Earlier harvesting generally preserves more acidity and limits potential
alcohol, while later harvesting can bring more sugar, weight, and flavour
development. Crop level matters alongside date: a larger crop distributes the
vine's resources among more fruit, while lower yields can permit greater
concentration. Neither choice alone determines quality. Early-picked grapes
can suit a deliberate light style, and ripe grapes still need enough acidity
and sound fruit to avoid a heavy or dull wine.

## Pinot Gris and Pinot Grigio styles

Alsace provides the reference point for the richer Pinot Gris model. Fruit is
often harvested later and at lower yields than for the widely produced light
Pinot Grigio model, giving more potential alcohol and concentration.[^2]
Fermentation may carry the wine to dryness or leave residual sugar, so the
region's Pinot Gris spans dry, off-dry, and sweet forms. Riper dry wines can
feel broad without tasting sweet; later-picked wines may add greater density
and, depending on the season and fruit condition, honeyed or smoky
impressions. Pinot Gris is also one of the varieties eligible for Alsace's
*Vendanges Tardives* late-harvest wines, in which overripe grapes and sometimes
noble rot produce a distinctly sweeter, more concentrated style.

The familiar Pinot Grigio model takes a different route. In northern Italy,
especially in large-volume categories, earlier picking is commonly used to
retain acidity, while higher yields help produce a less concentrated wine.
Fermentation in inert vessels and limited skin contact keep the result pale and
direct. The Delle Venezie protected designation describes its still Pinot
Grigio as fresh and harmonious, from dry to medium-dry; it also permits
sparkling, semi-sparkling, pink, and *ramato* versions. The regulations
therefore allow more variation than the shorthand “light dry white” implies.

Nor does Italy stop at that shorthand. Friuli-Venezia Giulia and Alto Adige
produce fuller examples, while skin contact gives some wines conspicuous
texture and copper colour. Producers in Oregon, New Zealand, and elsewhere may
choose *Gris* or *Grigio* partly to signal an intended style, but the word on
the label cannot by itself reveal harvest date, sweetness, skin contact, or
weight. The distinction is most useful as a guide to two influential
traditions, not as a rule about the grape.
