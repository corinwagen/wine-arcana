# Verdejo

Verdejo is a white *Vitis vinifera* variety most closely associated with Rueda,
on Spain's high Castilian plateau. Its wines can be vividly aromatic, with a
green-herbal or fennel-like lift and fruit that ranges from citrus to orchard
fruit. A dry, pleasantly bitter finish is often as useful a clue to Verdejo as
the aroma. Ripeness, [skin contact](../styles/skin-contact-white-wine.md),
fermentation, and oxygen management create substantial variation within that
profile.

Rueda gives the grape its clearest historical frame. For much of the region's
past, its whites included deliberately oxidative wines. Since the late
twentieth century, however, rapid, protected handling has made pale, youthful
Verdejo the region's most familiar expression. The contrast explains both the
grape's reputation for freshness and why apparently similar bottles can taste
quite different.

## Rueda and its changing wines

The Consejo Regulador dates Verdejo's presence in the Rueda area to the
repopulation of the Duero basin under Alfonso VI, and connects its arrival with
Mozarab settlers. That regional historical account documents a local tradition,
while the variety's precise origin remains uncertain. The Tierra de Medina and
Rueda area has a long history of wine production, including golden, oxidative
styles.

The current Rueda product specification still recognizes *Dorado* among the
appellation's wine types. It describes a historical practice of ageing wines in
underground cellars and large vessels to develop the colour and aromas of
oxidation. Dorado therefore uses oxidation as an intentional part of its style.

A different course began in 1970, when Marqués de Riscal arrived in the area
with the œnologist Émile Peynaud to explore a high-quality dry white based on
Verdejo. The Rueda regulatory council records this as a key moment in the
region's revival; the denomination was formally recognized in 1980.[^1] The
change did not replace every older method, but it focused attention on a wine
made to retain primary aroma and a pale colour.

## A vine for a continental plateau

Rueda's vineyards occupy a broad, elevated plain, where cold winters and a
short growing season sit beside hot, dry summers and marked day–night
temperature shifts. The official specification places the production area at
roughly 700 to 870 metres above sea level and describes gravelly, stony soils
across much of it.[^2] Seasons and sites vary across that range; water
availability, crop level, and harvest date all affect the balance between
acidity, alcohol, and aroma.

Outside Rueda, Verdejo is grown elsewhere in Spain and has travelled farther
afield. Rueda likewise permits several white varieties, including [Sauvignon
Blanc](sauvignon-blanc.md), and several wine styles. The grape and the place are
closely connected but remain distinct terms.

## Aroma, bitterness, and oxygen

Young Verdejo often combines floral and fruit notes with a distinctly herbal
edge. Some of that aroma comes from volatile thiols, sulfur-containing
compounds released during fermentation from non-volatile grape precursors. A
recent experimental study describes these thiols as major contributors to the
variety's sensory profile, especially its box-tree-like herbal nuance.[^3]
Yeast choice and fermentation conditions therefore help decide how conspicuous
that character becomes; it is not a simple measure of vineyard quality.

The finish can carry a mild bitterness, sometimes described as almond-like or
phenolic. It commonly supplies a structural counterpoint to fruit. Its intensity
depends in part on extraction: pressing and any time the juice spends with skins
change what enters the must. In one Rueda experiment, a steel-made,
skin-macerated Verdejo was judged more bitter than versions fermented and aged
with lees in oak; the authors suggest that yeast-derived mannoproteins also
softened or masked bitterness. That finding describes the trial wines, rather
than setting a rule for the variety.[^4]

Verdejo's aromatic appeal is closely tied to careful protection from oxygen.
Oxidation can deepen colour and move the aroma away from fresh fruit and herbs;
the risk is especially important from crushing through bottling. In the same
Rueda study, grapes were handled cool, under nitrogen during maceration, and
with sulfur dioxide to limit must oxidation. Those are examples of a protective
approach. Time in bottle also matters: the study found that several fruity ester
compounds and the panel's tropical-fruit impression declined over seven months.

## In the cellar

For the modern, fruit-led style, producers may harvest into the cooler part of
the day, move fruit quickly, exclude air where practical, clarify the must, and
ferment in inert vessels at controlled temperatures. These choices aim to carry
delicate precursors and fermentation aromas into bottle before oxygen and time
reshape them, and suit wines intended for early drinking.

That is only one interpretation. Fermentation or maturation on lees can broaden
texture and moderate the impression of bitterness; [lees
ageing](../concepts/lees-aging.md) is not automatically oxidative. Oak has a
different effect again, adding its own flavour and allowing controlled oxygen
exposure, as explained under [oak maturation](../concepts/oak-maturation.md).
More deliberately oxidative Rueda, including Dorado, moves in the opposite
direction from a tightly protected young wine. Verdejo consequently offers a
useful lesson: grape character is real, but it emerges through a chain of
decisions in vineyard, press, cellar, and bottle.
