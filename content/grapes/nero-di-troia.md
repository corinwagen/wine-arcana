# Nero di Troia

Nero di Troia, also called Uva di Troia, is a dark-skinned grape associated
with northern and central Puglia, including Castel del Monte. It gives the
region a red-wine identity distinct from the [Negroamaro](negroamaro.md)
tradition farther south in Salento. Its wines can combine floral character
with substantial colour and tannin.

## Ripeness, exposure and plant material

Rauscedo describes a vigorous vine with a drooping growth habit, thick skins
and mid-to-late ripening. Its warning about sunburn is particularly relevant
to canopy decisions in hot weather: exposing more fruit is not invariably
beneficial. [Canopy management](../concepts/canopy-management.md) has to balance
ventilation and ripening with protection from excessive heat and sunlight.

The nursery's clonal descriptions also qualify generalizations about acidity.
Although its overview describes relatively low acidity, particular selections
retain more. The identity of the planting material and the conditions of the
season therefore matter when comparing wines.

## Two routes at Castel del Monte

Rivera's Violante demonstrates a style intended to be approachable relatively
young. Its 2021 technical sheet describes October picking, fermentation in
steel with controlled extraction, and a year in lined concrete. The producer
explicitly aims to retain fruit and violet-like aromas while avoiding
excessive tannin extraction. This is a substantial red made without an oak
maturation requirement in its published method.

Puer Apuliae follows a different route. Rivera identifies its source as the
Tafuri vineyard and emphasizes a small-berried form of Nero di Troia. The
wine matures in new barriques. Both the selected fruit and that wood treatment
contribute to a more ambitious maturation style; comparing the wines does not
isolate the effect of barrels alone.

This range explains Nero di Troia's contemporary appeal as a varietal wine.
Its [tannin](../concepts/tannin.md) gives the producer material to work with,
but the amount extracted and the way it develops depend on cellar decisions.
A grape capable of firm reds can also support a fruit-led style when that is
the production goal.

For readers exploring Puglia, Castel del Monte is thus a useful geographical
anchor. The combination of local plant selection and contrasting maturation
choices gives more insight than treating every southern Italian red as
equally ripe, heavy or oak-driven.
