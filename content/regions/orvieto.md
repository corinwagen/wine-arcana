# Orvieto

Orvieto is a white-wine appellation around the city of that name in southwestern
[Umbria](umbria.md), extending into neighbouring Lazio. Its wines range from
dry whites to sweet late-harvest and noble-rot expressions. The common thread
is a delimited growing area and a blend of white grapes, rather than one
variety or one level of sweetness.

The surrounding district also produces wines with other designations. That
broader context matters when comparing a traditional Orvieto blend with a
[Chardonnay](../grapes/chardonnay.md)-led wine from a nearby estate.

## Geography and the blend

The appellation crosses the provinces of Terni and Viterbo. Volcanic deposits
form part of its geology, alongside marine clays and sands and other
sedimentary material. Soils and exposures vary among vineyards, affecting
water availability and ripening. The dramatic volcanic rock beneath the city
is not a complete description of every vineyard in the denomination.

Under the specification consolidated in June 2025, Trebbiano Toscano, locally
called Procanico, and Grechetto together must make up at least 60% of the
blend. Other permitted white varieties can supply the balance. *Classico*
identifies the delimited historic subzone; the denomination also provides for
*superiore*, late-harvest, and noble-rot wines.[^rules]

[Grechetto di Orvieto](../grapes/grechetto-di-orvieto.md) is therefore important
to understanding the area's whites, but an Orvieto label does not describe a
single-variety wine. Different proportions and cellar methods can give
noticeably different weight and texture within the same appellation.

## Dry whites and the work after pressing

Making a dry white requires decisions about how to extract juice, clarify it,
ferment it, and mature the resulting wine. Gentle
[pressing](../concepts/pressing.md) can limit the extraction of coarse phenolic
material. Temperature management during fermentation and subsequent vessel
choice then influence how directly fruit character appears in the finished
wine.

Palazzone's Campo del Guardiano, an Orvieto Classico Superiore, provides one
concrete approach. The estate describes soft pressing, temperature-controlled
fermentation in steel, and maturation in steel and bottle. A white wine can
therefore receive deliberate maturation without oak supplying its flavour.
The *superiore* designation should not be read as a synonym for barrel ageing.

Nearby Castello della Sala offers a different cellar comparison. For its
2024 Cervaro della Sala, Antinori fermented Chardonnay in barriques and allowed
partial [malolactic fermentation](../concepts/malolactic-fermentation.md),
then blended it with separately fermented Grechetto. These decisions help
explain why the wines of the wider Orvieto area cannot be understood from
geography alone.

## Sweetness and noble rot

A sweet Orvieto may involve a different harvest strategy from a dry white.
*Noble rot* develops when *Botrytis cinerea* affects ripe grapes under suitable
conditions, concentrating the fruit while changing its composition. The same
fungus can damage a crop, so a grower cannot equate any rotten bunch with
suitable material for sweet wine. Selection and the sequence of weather
conditions matter.

Palazzone's Muffa Nobile illustrates the labour involved. The producer
selectively harvests botrytized fruit during October and November, then
presses it and ferments the juice in steel. Individual bunches do not reach
the same condition together, making the picking decision more selective than
simply choosing a later date for the whole vineyard.

This differs from [appassimento](../concepts/appassimento.md), in which
harvested grapes are dried. Both methods can concentrate sugar, but the timing
and biological processes differ. Comparing Orvieto's noble-rot wines with
[Montefalco](montefalco.md)'s [Sagrantino](../grapes/sagrantino.md) passito gives
a useful Umbrian example of that distinction.
