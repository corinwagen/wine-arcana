# Burgundy

Burgundy, or Bourgogne, is a wine-growing region of eastern France in which
labels often identify a village or vineyard before naming a grape.
[Chardonnay](../grapes/chardonnay.md) and [Pinot Noir](../grapes/pinot-noir.md)
connect its districts, but they become different wines as growing conditions,
harvest, and cellar practices change.

The region includes northern vineyards around [Chablis](chablis.md), the Côte de
Nuits and Côte de Beaune, the Côte Chalonnaise, and the Mâconnais. These names
provide a geographical framework before the finer distinctions between villages
and vineyard parcels begin.

## Districts and growing conditions

Chablis lies apart from the main sequence of vineyards farther south. Spring
frost is a recurring concern in this northern setting. The Côte de Nuits and
Côte de Beaune form the Côte d'Or, where slopes, combe openings, elevation, and
aspect change conditions over short distances. The Côte Chalonnaise continues
the patchwork southward; the Mâconnais generally receives a warmer, more
southerly influence.

Clay, limestone, and marl are common parts of the geological vocabulary. Their
effects depend on soil depth, drainage, rooting, and available water, alongside
exposure and weather. Shallow soils can drain readily in a wet season yet leave
vines short of water during drought.

The name *climat* has a particular regional meaning: a delimited vineyard parcel
recognized through its physical conditions and history of cultivation. It is
distinct from the ordinary meaning of climate. UNESCO's account of the Côte d'Or
climats emphasizes both natural variation and the work of growers, religious
institutions, towns, and regulators in developing this landscape.

## Grapes beyond the familiar pair

Pinot Noir supplies the main still reds, while Chardonnay supplies the main
whites. The Côte de Nuits is especially associated with red wine; the Côte de
Beaune includes both red and white traditions.

[Aligoté](../grapes/aligote.md) gives another white-wine route, including
Bouzeron and Bourgogne Aligoté. Gamay appears in some regional wines and
connects to neighbouring [Beaujolais](beaujolais.md), to the south. Crémant de
Bourgogne applies [traditional-method sparkling
production](../styles/traditional-method-sparkling-wine.md) to the regional
fruit.

## How the appellation hierarchy works

Bourgogne's wine body presents four familiar levels of labeling: regional,
village, village premier cru, and grand cru. Regional appellations cover broader
origins; village appellations refer to defined local areas. Premier cru
identifies classified sites within applicable village appellations, while grands
crus have their own appellations. Additional geographical denominations can
narrow an existing appellation without creating a separate one.[^names]

## Producer, vintage, and cellar

The same named site may be divided among several owners. Growers can differ in
crop, picking, whole-bunch use, extraction, lees work, and new-oak exposure.
Those differences remain important even when the wine comes from closely
neighbouring rows.

Comparing different sites from one producer in the same year helps hold some
cellar choices steady. Following one site across vintages shows how weather
affects its wine. Both approaches can help distinguish the influences that
contribute to [terroir](../concepts/terroir.md).
