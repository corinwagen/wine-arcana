# Santa Lucia Highlands

Santa Lucia Highlands is a wine-growing appellation on the slopes above the
western side of California's Salinas Valley, in Monterey County.
[Pinot Noir](../grapes/pinot-noir.md) and
[Chardonnay](../grapes/chardonnay.md) are its principal reference grapes.
Morning sunlight and cooling air from Monterey Bay give these inland-facing
vineyards a substantial maritime influence.

The American Viticultural Area, or AVA, was recognized in 1991. Vineyard names
such as Sleepy Hollow and Double L are useful further distinctions: the
appellation describes a stretch of benchland with differences in exposure,
soil, and proximity to the bay.

## Sunlight and marine air

The vineyards occupy broadly east- and southeast-facing slopes of the Santa
Lucia range. They receive morning sun, while fog and afternoon winds move
inland from Monterey Bay through the Salinas Valley. This cooling can extend
ripening, allowing grapes to develop over a long season despite California's
summer sunshine.

Soils include weathered granitic material and gravelly loams. At Sleepy
Hollow, Talbott describes differences among its northern, western, and
southern parcels; at Double L, Morgan identifies a granitic Chualar loam.
These are vineyard descriptions, rather than evidence for one soil type
throughout the AVA. Root-zone texture and depth help govern water supply;
they do not directly establish a flavour of granite in the wine.

Marine cooling also varies by season. Morgan's 2022 report records dry
winter conditions, cool and windy flowering weather, and reduced cluster
counts. Above-average summer temperatures, less marine cloud cover, and a
September heatwave then accelerated harvest. A cool regional setting remains
exposed to heat and water shortages.

## Grapes, parcels, and winemaking

Pinot Noir's response to harvest date, extraction, and stem use makes cellar
information particularly helpful alongside a vineyard name. Morgan's 2022
Double L Dijon Clones bottling used clones 667 and 777, with 25%
[whole-cluster fermentation](../concepts/whole-cluster-fermentation.md),
followed by eleven months in French oak, partly new. This documents one way
to build aroma and structure from a named estate site.

Chardonnay offers a different set of decisions. Talbott's account of its
Sleepy Hollow wines describes pressing fruit in small lots and choosing
stainless steel, oak, or a combination according to vintage and intended
style. Separate parcels and clonal material supply options before vessel
choice changes the wine further. A broad-textured Chardonnay and a fresher,
less overtly wooded one can consequently share the same regional origin.

The two estates provide complementary examples: Double L connects organic
vineyard management and documented Pinot Noir methods, while Sleepy Hollow,
first planted in 1972, illustrates a longstanding vineyard source used for
both Chardonnay and Pinot Noir. Their practices help make the regional name
concrete without defining a compulsory Santa Lucia Highlands style.
