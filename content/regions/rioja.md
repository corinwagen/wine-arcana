# Rioja

Rioja is a protected wine region along the Ebro and its tributaries in northern
Spain. The name belongs to a Denominación de Origen Calificada (DOCa), whose
rules cover origin, authorized grapes, production, and labelling. It does not
identify one grape or one recipe. Rioja includes red, white, rosé, and
[traditional-method sparkling wine](../styles/traditional-method-sparkling-wine.md),
although oak-matured red wine has shaped its international image most strongly.

Two parallel systems help explain a Rioja label. Rioja Alta, Rioja Alavesa, and
Rioja Oriental identify broad origins, while newer indications can narrow the
source to a municipality or a recognized vineyard. *Crianza*, *Reserva*, and
*Gran Reserva* instead certify minimum maturation. Place and time may appear
together, but neither determines a single house style.

## Geography and the three zones

The denomination runs roughly west to east through a varied corridor of river
terraces, slopes, and tributary valleys. Atlantic weather has greater influence
in the west, while Mediterranean influence increases eastward. The Sierra de
Cantabria and other ranges interrupt weather systems, and elevation, exposure,
and distance from the Ebro create important differences within each zone. The
three-zone map is therefore an orientation, not a set of uniform terroirs.

Rioja Alta occupies most of the western and central part of the denomination.
Its generally cooler, wetter, more Atlantic-influenced conditions tend to slow
ripening and help grapes retain acidity, though lower and warmer sites do not
behave like high, exposed ones. Rioja Alavesa lies mainly north of the Ebro,
between the river and the Sierra de Cantabria. It also receives Atlantic
influence but sits where Atlantic and Mediterranean patterns meet; its small
terraced parcels vary with altitude and shelter as much as with the zone name.

Rioja Oriental, called Rioja Baja until 2018, extends into the warmer and
generally drier east. Mediterranean influence and lower elevations can advance
ripening and favour grapes that need a long warm season, especially
[Garnacha](../grapes/grenache.md). Yet the zone also contains cool upland and
mountain sites. A warm low-lying vineyard in the east and a high eastern slope
may differ more than the word *Oriental* suggests. Research across the DOCa
likewise finds a substantial west-to-east temperature range rather than three
neat climatic blocks.[^1]

## Grapes and wine

[Tempranillo](../grapes/tempranillo.md) is the principal red grape and ripens
relatively early. It can supply colour and tannin for long maturation, but its
balance changes with heat, water, crop level, and harvest date. Garnacha is
later-ripening and has a particularly strong association with warmer eastern
sites, although it is grown elsewhere and may lead a wine rather than merely
soften a blend. Graciano and [Mazuelo—the Rioja name for
Carignan](../grapes/carignan.md)—occupy much
less vineyard area but can contribute acidity, tannin, aroma, or colour.
Mazuelo's later cycle may be useful in warm conditions, though that potential
does not make it a simple replacement for Tempranillo.

[Viura, also known as Macabeo](../grapes/macabeo.md), is the predominant white
grape. [Garnacha Blanca](../grapes/grenache-blanc.md), Malvasía de Rioja,
Tempranillo Blanco, and other authorized local and international varieties
broaden the possible blends. The current rules name five red and nine white
varieties; authorization does not imply equal regional importance or a fixed
formula.[^2]

Variety and zone interact with cellar choices. A producer may blend grapes and
origins to balance ripeness, acidity, and structure from one vintage to the
next, or bottle a single variety and place. Short extraction or [carbonic
maceration](../concepts/carbonic-maceration.md) can make a supple red for early
drinking. Longer extraction, barrel maturation, and bottle age can produce a
firmer and more developed wine. Rioja white can likewise be youthful and
unwooded or mature for years in wood and bottle. These are production choices,
not sensory profiles guaranteed by the regional name.

## Maturation terms

For still red wine, *Crianza* requires at least 24 months of maturation,
including 12 months in 225-litre oak barrels. *Reserva* requires at least 36
months in barrel and bottle, including 12 months in barrel and six months in
bottle. *Gran Reserva* requires at least 60 months, including 24 months in
barrel and 24 in bottle. The corresponding minimums for white and rosé are 18
months with six in barrel for Crianza, 24 months with six in barrel for Reserva,
and 48 months with six in barrel for Gran Reserva.[^3]

These are eligibility thresholds, not instructions to release every wine as
soon as it qualifies. They do not specify the grape blend, vineyard, oak
species, barrel age, extraction, racking regime, or the character of the
remaining maturation. The official *Genérico* category is equally easy to
misread: it includes wines without one of the certified maturation terms, not
only simple young wine. A producer can deliberately mature a wine outside the
prescribed sequence and release it as Genérico.

## Origin indications and house style

Rioja's 2017 reforms made origin more prominent alongside maturation. *Vino de
Zona* names Rioja Alta, Rioja Alavesa, or Rioja Oriental; *Vino de Municipio*
names a municipality. Both require traceable local production, ageing, and
bottling, subject to a limited exception for long-established supplies from a
neighbouring area. *Viñedo Singular* is narrower and more demanding: the grapes
must come from the recognized parcel or parcels, the vines must be at least 35
years old, harvest is manual, yields are lower, and the wine undergoes an
additional assessment.[^4] These conditions establish origin and production
requirements, not a formal ranking of every site or wine.

The geographical and maturation axes can overlap: a Viñedo Singular may also
qualify as Reserva, while a multi-zone blend may qualify as Gran Reserva.
Historically, blending fruit from different grapes and sectors allowed large
houses to pursue continuity across vintages. Extended maturation and American
oak became strongly associated with a recognizable traditional Rioja, but they
were never the only practices. Producers now also use French or neutral wood,
shorter élevage, concrete, single-vineyard fruit, and minority varieties.

Consequently, *Gran Reserva* means that a wine met specified minimum periods;
it does not mean that every Gran Reserva shares the same oak flavour, maturity,
weight, or quality. House style comes from sourcing, blending, extraction,
vessels, time, and release decisions. Reading a Rioja label well requires all
three coordinates—origin, maturation term, and producer—rather than treating
one legal word as a complete description.
