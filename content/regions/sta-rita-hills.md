# Sta. Rita Hills

Sta. Rita Hills is an American viticultural area (AVA) in the western Santa
Ynez Valley of [Santa Barbara County](santa-barbara-county.md), California.
Ocean air moves inland through its east–west corridors, supporting a long,
cool growing season associated especially with
[Pinot Noir](../grapes/pinot-noir.md) and
[Chardonnay](../grapes/chardonnay.md). The AVA was established in 2001.[^1]

Its vineyards differ in exposure, soils, and planting material, and producers
make distinct decisions about harvest, stems, extraction, and barrels.

## Marine air and hillsides

The Purisima Hills lie to the north and the Santa Rosa Hills to the south.
Between them, valley corridors provide routes for Pacific fog and wind.
These openings help explain why vineyards at this southerly latitude can
ripen varieties associated with cooler climates.

Marine sedimentary material is an important part of the region's geology,
but individual vineyards contain different combinations of sand, clay, shale,
and other material. Those differences affect rooting and water supply.
Aspect and shelter also matter: a slope's exposure to afternoon wind changes
the conditions experienced by leaves and fruit.

Sanford's adjacent vineyards illustrate that local variation. Sanford &
Benedict has clay-loam soils with fractured shale and chert. At La Rinconada,
the estate describes sandy loam on lower ground and more clay, shale, and
diatomaceous material higher up. The winery farms and vinifies individual
blocks separately. A named vineyard can therefore contain several distinct
sources of fruit, even before differences in vine age and clonal selection
are considered.

## Ripeness and winemaking

The maritime setting gives growers time to develop flavor while retaining
[acidity](../concepts/acidity.md), but harvest date remains a stylistic
choice. Picking later can increase sugar and potential alcohol; the outcome
also depends on crop level, weather, and fruit condition.

In Pinot Noir, the decision to retain stems during
[whole-cluster fermentation](../concepts/whole-cluster-fermentation.md) adds
another source of variation. Stems can change aroma and tannin, while the
proportion of intact berries changes how fermentation proceeds. The
extraction regime and length of [maceration](../concepts/maceration.md) then
shape the wine's texture.

Melville's 2022 Estate Pinot Noir is one documented example. The producer
fermented 40% of the fruit as whole clusters, with the balance destemmed.
Skin and stem contact averaged about a month, and maturation used well-seasoned
French oak. This combination distinguishes extraction from oak flavor:
a wine can receive substantial skin contact while acquiring little fresh
wood aroma.

## Vineyard continuity

The local winegrowers' alliance places the planting of Sanford & Benedict in
1971 at the beginning of the area's modern vineyard history. The vineyard
remains part of Sanford's estate, alongside La Rinconada, planted in 1997.
Sanford's continuing work with surviving early plantings gives readers a
reference for the region's development over several generations of vines.

Melville provides a complementary reference through its detailed estate
bottlings and published fermentation records. Comparing those methods with
Sanford's block-based vineyard account is useful because each describes a
different part of the process: where differences in fruit originate, and how
a winemaker chooses to handle that fruit.
