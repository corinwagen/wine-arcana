# Sierra Foothills

Sierra Foothills is a broad California wine region between the Central
Valley and the higher Sierra Nevada mountains. Its vineyards occupy a belt
of hills, ridges, and river valleys on the mountains' western side.
[Zinfandel](../grapes/zinfandel.md),
[Barbera](../grapes/barbera.md), and Rhône varieties connect a nineteenth-century
wine history with continuing choices about grapes and vineyard sites.

The American Viticultural Area, or AVA, extends through parts of eight
counties, from Yuba in the north to Mariposa in the south.[^boundary] Such a
large origin leaves room for substantial variation. Amador County, El
Dorado, and Fair Play supply more local context for many wines bearing the
broader regional name.

## Between the valley and the mountains

The region has warm summer days, cooler nights, and predominantly winter
rainfall. Elevation and hillside exposure can moderate heat relative to the
Central Valley, but the foothills should not be confused with the colder,
snowier high mountains. The original appellation proposal described both
the summer warmth and cooling foothill winds.

Soils formed from several geological materials, including granitic,
metamorphic, and volcanic rock. Texture, depth, and slope affect rooting and
water storage. Cedarville's organically farmed estate in Fair Play, for
example, sits at about 2,500 feet on granitic ground. That identifies a
particular growing environment within the region rather than a universal
foothill soil.

The US Department of Agriculture's broader Sierra Nevada Foothills
description notes that rainfall generally increases with elevation and from
south to north. Its ecological region and the wine appellation are different
boundaries, but the pattern helps explain why one water-management recipe
would be inadequate across this long belt of vineyards.

## Gold-country history and changing grapes

The Gold Rush created settlements and demand for agricultural products,
including wine. Viticulture developed alongside mining, followed by decline
as mining populations fell, phylloxera spread, and Prohibition interrupted
the industry. The twentieth-century revival built on surviving vineyards
while introducing new plantings and ambitions.

Barbera provides a documented example of that renewal. University of
California historian Nancy Sweet records Cary Gott's 1971 planting at
Monteviña in Amador County, following Darrell Corti's suggestion, and the
1974 wine that followed. Barbera's ability to retain appreciable acidity at
maturity helped make it interesting for warm California sites. It supplies
a regional comparison with its established home in [Piedmont](piedmont.md).

The current grape range also includes [Syrah](../grapes/syrah.md),
[Grenache](../grapes/grenache.md),
[Viognier](../grapes/viognier.md), and
[Petite Sirah](../grapes/durif.md), alongside Zinfandel. Cedarville's estate
and documented wines illustrate that diversity in Fair Play. Thinking of
the foothills only as a Zinfandel region obscures both the revival-era
Italian varieties and the subsequent Rhône plantings.

## Reading a foothill wine

A county, smaller appellation, or vineyard name helps narrow the origin.
Elevation alone is insufficient: slope exposure, water supply, crop load,
and harvest date remain important. For the red wines, extraction and
[oak maturation](../concepts/oak-maturation.md) further change how ripeness
and tannin are expressed. Comparing a warm Amador site with a higher Fair
Play vineyard is more informative when grape and cellar methods are known.
