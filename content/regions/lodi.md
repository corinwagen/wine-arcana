# Lodi

Lodi is a California wine region east of the Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta,
where inland sunshine meets cooling air arriving from the San Francisco Bay
area. It is associated with old [Zinfandel](../grapes/zinfandel.md) vineyards,
but its contemporary wines also include
[Cabernet Sauvignon](../grapes/cabernet-sauvignon.md),
[Chardonnay](../grapes/chardonnay.md),
Spanish varieties, and many smaller plantings.

The Lodi American viticultural area (AVA) was established in 1986. Seven
smaller AVAs followed in 2006: Alta Mesa, Borden Ranch, Clements Hills,
Cosumnes River, Jahant, Mokelumne River, and Sloughhouse.[^1] These names help
separate river plains from older terraces and eastern hills.

## Delta air and different soils

The growing season is warm and predominantly dry. Marine air moves inland
through the Bay and Delta, moderating temperatures, especially later in the
day. Lodi therefore experiences a different combination of heat and air flow
from inland districts with less access to those breezes. Winter rainfall and
the soil's capacity to store water matter through the dry summer.

The ground also changes across the appellation. Around the city, Mokelumne
River vineyards often occupy deep sandy loam. Farther east, rolling terrain
includes older clay-rich and stony soils. Jahant has distinctive soils with
cemented clay and gravel layers, while Borden Ranch includes older stream
and volcanic deposits. These differences influence rooting depth, drainage,
and the water available to vines.

That variation makes a single regional soil description inadequate. The
practical questions are how quickly a particular block drains, how much
water remains within rooting reach, and whether restricted layers limit root
growth. The answers affect irrigation, canopy growth, and how evenly fruit
ripens.

## Old vines and a changing identity

For much of its commercial history, Lodi supplied grapes to wineries whose
labels emphasized California rather than the district. Wine writer Mike
Dunne's regional account describes the later effort to establish Lodi's own
identity through vineyard-designated wines and explicit regional labeling.
That change made the source of familiar wines more visible to drinkers.

Zinfandel offers a direct connection between those histories. Old vineyards
preserve particular planting layouts, vine populations, and farming habits.
The Lucas Winery's ZinStar vineyard, planted in 1933, is one continuing example.
Dunne identifies the producer's emphasis on freshness and a less forceful
style, illustrating the range possible within Lodi Zinfandel. Lucas continues
to farm ZinStar organically and bottle wines under that name.

The grape's uneven ripening helps explain this stylistic range. Waiting for
less mature berries can leave other berries increasingly ripe or shriveled.
Harvest timing consequently affects the balance of fresh fruit, concentrated
fruit, [acidity](../concepts/acidity.md), and potential alcohol. Vine age
provides historical context, while current farming and picking decisions
still determine the fruit delivered to the winery.

## Beyond Zinfandel

Bokisch Vineyards offers a contrasting view of Lodi through Spanish grapes.
Markus and Liz Bokisch began cultivating them in the late 1990s; the
producer's current range includes [Albariño](../grapes/albarino.md),
[Tempranillo](../grapes/tempranillo.md),
[Garnacha](../grapes/grenache.md), and [Graciano](../grapes/graciano.md).
Its presence connects regional
varietal diversity to a sustained grower and winemaker commitment.

These wines widen the useful comparisons within Lodi. A white wine built
around freshness, an earlier-picked Zinfandel, and a structured Tempranillo
have different harvest and cellar requirements even when grown in the same
broad climate. The smaller AVA and vineyard name locate the source more
precisely, while the producer's harvest and maturation choices help explain
the wine's weight and texture.
