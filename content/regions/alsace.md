# Alsace

Alsace is a narrow French wine region on the eastern side of the Vosges,
extending north and south through the foothills above the Rhine plain. Its
wines are often labelled with a grape variety, but the regional name covers
several distinct legal levels and a wide range of dry, sweet, and sparkling
styles. Geography matters here because a small change in slope, soil, or
shelter can alter ripening and the wine a grower chooses to make.

## Geography and climate

The vineyards occupy the lower slopes and fans at the foot of the Vosges,
chiefly in the Bas-Rhin and Haut-Rhin. The current Alsace specification
describes a semi-continental climate with cold winters, warm summers, marked
temperature differences, and varied mesoclimates created by valleys, mountain
positions, and exposure.[^1] The Vosges form a rain shadow: westerly Atlantic
air rises over the mountains, losing moisture on the western side, so the
eastern foothills are comparatively dry and sunny. This helps grapes remain
healthy late into the season, while cool nights and the region's northern
latitude can preserve [acidity](../concepts/acidity.md).

The shelter is not uniform. Valley mouths, hillside orientation, elevation,
and local airflow change the balance between warmth, drying, and frost risk.
The geological mix is equally discontinuous. Vosges-derived granite,
sandstone, schist, and volcanic material occur alongside limestone and marl on
the sub-Vosges hills, with colluvial fans and alluvial ground lower down. Soil
depth and water-holding capacity therefore vary between adjacent parcels: a
shallow, stony slope and a deeper marl-rich site do not give vines the same
supply of water or the same pace of ripening.

## Grapes and the Pinot blanc name

The principal white grapes are [Riesling](../grapes/riesling.md),
[Gewürztraminer](../grapes/gewurztraminer.md), [Pinot Gris](../grapes/pinot-gris.md), [Pinot Blanc](../grapes/pinot-blanc.md),
Auxerrois, Muscat, and [Sylvaner](../grapes/silvaner.md).
[Pinot Noir](../grapes/pinot-noir.md) is the
important red grape and is also used for rosé and for some sparkling wine.
[Chardonnay](../grapes/chardonnay.md) has a smaller but legally recognized role, especially in Crémant
d'Alsace.

Alsace labelling makes the relationship between Pinot blanc and
[Auxerrois](../grapes/auxerrois-blanc.md) unusually important. They are separate
grape varieties. Under the current
regional specification, the usage name “Auxerrois” refers to Auxerrois B,
while “Pinot blanc” may refer to Pinot blanc B and Auxerrois B; “Pinot” or
“Klevner” likewise covers those two varieties.[^1] A bottle labelled Pinot
blanc therefore does not necessarily contain only the variety botanically
called Pinot blanc.

## Wine styles

Most Alsace wines are still whites, and the basic AOC “Alsace” or “Vin
d'Alsace” designation can carry a grape name, a blend, a commune-level
geographical name, or a smaller lieu-dit. A dry wine may be fermented until
little grape sugar remains, while a wine from the same grape and area can
retain sweetness because fermentation is stopped or slows before all the sugar
is consumed. Acidity, harvest date, botrytis, and cellar decisions change the
balance, so the grape name alone does not settle sweetness.

The regional specification reserves “vendanges tardives” and “sélection de
grains nobles” for wines made from Gewürztraminer, Muscat, Pinot Gris, or
Riesling. Vendanges tardives indicates late-harvest grapes meeting higher
ripeness requirements; sélection de grains nobles requires selected,
concentrated berries and higher thresholds. Both terms must be accompanied by
the permitted grape denomination and the vintage.[^1]

Alsace also makes red and rosé Pinot Noir, blends sold under names such as
Edelzwicker, and [Crémant d'Alsace](../styles/traditional-method-sparkling-wine.md).
Crémant is a separate AOC for white or rosé sparkling wine made by the
traditional method. Its current specification permits Riesling, Pinot Blanc,
Pinot Noir, Pinot Gris, Auxerrois, and Chardonnay; the white wine may use one
or more of these, while the rosé is made exclusively from Pinot Noir.[^2] The
grapes are harvested and pressed under rules suited to a base wine, then
undergo a second fermentation in bottle.

## Reading the designations

**Alsace AOC** is the broad regional designation. It allows the widest
combination of permitted varieties and label options, including a grape-named
wine, a blend, certain geographical complements, lieu-dit names, and the
late-harvest terms described above.

**Alsace grand cru AOC** applies to individually delimited vineyard parcels,
not to every vineyard in a commune. The current adopted specification
recognizes 51 named grand cru appellations and requires hand harvest, lower
yields, and higher grape-maturity thresholds than the broad regional category.
Most are reserved for still white wines from Riesling, Gewürztraminer, Pinot
Gris, or specified Muscat grapes, with particular exceptions and blend rules;
Hengst, Kirchberg de Barr, and Vorbourg also permit still red wines from Pinot
Noir.[^3] The name is therefore a delimited appellation with its own rules,
not a general quality adjective that a producer can add to an Alsace wine.

**Crémant d'Alsace AOC** is the sparkling designation. It shares the Alsace
area but is defined by sparkling-wine categories, permitted grapes, and
traditional-method production.
