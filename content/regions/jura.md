# Jura

Jura is a small wine region in eastern France, along the western foothills of
the Jura mountains. Its vineyards run through the Revermont, the uneven strip
between the Bresse plain and the first limestone plateau. The region is known
for Savagnin, [vin jaune](../styles/vin-jaune.md), and unusual cellar
traditions, and also produces fresh white wines, pale and darker reds,
[rosé](../styles/rose.md), [traditional-method sparkling wine](../styles/traditional-method-sparkling-wine.md), and sweet [vin de paille](../styles/vin-de-paille.md).

The name Jura describes this wider region. **Côtes du Jura** is a separate,
delimited appellation that runs through much of it. [Arbois](arbois.md),
[Château-Chalon](chateau-chalon.md), and
L'Étoile are other geographic appellations; Crémant du Jura and Macvin du Jura
are product appellations whose areas overlap the vineyard zone. A bottle from
the Jura is therefore not necessarily a Côtes du Jura, and Côtes du Jura is not
a synonym for every wine made in the region.

## Geography and climate

The vineyards occupy the lower slopes and bases of the first Jura plateau,
where hard Jurassic limestone stands above older marls and clays. Faulting and
erosion have left elongated limestone hills and marly hollows. Many vineyard
parcels lie on slopes under the plateau's wooded cornice or on the better-
exposed sides of these hills. Limestone scree on the surface can improve
drainage and warm quickly, while marl and clay below retain coolness and water.
The resulting patchwork makes slope, exposure, soil depth, and water supply
important at short distances.[^1]

Jura has a cool, wet climate with oceanic and continental influences. The
adopted Côtes du Jura specification describes annual rainfall above 1,000
millimetres, a mean annual temperature of about 10.5 °C, warm humid summers,
and relatively dry, windy autumns.[^1] The climate makes disease and ripening
decisions significant, but the autumn dryness can help late-season harvests and
the drying of grapes for vin de paille. South-, southwest-, and west-facing
slopes receive more sun and some shelter from cold easterly and northerly
winds.

## Appellations and grapes

Arbois is centered on the northern Jura town and its surrounding communes.
Côtes du Jura follows a longer north–south strip. Château-Chalon is a small,
steep appellation especially associated with vin jaune, while L'Étoile is a
small white-wine appellation near Lons-le-Saunier that can also produce vin
jaune and vin de paille. Crémant du Jura overlaps the geographic appellations
and is reserved for sparkling wine. Macvin du Jura is a wine of liqueur made
from fresh grape must and Jura marc brandy.

The principal grapes are [Chardonnay](../grapes/chardonnay.md) and
[Savagnin](../grapes/savagnin.md) for white wines, and [Pinot Noir](../grapes/pinot-noir.md),
[Poulsard](../grapes/poulsard.md), and [Trousseau](../grapes/trousseau.md) for red
and rosé wines. Their roles overlap: the appellation rules permit red grapes
in some white wines and white grapes in some red or rosé wines, and all five
can contribute to the region's broader range. Poulsard often gives lightly
coloured reds, Pinot Noir can deepen the colour and fruit profile, and Trousseau
usually needs warmer, gravelly sites to ripen fully. Savagnin and Trousseau are
planted less widely in part because their site requirements are relatively
strict.[^1]

## Wine styles and maturation

Jura's ordinary still whites are commonly based on Chardonnay, Savagnin, or a
blend of the two. A topped-up, or *ouillé*, wine is kept close to full in its
barrel or other vessel: the producer replaces wine lost to evaporation. Lorch
describes *ouillé* as one of Jura's two main white-wine families, alongside
*sous voile*.

The contrasting Jura tradition is *sous voile*, meaning “under a veil.” The
wine ages in oak without topping-up, under a surface veil of yeast. Savagnin is
the classic grape for this style. Vin jaune begins as a dry white and, under the
relevant Jura appellation rules, must age in oak without topping-up until at
least 15 December of the sixth year after harvest, including at least 60 months
under the veil. It is released from the seventh year after harvest and is
bottled in the squat 62-centilitre *clavelin*.[^2]

The region's reds range from pale, delicate Poulsard wines to more deeply
coloured Pinot Noir and firmer Trousseau. Rosé can be made from the same red
grapes.

Crémant du Jura is white or rosé sparkling wine made by the traditional method,
with a second fermentation in bottle. Vin de paille takes a different route:
selected Chardonnay, Savagnin, Poulsard, or Trousseau grapes are dried off the
vine for at least six weeks before slow pressing. Under the Côtes du Jura
specification, the resulting sweet wine ages until at least 15 November of the
third year after harvest, including at least 18 months under wood.[^3]
