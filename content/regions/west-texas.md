# West Texas

West Texas contains several distinct winegrowing landscapes, from the broad
Texas High Plains around Lubbock and Brownfield to the Davis Mountains near
Fort Davis and the irrigated borderlands around El Paso. The High Plains is
a major source of grapes for wineries throughout Texas. Mountain vineyards
farther southwest supply a different, smaller part of that regional story.

“West Texas” is a geographical overview rather than the name of one American
viticultural area (AVA). Reading a bottle therefore requires distinguishing
its vineyard origin from the winery's address. Grapes grown near Lubbock can
be made into wine or sold in the Hill Country, hundreds of miles away.

## The High Plains: an elevated agricultural plateau

The Texas High Plains AVA occupies relatively flat country west of the
Caprock Escarpment. The High Plains Winegrowers describes much of its vineyard
land as roughly 3,000–4,000 feet above sea level. Long, hot summer days support
ripening, while cooler nights change the conditions under which the grapes
retain acids. [Altitude](../concepts/altitude.md) is part of that explanation,
alongside latitude, season, and harvest date.

Deep sandy and sandy-loam soils occur across important vineyard districts,
with differences in subsoil clay, drainage, and water storage. Most vineyards
need [irrigation](../concepts/irrigation-and-vine-water-stress.md), commonly
supplied from the Ogallala Aquifer. The quantity and quality of available water
therefore belong in a planting decision alongside the site's temperature
profile.

Growers cultivate both familiar international grapes and varieties associated
with warmer European regions. [Tempranillo](../grapes/tempranillo.md),
[Sangiovese](../grapes/sangiovese.md), [Viognier](../grapes/viognier.md), and
[Roussanne](../grapes/roussanne.md) are useful points of entry. The regional
range also includes [Cabernet Sauvignon](../grapes/cabernet-sauvignon.md) and
[Chenin Blanc](../grapes/chenin-blanc.md). Choosing among them involves cold
survival and harvest timing as well as summer heat.

## A warm region with cold and storm hazards

Texas A&M's account of High Plains research identifies winter freezes,
spring frost, and hail as recurring grower problems. Bud-hardiness monitoring
compares the changing condition of dormant buds with weather forecasts;
hardiness is seasonal rather than a fixed minimum temperature attached to
a grape name.

The same programme tested hail netting and mechanical crop thinning. In the
reported thinning work, lower crop loads accelerated ripening and produced
wines with higher alcohol and pH. Removing fruit therefore changes the
composition and balance of what remains; its value depends on the intended
wine.[^research]

These pressures make [crop load](../concepts/crop-load.md),
[winter hardiness](../concepts/winter-hardiness.md), and
[spring frost](../concepts/spring-frost.md) useful companions to the regional
name. A hot afternoon gives little information about whether buds survived
the preceding winter or fruit escaped a summer hailstorm.

## Davis Mountains and the far west

The Davis Mountains rise from the surrounding desert and create a much more
varied pattern of elevation, slope, and rainfall. Hellman and colleagues'
2011 geographical study distinguishes vineyard land with suitable soils and
water from the much larger extent of rocky mountain terrain. Higher ground
can provide cooler ripening conditions, but plantable soils and irrigation
access constrain where that opportunity becomes a vineyard.[^geography]

The same study compares the Texas Davis Mountains and High Plains with the
warmer Escondido Valley and Mesilla Valley. The latter extends along the
Rio Grande through New Mexico and into Texas near El Paso. These are
different districts within the wider western landscape, with different
temperature and soil profiles.

Contemporary sourcing adds another layer. Alta Marfa's published grower
directory identifies Blue Mountain Trail Vineyard in the Texas Davis
Mountains AVA as a Tempranillo source farmed by Château Wright. It separately
lists vineyards in the Hill Country and New Mexico. A producer based in
Marfa can consequently make wines from several origins; the individual
wine's vineyard information establishes which landscape supplied its fruit.

## From western vineyards to finished wine

McPherson Cellars in Lubbock supplies a concrete High Plains cellar example.
Its Roussanne technical sheet names Farmhouse and La Pradera as vineyard
sources, records no skin contact, and describes seven months in stainless
steel. That approach gives the variety a different setting from an
oak-matured Rhône white: vessel and handling contribute to the finished wine
alongside the grape and climate.

Acid balance also remains a cellar decision. Texas A&M researchers have
investigated using juice from unripe grapes, or *verjus*, to acidify regional
wines with low acidity or high pH. The work illustrates why cool nights alone
cannot establish a finished wine's [acidity](../concepts/acidity.md).
Harvest, blending, and subsequent treatments continue to shape it.

For a deeper comparison, read the vineyard name and production account
together. A High Plains varietal Roussanne, a blended red made from purchased
fruit, and a Davis Mountains vineyard wine represent different combinations
of agriculture and winemaking within West Texas.

[^research]: Kay Ledbetter, “Meeting Grape Producers In The Vineyard,”
    Texas A&M, 25 April 2022. The crop-thinning results concern the reported
    trials, not a universal response to reducing yield.
[^geography]: Hellman and colleagues (2011), district climate, geology, and
    soil comparisons. Its vineyard and winery counts describe that period
    and are not used here as current production totals.
