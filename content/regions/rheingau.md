# Rheingau

The Rheingau is a German wine region along the Rhine and near its confluence
with the Main, west of Frankfurt. [Riesling](../grapes/riesling.md) dominates
its vineyards, while [Spätburgunder](../grapes/pinot-noir.md), the German name
for Pinot Noir, gives Assmannshausen a distinct red-wine association.

The Rhine's change of direction is central to the map. As the river turns
westward near Wiesbaden, vineyards on its northern bank can face south. The
Taunus hills provide shelter behind them. South-facing slopes receive more
sunlight, helping Riesling ripen in this northern setting.

## Vineyards along a bending river

The growing area includes vineyards near the Main and continues west through
Eltville toward Rüdesheim, then north toward Lorch. Broad slopes and narrower,
steeper sites occupy different parts of that route. The German Wine Institute
records a wide range of soils, including loess, clay, sand, quartzite, and
slate, across the region.

Sun exposure helps late-ripening fruit develop, while soil depth and water
storage affect how vines manage a dry season. A warmer slope can be useful in a
cool year and more demanding in heat.

For comparison, [Mosel](mosel.md) supplies a different network of winding river
slopes and tributary valleys. Both regions grow Riesling, but each contains
several kinds of vineyard environment and more than one wine style.

## Riesling across sweetness levels

Riesling's acidity and aromatic development support both dry and sweet wines.
Harvest timing determines the starting balance of sugars, acids, and aroma
precursors. Fermentation determines how much sugar remains in the finished wine.
The [label guide](../concepts/appellations-and-wine-labels.md) explains the
distinction between harvest and sweetness terms.

The terms Kabinett, Spätlese, and Auslese belong to the Prädikat framework, with
requirements related to grape must weight. Spätlese does not by itself mean that
the finished wine is sweet. *Trocken* identifies a legally defined dry category;
*feinherb* has no precisely fixed legal sweetness range in the German Wine
Institute's guidance.[^labels]

Selected, concentrated fruit can also produce sweet wines, including [botrytized
wines](../styles/botrytized-sweet-wine.md) when conditions permit. The weather
and selection required distinguish those wines from the ordinary act of
harvesting later for a dry bottling.

## Pinot Noir and cellar decisions

Assmannshausen's Spätburgunder broadens the regional picture beyond white wine.
As elsewhere with Pinot Noir, harvest, skin contact, stems, and oak shape the
balance of fruit, tannin, and maturation character.

For Riesling, fermentation vessels, retained sugar, lees contact, and release
age create differences within a shared vineyard origin. Wines from the same
locality can range from dry to sweet as producers choose different harvest dates
and fermentation endpoints.
