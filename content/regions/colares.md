# Colares

Colares is a small Atlantic wine denomination near Sintra, west of Lisbon in
Portugal. Its best-known vineyards grow low across coastal sand, with vines
on their own roots. Red [Ramisco](../grapes/ramisco.md) and white
[Malvasia de Colares](../grapes/malvasia-de-colares.md) connect the
wines to a surviving form of European viticulture from before the widespread
use of grafting against [phylloxera](../concepts/phylloxera.md).

That history remains part of a working landscape. Wind protection, demanding
vineyard labour, late ripening, and maturation all help explain the wines more
usefully than rarity alone.

## Sand, wind, and roots

The region lies between the Serra de Sintra and the Atlantic. Maritime winds,
humidity, and mist moderate summer heat but can slow ripening and complicate
keeping fruit healthy late in the season. The sand vineyards use low,
spreading vines and windbreaks, including bamboo screens, to reduce exposure
to coastal winds. Their appearance reflects deliberate cultivation.

In the traditional planting method, an ungrafted cane is rooted in the
underlying layer beneath the sand. The sandy environment allowed these
vineyards to escape the nineteenth-century phylloxera devastation described
in the official specification. This is a property of the vineyard environment;
it does not establish that Ramisco itself resists the insect.

The famous sand vineyards do not describe every permitted site. The
specification published by Portugal's Instituto da Vinha e do Vinho includes
both *chão de areia*, sand over consolidated material, and *chão rijo*, harder
ground with limestone and marl.[^rules] A bottle's vineyard information can
therefore reveal more than the regional name alone.

## Grapes and maturation

Ramisco gives the traditional red a firm combination of acidity and
[tannin](../concepts/tannin.md). Young wine can be astringent; maturation
softens its structure and changes its aromas. Malvasia de Colares supplies
the characteristic white. The local name matters: “Malvasia” is used for
several varieties elsewhere, so it should not be treated as a universal
identity or flavour description.

The official specification requires red wine to spend at least eighteen
months in vessel followed by six in bottle, and white wine six months in
vessel followed by three in bottle.[^rules] Those are minimum requirements.
An individual producer may keep a wine substantially longer.

Adega Regional de Colares's Arenae wines give a concrete comparison. The
coöperative's importer describes Malvasia matured in large, old wooden
vessels and Ramisco given several years of wood maturation. Old, large
containers provide a different relationship between wine, wood, and oxygen
from a new small barrel. The distinction helps explain why substantial
cellar ageing need not mean conspicuous toasted-oak flavour.
