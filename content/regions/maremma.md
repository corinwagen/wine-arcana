# Maremma

Maremma is the wine country of southern coastal Tuscany and its inland hills.
This article focuses on the area around Grosseto, where Mediterranean coastal
sites, volcanic uplands around Pitigliano, and the approaches to Mount Amiata
support different wines. [Sangiovese](../grapes/sangiovese.md),
[Ciliegiolo](../grapes/ciliegiolo.md), and
[Vermentino](../grapes/vermentino.md) offer three useful ways into the region.

The geographical name needs to be distinguished from **Maremma Toscana DOC**.
That denomination covers the province of Grosseto and permits a broad range
of grapes and wine styles. [Morellino di Scansano](morellino-di-scansano.md)
and the [Montecucco](montecucco.md) denominations identify other, more
specific production areas within this
wider wine landscape. Their wines follow their own rules, rather than being
categories of Maremma Toscana DOC.[^area]

## From the coast to the inland hills

The sea moderates coastal temperatures, while distance inland, elevation, and
exposure change the growing environment. The regional climate generally brings
dry summers and more rainfall in autumn and winter. For growers, the practical
challenge is to ripen grapes while retaining sufficient water and acidity;
the answer differs between a low coastal vineyard and an exposed inland slope.

The consortium describes a landscape extending from the Colline Metallifere
in the northwest towards Mount Amiata in the northeast. Around Pitigliano
and Sorano, volcanic tuff helps shape both the vineyard terrain and the towns
themselves. Farther west, Argentario and the island of Giglio include steep,
terraced vineyards above the sea. Clay, sand, alluvium, and volcanic material
all occur, so “Maremma soil” is too broad a description for an individual site.

## Several red-wine traditions

Around Scansano, Sangiovese takes the local name Morellino. The district lies
between the Ombrone and Albegna valleys, with marine influence varying from
site to site. Montecucco gives Sangiovese another setting towards Mount Amiata.

Ciliegiolo has both a blending history and a contemporary varietal role.
Sassotondo's San Lorenzo illustrates the latter around Pitigliano. The wine
comes from a single vineyard on volcanic soil; the estate propagates selected
older vines to replace missing plants and renew part of the vineyard. Its
published method combines fermentation and skin contact in steel with
18–24 months in large Slavonian oak and further bottle maturation. This gives
[extraction](../concepts/maceration.md) and time a substantial role alongside
the grape's familiar cherry-fruit character.

[Cabernet Sauvignon](../grapes/cabernet-sauvignon.md),
[Merlot](../grapes/merlot.md), and [Syrah](../grapes/syrah.md) also belong to
the region's present-day vineyard mix. *WineNews* describes Rocca di
Montemassi, near Roccastrada, as an estate working with both Tuscan and
international varieties. The mix provides scope for varietal bottlings and
blends with quite different proportions of fruit, tannin, and oak influence.

## White wine and cellar choices

Vermentino offers a white-wine counterpart to this range of reds. Maremma
Toscana's 2023 specification added a Vermentino Superiore category, requiring
at least 95% Vermentino, compared with 85% for the standard varietal
wine.[^vermentino] The designation sets production requirements; fermentation
vessel, lees contact, and harvest timing still shape the individual wine.

Rocca di Montemassi's Calasole 2024 provides a concrete example of texture
developed without barrel fermentation. Its technical sheet describes gentle
pressing, fermentation in stainless steel, and six months on fine lees before
bottling. [Lees ageing](../concepts/lees-aging.md) can therefore be an
important part of a white wine even when overt wood flavour is absent.

The coastline also retains Ansonica, the grape known in Sicily as
[Inzolia](../grapes/inzolia.md), while the area around Pitigliano has its own
white-blending tradition.
