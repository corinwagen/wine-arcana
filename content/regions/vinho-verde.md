# Vinho Verde

Vinho Verde is a wine region and protected designation in northwestern
Portugal, centred between the Minho and Douro rivers and extending from the
Atlantic coast toward the mountains of the interior. It is often reduced to a
light, young white wine with a faint prickle. That style is important, but it
does not define the region.

The words *vinho verde* literally mean “green wine,” yet accounts of *verde*
differ. Some relate it to the wine's traditional youth and fresh acidity;
others to the green, wet landscape. Whatever the name's origin, its present
legal meaning is geographical. The designation covers still and sparkling
white, red, and rosé wines, including wines made for development in bottle. A
still, dry, mature Vinho Verde is no contradiction.[^1]

## Atlantic geography and wet viticulture

River valleys carry maritime air inland through a landscape that rises from
the coast. Most vineyard soils derive from granite and tend to be acidic and
fairly shallow, though bands of schist and patches of clay and alluvium add
local variation. The official specification gives average annual rainfall of
about 1,200 millimetres, concentrated in winter and spring. That figure hides
a large gradient: exposed coastal valleys are generally milder and wetter,
while higher or sheltered inland sites have greater temperature swings and
less direct Atlantic influence.

Moisture and mild temperatures can slow ripening and help grapes retain
acidity. They also encourage vigorous shoots and fungal disease. Heavy spring
rain can disrupt flowering and fruit set while making downy mildew harder to
control; rain near harvest can dilute or damage fruit and delay work in the
vineyard.[^2] Growers therefore manage a tension between enough leaf area to
ripen a crop and an open fruit zone that admits light and air.

Traditional vines climbed trees, crossed between them, edged fields, or spread
over high pergolas called *ramadas*. These systems belonged to mixed farms and
kept the ground available for other crops, but dense, shaded canopies could
delay ripening. Modern cordon and other continuous trellises make pruning,
spraying, canopy control, and mechanization easier. Neither old nor new
training removes the basic pressures of a wet season, so drainage, exposure,
crop level, and timely canopy work remain consequential.

## Nine subregions, not one climate

The denomination recognizes Amarante, Ave, Baião, Basto, Cávado, Lima, Monção
e Melgaço, Paiva, and Sousa. These are legal subregions with their own permitted
grape lists, but they are most useful as a map of the region's climatic
transitions rather than nine self-contained styles.[^3]

Ave, Cávado, and Sousa occupy relatively low river basins with mild conditions
and strong maritime influence; Arinto, Loureiro, and earlier-ripening
Trajadura often meet there in blends. Lima is especially open to Atlantic air
and rain and is closely associated with Loureiro. Farther inland, Amarante,
Baião, and Basto are higher or more sheltered. Their larger temperature
ranges and warmer summers can bring later varieties such as Azal and Avesso to
greater maturity and can also favor red grapes. Paiva is another relatively
protected sector, with less rain and useful conditions for both Avesso and
local reds.

Monção e Melgaço forms a distinct northeastern pocket along the Minho River.
Surrounding mountains reduce direct maritime exposure, producing greater
temperature ranges and intermediate rainfall. These conditions help
[Alvarinho](../grapes/albarino.md) reach sugar and flavour maturity while
retaining acidity. The subregion is its Portuguese reference point, but the
same geography can yield different balances according to elevation, exposure,
season, and harvest date.

## Grapes

The principal white grapes solve different parts of the regional ripening
problem. [Loureiro](../grapes/loureiro.md), especially important in Lima and
other maritime sectors, brings marked aroma and acidity. Alvarinho has its
strongest association with sheltered Monção e Melgaço and can make broader,
more concentrated wines. [Arinto](../grapes/arinto.md), locally called
Pedernã, often reinforces acidity and structure. [Trajadura](../grapes/treixadura.md)
ripens earlier in several maritime subregions and can soften a blend, while
Avesso and late-ripening Azal find warmer inland sites particularly useful.
These roles are tendencies, not a required recipe; each grape can also appear
alone.

Red grapes are not a historical footnote. [Vinhão](../grapes/souson.md), the
regional name for Sousón, can give deeply colored wines with pronounced
acidity and tannin. Espadeiro and Padeiro are also used for rosé and less dense
reds. [Borraçal](../grapes/caino-tinto.md), Amaral, and
[Alvarelhão](../grapes/brancellao.md) add further differences in color,
acidity, aroma, and ripening season. Their importance varies by subregion, and
traditional red Vinho Verde remains more visible locally than in many export
markets.

## Still, lightly spritzy, and sparkling wine

Red wines appear to have led the region's early reputation; white production
rose during the twentieth century as vineyard training and cellar equipment
changed.[^4] The familiar modern export style is usually a youthful white
blend, sometimes with a little residual sugar and dissolved carbon dioxide.
Historically, quick transfer or bottling could retain fermentation-derived
gas. Some current bottlings instead use added carbon dioxide to preserve the
expected prickle. The PDO rules do not require still Vinho Verde to have it.

Still whites now range from light blends to dry, single-variety or
site-specific wines with more body and texture. Lees contact, wood, and bottle
age can broaden Alvarinho, Loureiro, and other varieties without erasing their
acidity. Still rosé and red wines likewise vary with grape, ripeness,
extraction, and maturation rather than conforming to the export stereotype.

The PDO also recognizes *Espumante de Vinho Verde* and *Espumante de Qualidade
de Vinho Verde* as separate sparkling-wine categories. Producers use both tank
and [traditional methods](../styles/traditional-method-sparkling-wine.md) in
practice, and make white, rosé, and red versions. These are fully sparkling
wines, not simply still Vinho Verde with a slight prickle. The distinction
captures the larger point: Vinho Verde names a varied Atlantic region first,
and no single level of sweetness, carbonation, alcohol, or maturity can stand
for all of its wines.
