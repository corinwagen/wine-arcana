# Provence

Provence is a wine-growing region of southeastern France whose vineyards extend
from Mediterranean coastal settings into inland hills and valleys.
[Rosé](../styles/rose.md) is central to its contemporary identity, alongside red
and white wines that reveal a wider range of grapes and production traditions.

Understanding Provençal rosé begins in the vineyard. Growers choose fruit,
harvest timing, and pressing methods for the intended pink wine. Skin pigment
and its extraction determine colour, while fermentation determines how much
sugar remains.

## Sea, hills, and wind

The Mediterranean brings a broadly warm, dry growing environment. Distance from
the coast, elevation, surrounding hills, and exposure change the degree of
maritime moderation. Inland and coastal sites can therefore follow different
ripening schedules even within a single appellation.

The Mistral is another recurring influence. It can help dry a canopy after rain
but also increases physical exposure. Terraces called *restanques* allow
cultivation on some slopes, retaining soil while constraining machinery and
access. Limestone-rich and crystalline geological settings add differences in
rooting and water supply.

[Bandol](bandol.md) provides a particular coastal example: terraces occupy an
amphitheatre of hills facing the sea, with marl and limestone prominent in the
soils. Its sheltered geography helps explain the long-season role of Mourvèdre,
while the availability of water remains important through summer.

## Making wine for rosé

Grenache, Cinsault, Syrah, Mourvèdre, and other local varieties contribute in
different combinations. Picking sets a starting balance of sugar, acid, aroma,
and healthy skins. Gentle handling and temperature control then help the cellar
preserve the chosen fruit character.

The Provence wine body's account describes two main routes: direct pressing and
a period of skin maceration before pressing. Dark skins supply pigment; limiting
contact generally limits extraction of colour and phenolics.
[Pressing](../concepts/pressing.md) intensity, grape variety, temperature, and
fruit maturity all affect the result.

Fermentation follows the separation of juice from most solids. Producers can
ferment or mature in different vessels and retain fine lees where appropriate.
[Blending](../concepts/blending.md) selects the final combination. A pale wine
can still have texture, and a deeper colour need not mean greater residual
sugar.

## Appellations, reds, and whites

Côtes de Provence, Coteaux d'Aix-en-Provence, and Coteaux Varois en Provence are
distinct denominations represented by the Provence wine council. The broader
regional geography also contains appellations outside that grouping, including
Bandol. “Vins de Provence” as an institutional grouping should therefore be
distinguished from every wine geographically made in Provence.

Bandol's red wines make Mourvèdre and maturation especially visible. White-wine
traditions involve varieties such as Rolle, the regional name for
[Vermentino](../grapes/vermentino.md), Clairette, and Ugni Blanc in applicable
appellations.
