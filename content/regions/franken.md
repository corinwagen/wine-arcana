# Franken

Franken is a wine region in northern Bavaria, following the Main river and
its tributaries around Würzburg. [Silvaner](../grapes/silvaner.md) gives the
region a strong identity through dry white wines whose restrained fruit and
herbal character can accompany substantial texture.
[Müller-Thurgau](../grapes/muller-thurgau.md) is another major white grape,
while the western vineyards include an established red-wine tradition.

The Main's bends bring differently exposed slopes into close proximity.
Across the wider region, sandstone, shell limestone, and Keuper formations
create distinct growing environments. Cold winters, spring frost, and hot,
dry summers make the vine's access to warmth and water important in different
ways over the year.

## Three geological settings

In the western Mainviereck, the roughly rectangular course of the river,
Buntsandstein is prominent. This red or variegated sandstone underlies
vineyards around Klingenberg and Bürgstadt, where
[Spätburgunder](../grapes/pinot-noir.md) has a particular place in production.

Farther east, Muschelkalk, a shell-bearing limestone, shapes much of the
Maindreieck around Würzburg. The river's triangular course includes numerous
slopes with different angles to the sun. In the Steigerwald around Iphofen,
Keuper formations include clay-rich and marly materials. These three broad
geological settings all belong to the Triassic, with weathering and later
deposits adding variation to the soil above them.

For a grower, the depth of that soil and the proportion of clay, sand, and
stone affect drainage and available water. A shallow, exposed slope may dry
quickly during summer; deeper ground can support a larger canopy. Exposure
changes the heat reaching the vines, so the name of the underlying rock
works alongside the position of the vineyard in explaining ripening.

## Summer drought and spring frost

Franken lies in the transition from the more maritime climate of western
Germany toward continental conditions farther east. The Bavarian State
Institute for Viticulture and Horticulture describes warm, dry summers and
cold winters. South-facing vineyard slopes can become particularly hot,
and limited summer rainfall places pressure on their water reserves.

Ground cover helps protect sloping soil from erosion and provides habitat
for useful insects. It also consumes water. On a shallow, dry site, growers
may need to cut or otherwise manage that cover to reduce competition with
the vines. Decisions that protect soil through wet weather can therefore
need adjustment as the season becomes drier.

Spring brings a separate risk. After budburst, tender shoots are vulnerable
to freezing. The Bavarian institute's long-running observations show earlier
budburst, extending the period during which new growth can encounter late
cold weather.[^1] On clear, still nights, cold air can collect in hollows and
lower ground. Frost associated with an arriving cold air mass can affect a
different pattern of sites.

## Grapes and wines

Silvaner was recorded at Castell in 1659 and remains closely associated with
Franken. It can produce a light, discreetly aromatic white or a fuller wine
with a rounded middle palate. Site, crop, and harvest maturity affect that
range. Time on [lees](../concepts/lees-aging.md) and
[wood maturation](../concepts/oak-maturation.md) can further develop texture
and flavour.

Müller-Thurgau supplies another important strand of white-wine production,
alongside the more aromatic Bacchus. [Riesling](../grapes/riesling.md) and
Pinot varieties broaden the region's wines. Dry styles are strongly associated
with Franken, and the squat, flattened Bocksbeutel bottle remains a familiar
regional emblem.
