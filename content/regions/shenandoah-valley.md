# Shenandoah Valley

Shenandoah Valley is an Appalachian wine region extending through Virginia
and into West Virginia. Its mountain setting brings relatively dry growing
conditions compared with much of Virginia, alongside cold winters and
spring-frost exposure. Chardonnay, Cabernet Franc, aromatic whites, and a
varied group of other red grapes produce wines whose differences reflect
site and season as well as grape choice.

The American viticultural area (AVA) includes land in both states. It is
separate from California Shenandoah Valley in the Sierra Foothills; the similar
names refer to different places and growing conditions.[^boundary]

## Mountains, slopes, and the growing season

The regional landscape lies between the Blue Ridge to the east and the
Appalachian and Allegheny highlands to the west. Mountain shelter contributes
to the valley's comparatively dry conditions. Virginia Wine's regional account
emphasizes cooler nights and lower humidity, while also identifying winter
injury and late frost as continuing constraints.

Within that broad setting, a vineyard's position changes its risks.
[Altitude](../concepts/altitude.md) influences temperature and season length,
while slopes can let cold air escape from the fruiting zone. A low hollow
may therefore suffer a radiation frost that a nearby hillside avoids.
[Winter hardiness](../concepts/winter-hardiness.md) remains a separate trait
from the ability of young shoots to survive a freeze after budbreak.

Soils also change within a property. Muse describes an upper river terrace
with loam over gravelly clay, a steep shale outcrop, and a lower vineyard on
river-deposited silt loam. Those positions differ in drainage and water storage.
An estate's vineyard map can therefore explain differences that a broad
description of the valley's geology leaves unresolved.

The relatively dry climate creates opportunities for healthy fruit and
longer harvest decisions, but rainfall distribution still matters. Wet
flowering weather can reduce fruit set; subsequent growth and disease pressure
change the amount of [canopy work](../concepts/canopy-management.md) needed.

## A vintage makes the variation visible

John Kiers's Shenandoah contribution to the Virginia Vineyards Association's
2025 report describes a cool, frequently wet first half of the season at
Ox-Eye Vineyards, followed by a very dry finish. Poor flowering reduced the
Chardonnay and Lemberger crops. Some vines then produced excessive growth
relative to their small crop, requiring additional hedging.

Cabernet Franc behaved differently again: its ripening appeared to slow
during the extreme dryness. Kiers also documented a substantial spotted
lanternfly presence and continued management after harvest.[^vintage]
This is one estate's seasonal account, but it shows why rain shadow, cool
nights, and grape variety are only the beginning of a regional explanation.
Water stress, [crop load](../concepts/crop-load.md), and leaf health help
determine what reaches the cellar.

## Grapes and styles in practice

[Chardonnay](../grapes/chardonnay.md) offers several ways to use the valley's
fruit. Ox-Eye documents both steel-fermented and barrel-fermented still
versions, alongside a bottle-fermented blanc de blancs. Comparing those wines
separates the effects of [oak](../concepts/oak-maturation.md) and sparkling
production from the growing conditions shared by the fruit.

Its red range includes [Cabernet Franc](../grapes/cabernet-franc.md),
[Pinot Noir](../grapes/pinot-noir.md), and
[Lemberger](../grapes/blaufrankisch.md), the grape also known as Blaufränkisch.
The producer's 2023 Vallitage brings Lemberger, Cabernet Franc, and
[Saperavi](../grapes/saperavi.md) together. That particular blend illustrates
how regional winemaking extends beyond a familiar Bordeaux combination.

[Riesling](../grapes/riesling.md) and
[Grüner Veltliner](../grapes/gruner-veltliner.md) also enter Ox-Eye's
sparkling range. These are documented producer choices rather than a claim
that all sites can ripen the same set of varieties. A wine labelled with a
single grape and a blended wine answer different stylistic questions using
the region's available fruit.

## Producer reference points

Ox-Eye provides an instructive comparison of still, sparkling, and blended
wines, with its detailed harvest reporting connecting vineyard events to
finished wine. Its range shows how one farm can pursue several styles
without assigning a single flavour profile to the whole valley.

Muse Vineyards near Woodstock supplies a contrasting estate story on the
North Fork of the Shenandoah River. USDA's 2024 account documents its growth
after a first harvest in 2009, its Clio red wine, and the business planning
and agricultural partnerships behind the winery. Muse's own account identifies
Clio as a Bordeaux-style blend. It offers a useful reference for the valley's
estate red blends alongside Ox-Eye's broader range of cool-climate grapes.

[^boundary]: 27 CFR § 9.60 defines Shenandoah Valley across Virginia and
    West Virginia. The separate California appellation is named in § 9.37.
[^vintage]: John Kiers, “Regional Report – Shenandoah Valley,” in the
    Virginia Vineyards Association's *Fall 2025 Vintage Report*. The reported
    growing conditions and insect pressure concern Ox-Eye's vineyard.
