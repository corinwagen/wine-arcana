# Pico

Pico is a volcanic island in the central Azores, where vineyards occupy low
coastal ground beneath a steep mountain. Black stone walls divide the vines
into small plots called *currais*, sheltering them from Atlantic wind and
salt spray. The island produces dry whites from Arinto dos Açores,
[Verdelho](../grapes/verdelho.md), and Terrantez do Pico, alongside a tradition
of fortified wine.

The wine appellation and the UNESCO vineyard landscape have distinct
boundaries and purposes. Pico's protected designation of origin governs wine
production in specified coastal areas. The World Heritage designation
recognizes a landscape shaped by generations of farming, including working
vineyards, abandoned enclosures, cellars, paths, and ports.

## Growing vines among lava

The coastal vineyards contain shallow soils and extensive exposed volcanic
rock. Growers plant in fissures and pockets where roots can establish
themselves. Stones cleared from the ground become walls: the work of making
a planting space also supplies the material needed to shelter it. In the
small enclosures, pruning and harvesting demand close, manual attention.

Criação Velha, just south of Madalena, and Santa Luzia preserve conspicuous
examples of this landscape. UNESCO inscribed the vineyard culture in 2004.
Cultivation began in the fifteenth century, expanded through the nineteenth,
and subsequently contracted as plant disease and abandonment took their toll.
Restoring an enclosure brings both vines and the walls around them back into
use.

Pico Mountain changes the rainfall reaching different sides of the island.
The west, where much of the vineyard area lies, is generally drier than the
northeast. Near the coast, exposed lava absorbs warmth that helps grapes
ripen. Maritime humidity, wind exposure, the amount of shelter, and the
vine's rooting space all vary over short distances.

## The appellation

Under the specification approved in October 2024, Pico PDO encompasses
specified areas at elevations of 100 metres or less in Madalena, São Roque,
and Lajes. The designation covers still wine, sparkling wine, liqueur wine,
and wine from overripe grapes.[^1]

Arinto dos Açores, Verdelho, and Terrantez do Pico must collectively supply
at least 85% of the must. Galego Dourado and Malvasia Fina may supply
the remainder. This permits both blends and single-variety wines. Liqueur
wines and wines from overripe grapes require at least 36 months of ageing
before bottling.

## Dry whites and aged fortified wines

Arinto dos Açores supplies a clear example of the range within contemporary
dry production. Azores Wine Company's 2025 bottling uses whole-bunch pressing,
followed by fermentation in small horizontal tanks and maturation on fine
[lees](../concepts/lees-aging.md). The producer describes citrus fruit,
pronounced acidity, and a saline finish.

Picowines takes a different cellar route for its 2023 Arinto dos Açores:
fermentation in a 5,000-litre wooden vat, followed by six months there with
the fine lees. A large vat gives a different ratio of wood surface to wine
volume from a small barrel. Lees contact can add breadth while preserving
the impression of freshness supplied by acidity.

The cooperative's Edição Especial Licoroso 1998 illustrates the continuing
fortified tradition. Made from Verdelho, its fermentation was interrupted
with wine spirit, retaining grape sugar. Picowines reports 25 years in old
oak casks and describes candied citrus, toasted almond, and toffee. Here,
[fortification](../concepts/fortification.md) and prolonged
[wood maturation](../concepts/oak-maturation.md) give the wine a sweetness,
texture, and mature aromatic character quite different from the young dry
whites.
