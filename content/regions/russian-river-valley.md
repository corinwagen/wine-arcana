# Russian River Valley

Russian River Valley is a wine-growing area within
[Sonoma County](sonoma-county.md), California. It is closely associated with
[Pinot Noir](../grapes/pinot-noir.md) and
[Chardonnay](../grapes/chardonnay.md), whose early ripening works well with
the region's combination of sunshine and marine cooling. Old Zinfandel
vineyards add a different history to an area often introduced through those
two grapes alone.

The American Viticultural Area covers a varied landscape around communities
including Sebastopol, Forestville, and Santa Rosa. Its name should not suggest
that every vineyard sits beside the river or experiences the same amount of
fog.

## Fog and the shape of the growing season

Pacific air and fog moderate vineyard temperatures. Fog reduces morning
sunlight until it clears, while ocean breezes can restrain afternoon heat.
The duration and frequency of that cooling matter as much as a vineyard's
distance from the coast. A sheltered inland block can warm much sooner than a
site exposed to incoming marine air.

The growers' association describes six informal neighbourhoods to help
explain local variation. Sebastopol Hills is among the coolest; the Eastern
Hills experience less fog and stronger afternoon warmth. Green Valley,
south of Forestville, is associated with Goldridge soils and also has a
separate AVA designation. The Middle Reach, nearer Healdsburg, provides a
different setting from the cool southwestern vineyards. These neighbourhoods
are useful orientation, not six equivalent legal appellations.

Soils vary as well. Sandy Goldridge soils are prominent in parts of the west,
while other sites have loams, clays, volcanic materials, or different
sedimentary deposits. Rooting depth and water storage affect how vines carry
their crop through the dry part of the season. A soil name alone gives little
information about the depth, slope, or management of a particular block.

## Pinot Noir: ripeness and extraction

Russian River Pinot Noir can combine ripe fruit with persistent
[acidity](../concepts/acidity.md), but its weight and texture vary with site,
season, and harvest decisions. Skin contact, the inclusion of stems, and
barrel choices add differences after picking. A broad regional blend may
combine several of those growing conditions; a vineyard-designated wine
focuses attention more narrowly.

Williams Selyem's estate illustrates the importance of plant material as well
as location. Its Pinot Noir includes several named clones and selections.
The estate's Block 10 Mass Selection bottling uses a portion of whole bunches
in fermentation and matures in a mixture of new and previously used oak.
[Whole-cluster fermentation](../concepts/whole-cluster-fermentation.md) adds
stems to the extraction process, giving the winemaker another way to influence
aroma and tannin.

## Chardonnay and the wider range

Dutton-Goldfield's Dutton Ranch Chardonnay draws on the Dutton family's network
of vineyard sites. The name “ranch” here encompasses multiple growing places,
not a single uniform block. Comparing such a blend with a vineyard-specific
wine helps show two different ways of interpreting regional fruit.

Chardonnay's texture also reflects choices about fermentation vessels,
[malolactic fermentation](../concepts/malolactic-fermentation.md), and
[lees ageing](../concepts/lees-aging.md). These processes can soften perceived
sharpness or broaden texture while the grapes retain the influence of their
growing season. Ripe fruit, creamy texture, and oak aroma have different
causes and need not occur together.

The Santa Rosa Plains neighbourhood retains old
[Zinfandel](../grapes/zinfandel.md) plantings. Their presence is a useful
reminder that this region's modern Pinot and Chardonnay identity developed
within an older, more varied wine-growing landscape.
