# Condrieu

Condrieu is a white-wine appellation on the Rhône's right bank south of Lyon,
beside [Côte-Rôtie](cote-rotie.md). Its wines are made entirely from
[Viognier](../grapes/viognier.md), whose peach-and-apricot perfume and broad
texture define the region.[^1] Steep terraces supply the grape with sun and
shelter, while making vineyard work unusually labour-intensive.

## Cultivating the slopes

The vineyards extend through seven communes, from Condrieu south to Limony.
Granite and related rocks weather into fragile sandy-clay soils. Growers hold
these soils on the hillside with dry-stone walls supporting narrow terraces,
known locally as *chaillées*. Maintaining walls and controlling runoff are
continuing parts of farming here. Much of the ground offers little scope for
mechanisation.

South- and southeast-facing slopes provide sunlight and some protection from
cold northerly winds. Water supply also matters: roots exploit fissures in
the bedrock, and soil depth varies between parcels. Plantgrape identifies
adequate soil depth as useful for protecting Viognier against drought, while
its early budbreak exposes it to spring frost.

## Ripeness and wine

Viognier can accumulate considerable sugar while retaining relatively modest
acidity. This helps explain Condrieu's combination of aromatic intensity and
weight. Ripeness, water supply, and the growing season affect that balance;
the grape can also give a slight bitterness that contributes to the wine's
finish.

Most Condrieu is dry, though producers also make small quantities of sweeter
wine from very ripe grapes. The rules recognise wines containing at least
45 grams per litre of fermentable sugar, requiring overripe fruit gathered
in successive picking passes. All Condrieu grapes must be harvested by
hand.[^1] The appellation's floral and ripe-fruit aromas therefore accompany
several degrees of sweetness.

## Decline and recovery

Phylloxera, war, and employment in the industrial Rhône valley depleted the
labour available for the difficult slopes. The vineyard came close to
disappearing during the twentieth century. Its later recovery required
replanting and renewed commitment to terrace cultivation. Appellation
recognition came in 1940, followed by an extension southward in 1967.[^2]
