# Wachau

The Wachau is a wine region along the Danube in Lower Austria, within the
valley between Melk and Krems. Its terraced slopes and adjoining lower ground
produce a wide range of wines, especially dry whites from [Grüner
Veltliner](../grapes/gruner-veltliner.md) and [Riesling](../grapes/riesling.md).
The region combines sites capable of substantial ripeness with cooling air
from the surrounding uplands.

A Wachau label can describe both origin and style. Wachau DAC (Districtus
Austriae Controllatus, Austria's origin designation) identifies
regional, village, or vineyard origin under appellation rules. Steinfeder,
Federspiel, and Smaragd are separate terms belonging to the Vinea Wachau
growers' association and indicate different styles within its production
standards.

## River valley and terraces

Warm Pannonian air reaches the valley from the east. Cooler air descends from
the Waldviertel to the north and the Dunkelsteinerwald to the south through
wooded side valleys. Elevation, exposure, and the paths taken by this air make
conditions vary within a relatively small area. A sunny river-facing slope
and a site exposed to upland air can follow different ripening courses.

Weathered gneiss and other crystalline rocks underlie many hillside vineyards.
Patches of wind-deposited loess and deeper material occur lower down. These
differences affect rooting and water supply, while the slope and exposure
influence the sunlight reaching the vines. The Bruck vineyard in the Spitzer
Graben, for example, combines steep terraces with granodiorite gneiss and very
little loess, making it a useful specific example of the region's rocky
vineyards.

Growers built dry-stone retaining walls to make steep ground cultivable and
hold soil in place. The walls use fitted stone without mortar and require
continued maintenance. Their construction connects vineyard work with the
shape of the landscape: planting and retaining a narrow terrace involves work
that a broad, gentle slope does not require.

## Origin on the label

Wachau DAC applies from the 2020 vintage. The consolidated Austrian rules
consulted in September 2026 require hand-harvested grapes from the region and
distinguish three levels of geographical designation.[^1]

*Gebietswein*, regional wine, allows the broadest selection of varieties and
blends, including red grapes. *Ortswein* carries a permitted village or local
name and narrows the principal varieties to a group of white grapes. Besides
Grüner Veltliner and Riesling, these include [Chardonnay](../grapes/chardonnay.md),
[Pinot Gris](../grapes/pinot-gris.md), and other established regional varieties.
Village wines may have no more than a barely perceptible wood note.

*Riedenwein* carries a named vineyard, or *Ried*. Its principal variety must be
Grüner Veltliner or Riesling, although the legal text tolerates up to 15% other
quality-wine varieties. Enrichment to increase alcohol is prohibited, and wood
aroma must again be absent or barely perceptible.[^1] These provisions make
grape ripeness and the vineyard designation particularly relevant to this
category.

## Steinfeder, Federspiel, and Smaragd

Vinea Wachau reserves these registered trademarks for its members. Its current
description specifies dry wines without perceptible oak influence. Steinfeder
has up to 11.5% alcohol; Federspiel has no more than 12.5%; Smaragd starts at
12.5%. Steinfeder and Federspiel can also be used for [rosé](../styles/rose.md).[^2]

The terms give a practical indication of weight. Steinfeder occupies the
lighter end, while Smaragd is associated with grapes left longer on the vine
and a more substantial finished wine. Because the wines are dry, greater grape
sugar is expressed largely through alcohol following [fermentation](../concepts/alcoholic-fermentation.md).
The vintage and site still influence acid balance and the character of that
ripeness.
