# Santa Cruz Mountains

The Santa Cruz Mountains wine region follows the coastal mountains south of
San Francisco, between the Pacific Ocean and the valleys around San Francisco
and Monterey bays. Its American viticultural area (AVA), established in 1981,
is strongly shaped by mountain topography.[^1] Small vineyards on different
slopes and ridges experience different combinations of fog, sunlight, wind,
and shelter.

[Pinot Noir](../grapes/pinot-noir.md),
[Chardonnay](../grapes/chardonnay.md), and
[Cabernet Sauvignon](../grapes/cabernet-sauvignon.md) all have substantial
regional histories. Their coexistence makes this a useful place to understand
how a broadly maritime climate can support very different wines.

## Elevation in a coastal landscape

The region extends roughly from the Half Moon Bay area toward Mount Madonna.
Elevation helps distinguish its vineyard land from surrounding lowlands,
but [altitude](../concepts/altitude.md) interacts with marine air. A high ridge
can stand in sunshine above a low fog layer while a lower coastal vineyard
remains shaded. The duration of that shading changes the heat and light a
vine receives.

Ocean-facing vineyards, higher ridges, and slopes facing inland therefore
need separate consideration. Exposed sites feel more of the marine influence;
sheltered positions can receive long periods of sunlight. Soil depth, slope,
and underlying rock further change rooting conditions and stored water.
The region includes shale, clay, loam, and localized limestone formations.

Mount Eden illustrates the combination of cool air and high-elevation sunshine.
Its estate lies above the usual fog layer on Franciscan shale. The winery
reports a harvest season that commonly stretches from September into late
October and sometimes November. Such an extended window also reflects its
mixture of earlier- and later-ripening varieties.

## Mountain Cabernet and white wine

Ridge's Monte Bello site offers a specific example of Cabernet Sauvignon in
a maritime mountain setting. Its vineyards range from approximately 1,300 to
2,700 feet, with greenstone and clay over decomposing limestone. Ridge keeps
parcels and varieties separate through fermentation and evaluates their
contributions before assembling the final wine. Cabernet Sauvignon usually
leads, with potential contributions from
[Merlot](../grapes/merlot.md), [Petit Verdot](../grapes/petit-verdot.md), and
[Cabernet Franc](../grapes/cabernet-franc.md).

That blending process responds to the vintage: fruit maturity and the
balance of [tannin](../concepts/tannin.md), acidity, and aroma vary among
parcels. Ridge matures those lots in American oak, adding a specific cellar
choice to the vineyard's contribution.

Mount Eden's estate includes Chardonnay and Pinot Noir alongside Cabernet
and other Bordeaux varieties. Its nearby Domaine Eden property adds another
comparison, with north- and east-facing vineyards on clay-loam soils.

## Continuity and interpretation

The region's present vineyards draw on a history of nineteenth-century
planting and later renewal. Mount Eden traces the development of its estate
to Martin Ray in 1945. Ridge began its modern Monte Bello project after the
property purchase in 1959 and released its first commercial Monte Bello
in 1962. These continuing estates connect today's wines to earlier decisions
about planting material, site selection, and varietal identity.

When comparing bottles, vineyard origin is especially informative here. A
mountaintop Cabernet blend and a Pinot Noir from a more ocean-exposed slope
may share an appellation while experiencing different growing seasons.
Producer records of harvest and maturation help explain the remaining
variation in structure and readiness to drink.
