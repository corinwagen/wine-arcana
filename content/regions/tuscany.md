# Tuscany

Tuscany is a wine-growing region of central Italy stretching from inland hills
to the Tyrrhenian coast and offshore islands.
[Sangiovese](../grapes/sangiovese.md) links many of its red wines, but coastal
Cabernet blends, local white varieties, and dried-grape traditions make the
region much broader than one grape or style.

Inland hill districts around Florence and Siena differ from the coastal
landscapes. Elevation, exposure, distance from the sea, and the water stored in
different soils affect how vines complete the season. Individual denominations
then apply their own geographical boundaries and production choices.

## Inland hills and Sangiovese

[Chianti Classico](chianti-classico.md) occupies wooded hills between Florence
and Siena. Its vineyards vary in aspect, height, and soil depth, making the pace
of Sangiovese ripening an important part of the regional explanation. Chianti
Classico is a separate denomination from Chianti; the shared word does not make
their geographical definitions interchangeable.

Farther south, [Brunello di Montalcino](brunello-di-montalcino.md) applies a
pure-Sangiovese identity and extended maturation to the vineyards around
Montalcino. Sangiovese can retain marked acidity and tannin at ripeness, so
extraction and maturation remain important alongside the place.

Warmer exposure can aid a late-ripening crop in a cool year, while a dry, hot
season increases the value of shade and reliable water supply. An inland hill
district therefore contains several growing environments, even before producer
and vintage enter the comparison.

## Coast and southern districts

The coastal belt includes Bolgheri, Val di Cornia, and the broader
[Maremma](maremma.md). Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, and other international
grapes play a conspicuous role in some wines. Coastal influence, local shelter,
and soil conditions give these varieties a different setting from their
[Bordeaux](bordeaux.md) home.

Maremma also includes Sangiovese traditions such as [Morellino di
Scansano](morellino-di-scansano.md), while [Montecucco](montecucco.md) supplies
another southern comparison.

*Super Tuscan* is an informal historical and commercial expression, not a single
appellation specification. It can describe wines made from quite different
grapes and places.

## White and sweet wines

Vermentino connects parts of the coast with other Mediterranean vineyards.
Vernaccia di San Gimignano gives the inland hills a distinct white-wine
tradition; the variety is different from the red [Vernaccia
Nera](../grapes/vernaccia-nera.md). Trebbiano and other local grapes also
contribute to regional whites.

Sweet-wine traditions include Vin Santo, made with grapes dried after harvest,
and aromatic wines such as Moscadello di Montalcino. Drying changes the fruit's
concentration and composition; fermentation and maturation then determine how
much sugar remains and how the wine develops. The general process is explained
under [appassimento](../concepts/appassimento.md).
