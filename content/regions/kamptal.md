# Kamptal

Kamptal is a wine region in Lower Austria, named for the Kamp river and
centred on Langenlois. Its principal wines are dry whites from
[Grüner Veltliner](../grapes/gruner-veltliner.md) and
[Riesling](../grapes/riesling.md). Warm air from the southeast and cooler
influences from the Waldviertel meet across a landscape of loess-covered
hills, rocky slopes, and terraced vineyards.

That combination permits ripe fruit while preserving acidity. The range
extends from relatively light regional wines to concentrated wines from
named vineyards, with differences in water supply, exposure, and cellar
practice contributing to their character.

## Warmth, cool air, and soil depth

The vineyard country follows the lower Kamp from around Plank toward
Grafenegg. Langenlois forms a centre of production, with neighbouring
wine-growing settlements including Zöbing, Gobelsburg, and Kammern. The
Pannonian influence brings warmth from the east and southeast, while the
Waldviertel uplands supply cooler air, especially at night.

A sunlit slope can accumulate considerable warmth during the day and then
cool as air moves down from higher ground. The timing of that cooling matters
to ripening and acidity. Sheltered hollows and exposed terraces consequently
experience different conditions even within a short distance.

Loess is wind-deposited sediment rich in silt. Deep deposits can hold a large
reserve of water, helping sustain a canopy through dry weather. Shallower
soils over rock offer a different rooting environment, where fractures and
weathered material determine how far roots can penetrate. Grüner Veltliner
is prominent on loess, while Riesling has an important place on stonier
hillsides.

## Named vineyards

Heiligenstein, above Zöbing, has weathered reddish sandstone and conglomeratic
material. Its warm, southward-opening setting is closely associated with
Riesling. Nearby Grub occupies a sheltered hollow between Heiligenstein and
Gaisberg, with loess and local gravel deposits. Hasel provides another example
of deep loess and its capacity to retain moisture.

Gaisberg extends from the Manhartsberg and includes several geological
conditions. Hirsch describes its Riesling site there as southwest-facing,
with sandy, silty brown earth over weathered mica schist. Remnants of loess
and other rock types introduce further variation. Such descriptions help
explain vine growth through soil depth, drainage, and exposure; the vineyard
name also preserves the identity of the place across vintages.

## Wine and maturation

Grüner Veltliner commonly brings orchard fruit and peppery or herbal
impressions. Riesling can show citrus and stone fruit with a pronounced acid
line. Ripeness determines much of the difference between a lighter wine and
one with greater body, while pressing, fermentation, and maturation influence
texture and aromatic development.

Hirsch's 2019 Gaisberg Riesling, for example, underwent whole-bunch pressing
and fermentation with indigenous yeasts. It matured in both stainless steel
and large oak vessels, remaining on its
[lees](../concepts/lees-aging.md) until May before bottling in July. This
combination shows how time on yeast sediment and the use of large vessels
can shape a dry white intended for development.

## Kamptal DAC

The DAC regulation effective from 17 September 2024 defines three levels of
origin: regional *Gebietswein*, village-area *Ortswein*, and named-vineyard
*Riedenwein*. The prescribed styles move from lighter regional wines toward
medium to fuller vineyard wines.[^1]

The regulation permits Grüner Veltliner, Riesling,
[Chardonnay](../grapes/chardonnay.md),
[Pinot Blanc](../grapes/pinot-blanc.md), and
[Pinot Gris](../grapes/pinot-gris.md), individually or blended. It also limits
unfermented sugar to no more than the wine's titratable acidity, with both
expressed in grams per litre.[^2]

From the 2025 harvest, the rules generally require grapes certified organic
or under Nachhaltig Austria, with exemptions for small wine producers and
limited purchases from small grape growers.[^2] This introduces a regulated
farming requirement alongside the rules governing origin and wine style.

[^1]: Austria, *DAC-Verordnung “Kamptal”*, BGBl. II No. 250/2024, section 2,
    effective 17 September 2024; accessed 11 September 2026.
[^2]: The same regulation, section 3, paragraphs 1, 2, and 4. The
    certification exemption covers producers reporting less than 5,000
    litres of Kamptal DAC and allows up to 15% of grapes to come from
    growers reporting no more than 6,000 kilograms total grape production.
